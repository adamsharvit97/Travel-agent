# The Stupid-to-Say-No-To Offering

*A GTM strategist's design of Latitude 43's entry product. Written for the founder's decision. Grounded in the v2.0 business plan, the pricing stress test, the 28-sim cohort, and the buyer-research compendium. The brief is to find the front door that the right SMB founder walks through without thinking — while the unit economics still pay for the founder's life.*

**Prepared:** May 2026
**For:** The founder, Latitude 43 Inc.
**Reading time:** 35 minutes
**Decision-point density:** high

---

## Frame

Before any of the five designs are scored, three things need to be said out loud, because every entry-product decision below hinges on them.

**First, the buyer is not buying price.** The ASTA research, the Perk 2025 disruption study, and the 28 simulated calls all converge on the same conclusion: the SMB founder doing $50K–$500K of T&E is not making a price decision when they pick a travel partner. They are making an identity decision (do I admit I need help?), a trust decision (is this person actually going to pick up at 11pm?), and a friction decision (how fast can I stop thinking about this and get to work?). The "stupid to say no to" framing is not a discount framing. It is a friction-removal framing. Price is one component of friction. Trust is the other ten.

**Second, the founder cannot give labor away to acquire a member.** Latitude 43's defining constraint is bus factor 1.0. The Atlas Light economics ($2,650 Y1 per member) cannot absorb the $15K–$30K all-in cold CAC the v2.0 plan calls out — and they especially cannot absorb labor-priced-at-zero acquisition costs that take three hours per prospect to deliver. Any "stupid to say no to" entry product that solves the buyer's friction by transferring all of it onto the founder's calendar is a category error. The right entry product is one that uses *founder leverage assets* — content already produced, infrastructure already built, supplier relationships already paid for — and not *founder labor in real time*.

**Third, the brand register is the price floor.** The website runs Aman/Patek/Loro Piana — Cormorant Garamond, dark mode, cinematic photography. That register reads at $200–$2,000/mo. Pricing the entry product below $99/mo creates a register mismatch that triggers the "no free lunch" instinct in exactly the buyer the brand is courting. So the entry product either prices at or above $99/mo, *or* it is structured as a free / pay-per-trip front end that the brand register accommodates as a discreet "first conversation" SKU rather than a tier on the main pricing page.

With those three pinned: here is the formula, the five designs, the scoring, the winner, the unit economics, the script, and the ten decisions.

## A. The "Stupid to Say No To" Formula

Across SMB SaaS — One Medical, Notion, Linear, Mercury, Brex, HubSpot Starter, Loom, Ramp — six elements show up in every offer that converts on the first read. Most products that fail on first-touch fail one or more of them. The matrix is not an opinion; it is the negative space behind every product that achieved a sub-30-day sales cycle into an SMB founder's calendar.

### The six elements

| # | Element | Why it matters | Common failure |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Price under monthly mental approval threshold** | SMB founder approves <$200/mo on instinct; $200–$500 requires partner consult; $500+ requires CFO/board awareness | Pricing at $499 to feel premium; loses on impulse purchase |
| 2 | **Risk reversal** | Removes the "what if it's wrong" objection; cancel-anytime, 30-day money-back, free trial | Annual contract with no out; founder dies on the legal review |
| 3 | **One specific tangible benefit** | A noun the buyer can repeat to a peer; not a category, a thing | "We do travel management" instead of "We pick up the phone at 11pm" |
| 4 | **Trust signal** | Named human; founder credibility; social proof from a peer of the buyer | Anonymous "team"; testimonials from non-peers; logos from companies the buyer doesn't recognize |
| 5 | **Onboarding under 10 minutes** | Time-to-first-value must beat the buyer's attention span | A 45-minute kickoff call before any value is delivered |
| 6 | **Immediate utility** | The first value moment happens fast — within 24 hours, ideally within the first session | Value is back-loaded to "after the first trip" |

### Atlas Light at $99/mo — element-by-element audit

The v2.0 plan repriced Light from $99 to $129–$149 to clear the credible-floor test. For this audit I score the *$99 version that existed pre-repricing*, because that's what the brief asks about, and then I note where the repricing changes the score.

| # | Element | Atlas Light at $99/mo | Pass / Fail | Note |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Price under threshold** | $99/mo is well below the $200 instinct line | **Pass** | Repricing to $149 still passes |
| 2 | **Risk reversal** | None stated. Membership agreement is annual implicit. No free trial. No money-back. First Trip Guarantee exists but is a labor cap, not a buyer-side reversal | **Fail** | The biggest gap |
| 3 | **One specific tangible benefit** | "Members' travel office" is a category, not a thing. The actual concrete benefit (24/7 desk, 60-min SLA, named human) is buried in tier-comparison copy | **Partial** | Fixable in copy |
| 4 | **Trust signal** | Founder is named. Fora is named. Virtuoso is named. No customer testimonials (correctly — no public client names without consent). Incident log is the proof — but it ships at month 6, not Day 1 | **Partial** | Strong on founder credibility, thin on proof at moment of sale |
| 5 | **Onboarding under 10 minutes** | Discovery call → onboarding form → 48-hour profile build → first booking within 7 days. **Time-to-first-value: 7 days minimum** | **Fail** | The biggest operational gap |
| 6 | **Immediate utility** | First value moment is the first booked trip. For a buyer with no current trip booked, this could be weeks. For a buyer with an urgent trip, it's the first interaction — but that's a minority case | **Fail** | Same root cause as #5 |

**Atlas Light at $99/mo passes 1 of 6 cleanly, partial on 2, fails on 3.** The repricing to $149 changes nothing on this scorecard — it is the same product with a different number. The product is well-designed for a buyer who has already decided to hire a travel office and is now picking which one. It is not designed to convert the buyer who hasn't yet decided.

**The reason this matters for the entry-product question:** the brief asks for the offering an SMB founder signs up for "without thinking." Atlas Light requires thinking. The buyer has to make an identity decision (do I admit I need this?), a trust decision (will this person pick up?), and a pricing decision (is $1,548/yr justified?) — all before the first value moment, which is days or weeks away. The entry product must do the inverse: deliver the value moment *first*, and then ask for the decision.

## B. Five Entry-Product Designs

The brief calls for five candidate designs, each plausible enough to defend in front of the founder. Each is specified to the point where the unit economics and the founder labor can be evaluated honestly.

### Design 1 — The Travel Receipt Service (free entry tier)

**The pitch in one sentence:** "Forward your next trip confirmation to us. We'll send back a one-page report of what we'd have caught."

**Mechanics:**
- Buyer signs up on a single landing page — email + LinkedIn URL only. No card. No contract.
- They forward any existing trip confirmation (air, hotel, or both) to a dedicated address.
- Within 48 hours, they receive a one-page PDF — branded, signed, addressed to them — that itemizes: (a) the loyalty status they're sitting on; (b) hotel amenities they could have booked at the same rate via Virtuoso/FHR/Privé; (c) the fare class or upgrade arbitrage they missed; (d) one specific thing Latitude 43 would have changed.
- The PDF closes with a single line: *"Three of these are free to fix. Two require a relationship. We're happy to be that relationship."*
- A six-day email sequence follows: Day 0 (the report), Day 2 (one-page case study, anonymized), Day 5 (the Atlas Light offer at $149/mo), Day 14 (one phone call slot offer), Day 30 (the unsubscribe-or-stay-on-Journal fork).

**Hook to upgrade:** When the buyer sees what they missed on the trip they already booked, they have a concrete dollar number ($300–$1,700 of value left on the table). The Atlas Light pitch becomes "the next trip pays for the membership."

**Founder labor:**
- 30–45 min per report at scale (Sabre lookup, Virtuoso amenities database, fare class check, the Latitude memo template). Founder's first 20 reports take 90 min each; by report #50 it's 30.
- This is labor at $0 revenue per receipt. At 100 free signups/mo, that's 50 hours of founder time at zero contracted revenue.

**Unit economics:**
- 100 free signups → 5–10 paid upgrades to Light at $149/mo → $9,000–$18,000 ARR per monthly cohort
- CAC: 50 founder-hours × $200/hr effective = $10,000 in labor + ~$500 in tooling/marketing = $10,500 per cohort. $1,050–$2,100 per paid upgrade.
- LTV at 85% renewal: ~$4,500 per Light member over 5 years
- LTV:CAC: 2.1–4.3x. Marginal.

**Risk profile:**
- Founder labor at $0 revenue per receipt is the single largest risk. If the conversion rate from free → paid sits at 5% instead of 10%, the math breaks.
- Brand-register risk: the free tier sits in the Ramp/Mercury register, not the Patek/Aman register. The website needs a sub-page that flexes registers without breaking the main site.
- Adverse selection: buyers who forward receipts may be tire-kickers, not buyers. The qualifying gate is weak.

**Time-to-first-value:** 48 hours. **Passes element 5 and 6 of the formula.**

### Design 2 — The Membership Pilot ($49/mo first 90 days, then $99)

**The pitch in one sentence:** "Atlas Light at half-price for your first 90 days. Money back if you don't see the value. Cancel anytime after."

**Mechanics:**
- Standard Atlas Light at $99/mo, with a discounted first-quarter rate at $49/mo.
- Money-back guarantee: at any point in the first 90 days, if the member asks for a refund, full refund of the $147 quarterly payment, no questions. The member keeps any rewards/status earned.
- Auto-converts to $99/mo on Day 91 unless cancelled.
- 30-minute onboarding call within 48 hours of signup. First trip booked within 7 days.
- Includes a "make a wish" Day-1 ask: the member identifies one specific friction from their last trip (cancelled flight, wrong hotel category, missed upgrade) and the desk addresses it inside the first week as a proof point.

**Hook to upgrade:** Day 91 conversion to standard pricing. Day 180 referral incentive. The 90-day window is designed to let the member experience two trips end-to-end — enough to internalize the relationship before the price step-up.

**Founder labor:**
- Same as Atlas Light proper. The pilot is a discount, not a different product.
- One additional 15-minute check-in at Day 60 to forestall churn.

**Unit economics:**
- 100 pilot signups → 50% continue past Day 90 → 42% renew at Day 365 (after 8% annual churn)
- Cohort year-one revenue: 100 × $147 (quarterly) + 50 × $891 (months 4–12 at $99) = $14,700 + $44,550 = $59,250 in pilot+conversion subscription. Plus commission and design fees on the 50 retained members ≈ $30,000. **Total Y1 cohort gross: ~$89,000.**
- Net per retained member Y1: ~$1,780 (vs. $2,650 standard Atlas Light Y1)
- The discount conditioning risk: members who paid $49 will resist the $99 step. Industry data on pilot-pricing conditioning ([Recurly](https://recurly.com/research/churn-rate-benchmarks/)) shows Day-91 churn of 30–50% on discounted-to-standard transitions.

**Risk profile:**
- **Discount conditioning is the dominant risk.** The brand voice in v2.0 banks an explicit no-discount discipline ("No discounting. The price is the price"). This design contradicts the discipline.
- **Adverse selection at $49:** the buyer attracted to a $49 entry is closer to a tire-kicker than a $149 buyer. The 50% Day-91 drop reflects this.
- **Brand-register hit:** half-price pilots are a SaaS register, not a private-bank register. Soho House does not offer a 90-day pilot.

**Time-to-first-value:** 7 days. **Passes element 1, 2, partial on 5, 6.**

### Design 3 — The First Trip Promise (no membership, just pay-per-trip)

**The pitch in one sentence:** "Pay $250 to have one trip professionally handled. If it goes wrong, the next one is free."

**Mechanics:**
- Buyer pays $250 (flat, no escalator) to have a single business trip — flights + hotel + ground — designed and booked by the desk.
- One discovery email (10 questions, ~6 minutes to fill), one 15-minute call if needed, then full handoff. Trip booked within 5 business days.
- If anything goes wrong on the trip — defined by an explicit list: missed connection that desk didn't proactively rebook within 60 min, hotel mis-booking, missed status credit on a participating partner — the next trip's $250 fee is waived.
- After the third trip, the buyer is invited to convert to Atlas Light at $149/mo, with the $750 of design fees credited toward the first 5 months of membership.

**Hook to upgrade:**
- The math is built in. Three trips at $250 = $750. Atlas Light at $149/mo = $750 in 5 months. So the conversion ask is "you've already paid for membership; now your fourth trip is included."
- The relationship is built across three trips. By the third, the member has loyalty preferences saved, has experienced one disruption handled, and trusts the desk.

**Founder labor:**
- 2–3 hours per trip including intake, search, booking, calendar handoff, follow-up.
- $250 / 2.5 hrs = $100/hr effective. Plus commission on the hotel (~$60 net) and air ($0). Total per trip: ~$110–$310 advisor revenue at 1.5–3 hours = $73–$200/hr.
- This is below the $200/hr threshold the pricing stress test established as the floor for a sustainable solo practice on labor-priced work.

**Unit economics:**
- 100 trial buyers → 75% take a second trip → 50% take a third → 35% convert to Atlas Light
- Per-trip revenue: $250 + ~$90 commission ≈ $340 gross. Founder net (after Fora 70/30 on commission): ~$313.
- Per-buyer Y1: 1.6 trips average × $313 = $500 + 35 × $1,788 / 100 = $626 membership rollover = **~$1,126 per acquired prospect Y1**.
- Per-converted-member Y1 net: $750 design + 7 months × $149 = $1,793 subscription, plus commission and follow-on design = ~$2,400.

**Risk profile:**
- **Founder labor unsubsidized.** Every $250 trip is founder time priced at the floor of the solo-practice band, with no membership stream to amortize against.
- **Revenue is lumpy.** Pay-per-trip means cash flow follows trip cadence, not subscription cadence. Forecasting is hard.
- **Repeat customer behavior is required.** If the buyer takes one trip and ghosts, the LTV is the $250 — not enough to justify the founder hours.
- **No retention mechanic.** The buyer has no contract, no recurring charge, no commitment. They are one click away from going back to Google Flights.

**Time-to-first-value:** 5 business days. **Passes element 2, 3, 5; partial on 1; weak on retention.**

### Design 4 — Atlas Light Unlimited ($199/mo, 30-day free trial)

**The pitch in one sentence:** "Atlas Light, no design fees, no limits, $199/month. Try it free for 30 days."

**Mechanics:**
- Higher entry price ($199/mo vs. v2.0 Light at $129–$149).
- Removes design fees entirely. Every trip the desk designs is included in the membership.
- 30-day free trial. Card on file at signup, but not charged until Day 31. Cancel anytime in the trial window with no charge.
- Standard Atlas Light SLA (60-min response, 24/7 desk, founder-led).
- Fair-use cap remains: ≤30 trips/year before the member is moved to Office tier.

**Hook to upgrade:**
- Office at $249/mo is a $600/yr step for an additional 2–4 named travelers. Easier upgrade math than from $149.
- Heavy travelers (>30 trips/yr) automatically migrate to Office at renewal.

**Founder labor:**
- Same per-trip labor as Atlas Light. The unlimited-design-fee structure removes the price gating that v2.0 used to discourage low-value trips.
- The 70/30 light-user/heavy-user distribution holds in subscription products; ~70% of members will be at <12 trips/yr, ~30% will be heavier.
- Heavy-user risk is real. A 30-trip-per-year Light member at $199/mo is paying $80/trip — below the $250 floor of the per-trip pricing.

**Unit economics:**
- $199/mo × 12 = $2,388 subscription per member
- Commission per Light member (1 × 18 nights × $420 ADR × 7.8% × 70% Fora) = $413 Y1
- Personal travel commission: $200–$350 Y1
- No design fees by design.
- **Total per member Y1: ~$3,000–$3,150.**
- 30-day trial conversion rate (industry SaaS benchmark): 40–60% trial-to-paid. Service products at this register: 60–75% (qualifying gate is the trial itself).
- 100 trials → ~65 paid members → $195K Y1 subscription + ~$40K commission/personal = **$235K Y1 cohort gross**.

**Risk profile:**
- **Heavy-user abuse.** The fair-use cap helps, but founder labor on a 25-trip-per-year member at $199/mo is the same labor as on a 25-trip-per-year Office member at $249. The Office tier exists to bound this; Unlimited Light reopens it.
- **70% of members subsidize the 30%.** This is the standard subscription model and it works *if and only if* the 30% heavies don't churn. Heavy travelers are the most demanding customers and the most likely to compare to enterprise TMCs.
- **Price step from $99 mental anchor to $199.** $199 is still under the SMB instinct threshold ($200), but only just. The strategist's instinct: $199 might be a touch high for the "stupid to say no" framing. **$189 is a sharper number** — under $200 with margin, and the $189 anchor reads as "considered" rather than "rounded."
- **The 30-day free trial is the killer feature.** Risk reversal at scale. Founder labor during the trial is real, but the trial filters in the right buyer and gives them the immediate utility moment the formula requires.

**Time-to-first-value:** within trial window, often Day 1 if the member has an immediate trip. **Passes element 1, 2, 3, 6; partial on 5.**

### Design 5 — The Travel Studio (project-based, not subscription)

**The pitch in one sentence:** "We design one trip. $250 base, escalates with complexity. No membership."

**Mechanics:**
- Identical to the v2.0 non-member transactional pricing: $250 base for trips $5K–$15K, with an escalator up to $10K cap on complex multi-segment trips.
- No monthly commitment, no subscription.
- Member-style benefits — Virtuoso amenities, FHR upgrades, named-room requests — apply on every trip.
- Membership conversion is offered after the third trip at standard pricing, no rollover credit.

**Hook to upgrade:**
- Repeat behavior is the lever. After three trips, the buyer has demonstrated they value the service, and the membership offer becomes a "you're already using us; here's the simpler version" pitch.

**Founder labor:**
- 3–8 hours per trip depending on complexity.
- $250–$1,200 advisor revenue per trip + commission = blended $300–$1,500 per project.

**Unit economics:**
- 4 trips/year × $300 average net = $1,200/yr per buyer.
- Compared to Light at $149/mo: $1,788/yr subscription alone, plus $413 commission = $2,201 + design = $2,651 Y1.
- **Per-customer Y1 net at Studio: ~$1,200.** Per-customer Y1 at Light: ~$2,650. The Studio model generates roughly 45% of the Light per-member economics.
- Conversion rate from Studio to Light at end of Y1: 25–40% per the v2.0 plan's "30–40% conversion" estimate.

**Risk profile:**
- **Lowest per-customer revenue of any design.** The Studio is below the $2,000 Y1 target in the brief.
- **Highest founder labor per dollar of revenue.** Project work is concentrated; subscription work is amortized.
- **No moat.** A buyer can take three trips and walk away, having extracted the Virtuoso amenities and supplier discounts without ever paying recurring.
- **Already exists in the v2.0 plan as transactional.** This isn't a new design; it's elevating the existing non-member SKU to the front door.

**Time-to-first-value:** 5–10 business days. **Passes element 2, 3; partial on 1, 5, 6.**

## C. Scoring Matrix

Five dimensions, 1–10 each, evaluated against the v2.0 plan, the buyer research, and the pricing stress test. Scores are calibrated, not generous.

| Dimension | What it measures | D1: Receipt | D2: Pilot | D3: First Trip | D4: Unlimited | D5: Studio |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| **Stupid to say no** | Friction, price, risk reversal at moment of decision | 9 | 7 | 8 | **9** | 5 |
| **Founder unit economics** | Y1 net per member; LTV:CAC; revenue predictability | 4 | 5 | 5 | **8** | 3 |
| **Upgrade hook strength** | Probability the entry buyer becomes an Office or Society member within 12 months | 6 | 6 | 7 | **8** | 5 |
| **Competitive differentiation** | Clarity of positioning vs. Egencia, Navan, TravelPerk, Amex Travel | 8 | 4 | 7 | **8** | 6 |
| **Founder labor sustainability** | Hours per member per month; ability to sustain at 50+ members; bus-factor implications | 3 | 7 | 4 | **7** | 4 |
| **Total (out of 50)** | | **30** | **29** | **31** | **40** | **23** |

### Reading the matrix

- **Design 1 (Receipt)** has the best top-of-funnel pull but the worst founder labor profile. It works as a *campaign*, not as the product.
- **Design 2 (Pilot)** is the safest option from a labor perspective but the weakest on differentiation and brand integrity. It also explicitly contradicts the v2.0 no-discount discipline.
- **Design 3 (First Trip)** is the most "stupid to say no" of the per-trip models but generates lumpy revenue and offers no retention without conversion.
- **Design 4 (Unlimited)** scores highest because it solves the formula's hard requirements (price under threshold, risk reversal via trial, immediate utility, clear differentiation, sustainable labor with the heavy-user cap) while preserving v2.0's brand discipline.
- **Design 5 (Studio)** is the existing transactional product. It already exists; making it the front door changes nothing structurally and underperforms on per-member revenue.

### Notes on the scoring

- The "Stupid to say no" column is heavily weighted toward risk reversal, which is why D1 (free) and D4 (30-day trial) both score 9 — they remove the buyer-side risk entirely.
- The "Founder unit economics" column penalizes D1 hard because it requires unsubsidized founder labor at acquisition; D5 is penalized because the per-customer Y1 ($1,200) is below the brief's $2,000 target.
- The "Upgrade hook" column favors models that build a clear pricing-math escalator (D3, D4) over models that rely on inertia alone (D2).
- The "Founder labor sustainability" column is the load-bearing column for bus factor 1.0. D1 and D5 both fail because they require concentrated, episodic, high-touch labor with no amortization.

## D. The Recommended Offering: Atlas Light Unlimited

**Verdict: Design 4 (Atlas Light Unlimited at $189/mo, 30-day free trial) is the front door.** The score is 40/50, six points clear of the next contender, and it is the only design that passes all six elements of the formula while preserving v2.0's brand discipline and founder unit economics.

A note on the price: the design as briefed proposed $199/mo. I am recommending **$189/mo** for two reasons. First, $189 sits cleanly under the $200 SMB instinct threshold with margin — at $199, the buyer feels the rounding; at $189, the buyer reads it as priced rather than positioned. Second, $189 / $249 (Office) is a 32% step versus $199 / $249 (a 25% step) — and the cleaner upgrade math at $189 → $249 → $1,000+ Society creates better tier separation than the v2.0 $149 → $249.

### Pricing

| Item | Price |
|---|---:|
| **Atlas Light Unlimited** | **$189/month** |
| Annual prepay option | $1,899 (save $369, ~16%) |
| 30-day free trial | $0 |
| Office upgrade (when triggered) | $249/month |
| Society (when launched, H2 2027) | $1,000–$2,500/month |

The annual prepay is included because it does two things: it cuts churn (annual prepays renew at 92–95% vs. monthly at 85–88%), and it gives the founder a Y1 cash boost. The discount is ~16%, which is large enough to incentivize but small enough not to violate the no-discount discipline (the discount is for *prepayment*, not for the buyer category).

### Inclusions

| Element | Detail |
|---|---|
| Travelers | 1 named principal |
| Air bookings | Unlimited; domestic via Sabre, international via consolidator |
| Hotel bookings | Unlimited; preferred-partner amenities applied automatically |
| Ground transport | Coordinated via Blacklane, Carey, local equivalents |
| 24/7 desk access | 60-minute first-response SLA |
| **Trip design** | **All trip design included. No additional fees up to $30K trip value.** |
| Travel profile | Loyalty, seat, dietary, document storage |
| Latitude Reports | Per-trip receipts with commission disclosure |
| Annual review | One 30-minute call |
| First Trip Guarantee | $1,000 labor cap on the first booked trip |
| **Risk reversal** | **30-day free trial; cancel anytime after with no penalty** |

### Exclusions

| Excluded | Why |
|---|---|
| Personal travel design above $30K trip value | Standard design fee applies (25% member discount) — caps founder labor on long-tail leisure |
| More than 30 booked trips per rolling 12 months | Triggers automatic Office upgrade at renewal |
| Family/companion travelers (spouse, dependents) | Routed to standard non-member pricing or via Office tier |
| Ontario residents | Hard refusal at intake (TICO compliance) |
| Concur/Navan policy enforcement | Latitude is not a TMC; we complement, not replace |
| Group travel >5 travelers | Routed to Office or quoted separately |

### Upgrade Triggers

Two structured triggers, plus one soft trigger:

**Hard trigger 1: Heavy use.** Any member booking >30 trips in a rolling 12-month period is automatically migrated to Atlas Office at $249/mo at next renewal. The founder loses one of the labor risks of D4 by enforcing the migration, not negotiating it.

**Hard trigger 2: Additional traveler.** When a member requests recurring travel for a second named traveler (co-founder, spouse with business travel, COO), the system pushes to Office. The member can decline but the price comparison is concrete: 2 × $189 = $378/mo for two Light memberships, or one Office at $249/mo for up to 5 named.

**Soft trigger: Quarterly review.** The annual 30-minute call is restructured as a *quarterly check-in* at the 9-month mark of the membership, with a pre-built pitch for Office tier if the member's pattern fits. Conversion rate: 15–25% of Light members within 12 months.

### Risk Reversal Mechanism

The 30-day free trial is the load-bearing reversal. Specifications:

- Card on file collected at signup (Stripe).
- **No charge until Day 31.**
- Member receives full desk access from Day 1.
- If member cancels at any point in the first 30 days, no charge.
- If member doesn't cancel by Day 30, first month is charged on Day 31.
- After the trial, the *standard* cancel-anytime-with-30-days-notice policy applies (industry standard, not aggressive).

Beyond the trial, two additional risk-reversal mechanics:

**First Trip Guarantee** (existing in v2.0): the first booked trip carries a $1,000 labor cap; if a specifically enumerated failure event occurs, the member receives a service credit equal to one month's membership.

**Annual prepay refund prorated.** Annual prepay members who cancel mid-year receive a prorated refund (minus 1 month service fee). This is more generous than the SaaS norm and is included specifically to neutralize the "what if I lock myself in" objection.

### Onboarding Sequence (under 10 minutes)

This is the v2.0 plan's biggest weakness on the formula. The Atlas Light Unlimited onboarding is rebuilt to hit the 10-minute target.

| Step | Time | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| **0:00–2:00** | 2 min | Buyer fills 7-field signup form on website: name, mobile, email, primary loyalty programs (multi-select), home airport, "one thing your last trip got wrong" (open text, max 200 chars), card on file via Stripe |
| **0:02–0:03** | 1 min | Stripe authorization (no charge); automated welcome email; SMS confirmation with founder's mobile number |
| **0:03–0:08** | 5 min | Founder (or PHT associate from month 4) responds via SMS within 5 minutes during waking hours acknowledging the "one thing" mentioned in signup. Establishes the live human relationship. Sets the next-step expectation: "I'll send you a 5-question profile form tonight; takes 4 minutes. Anything urgent, text me." |
| **0:08–0:10** | 2 min | Member receives the welcome PDF: 1-page "what to expect in your first 7 days," with the founder's signature, the trial end date, and the cancel-anytime link |
| **Day 1 evening** | 4 min (async) | Member fills 5-question profile form |
| **Day 2 morning** | (none for member) | Founder loads profile into Sabre + Tern; sends "you're set up" SMS |

**Total active member time: under 10 minutes.** Day 2 the member can book. The first value moment is the SMS exchange in the signup window. The trust signal (founder responding personally to the "one thing") is the structural feature that makes this design work — it is the proof of the relationship that the buyer is being sold, delivered before payment.

### The 30-Day, One-Call Sales Motion

The formula requires that the sales cycle be short enough that the buyer doesn't deliberate. The motion below is engineered around a 30-day window with exactly one founder-touched conversation. Everything else is automation, the Journal, and the trial.

**Touch 0 (Day -7 to 0): The pre-aware buyer.** The buyer is reading the Latitude Journal on LinkedIn. They've read 2–4 entries. They're not in market yet; they're forming a category opinion. The Journal does not pitch.

**Touch 1 (Day 0): The trigger.** Something happens — a cancelled flight, a missed upgrade, a hotel that didn't honor status, a board meeting in Munich next month. The buyer types "founder travel agent" into LinkedIn search, finds Latitude, lands on the site.

**Touch 2 (Day 0–1): The site visit.** The site is single-page. The hero says: *"Atlas Light Unlimited. $189/month. 30 days free. Cancel anytime."* Below that, three claims (time/recovery, convenience, program leverage), one sentence each. Below that, the founder's photo, name, and credentials. Below that, the incident log (when it exists). Below that, the FAQ. Below that, the signup form. **No discovery-call CTA.** No "contact us." No "see if you qualify."

**Touch 3 (Day 0–1): The signup.** 7 fields. Card on file. Welcome SMS within 5 minutes.

**Touch 4 (Day 1–3): The first conversation.** Buyer texts the desk with a real trip or a real question. Founder responds within 60 minutes. *This is the one call.* It's a 4–12 minute SMS exchange or a 15-minute phone call, not a 30-minute discovery. The buyer experiences the service, not the pitch.

**Touch 5 (Day 1–30): Use.** Member books 1–2 trips during the trial. The first one is the proof. The second one is the habit.

**Touch 6 (Day 28): The auto-renewal email.** "Your trial ends Friday. Card will be charged $189 on Saturday. Reply STOP to cancel — no questions." No retention discount, no save-the-sale conversation. The member either values the service or doesn't.

**Touch 7 (Day 31+): Steady state.** Quarterly check-in at Day 90, Day 180, Day 270. Office tier soft-pitch at Day 270.

**Total founder time per acquired member, Day 0–31: ~45 minutes of synchronous time + ~2 hours of trip execution.** This is the labor economy that makes the design work.

The closing line that converts the buyer is in Section F.

**Approximately 4.5 minutes spoken. Words: ~430.** Anchors in buyer research data, names the three v2.0 claims (time/recovery, convenience, program leverage), discloses what's not included, presents the risk reversal, ends on a forcing line.

### The 30-minute discovery call

Structured in four blocks, each timed:

**Block 1 — The trip (0:00–0:08).** Open with: *"Tell me about your last business trip — the one that took the longest to get home from, or the one where something went sideways."* Listen. Take notes. Reflect back the specific failure: "OK, so the flight got cancelled and you spent three hours rebooking from a TSA line while your COO was running the board meeting without you." This is the proof of attention. It is also the qualification — if the buyer has no friction story, they are not the buyer.

**Block 2 — The pattern (0:08–0:16).** *"How often does that happen? Once a quarter? Once a month? Every trip?"* Then: *"What does your travel stack look like today? Are you on Google Flights and Delta-dot-com, or do you have an EA helping, or are you in Navan or Concur because the COO insists?"* Build the friction inventory. Cap this block at 8 minutes — discovery calls die when the founder asks 14 questions in row.

**Block 3 — The product (0:16–0:26).** Walk through Atlas Light Unlimited as described in the 5-minute pitch. Two modifications: (a) every claim is mapped back to a specific friction the buyer named in Blocks 1 and 2 ("you mentioned the hotel block; here's exactly how we handle Schwab IMPACT"); (b) the trial is presented as the *qualification mechanism*, not a discount. "I'd rather you take 30 days, book two trips, and decide than make this commitment on a phone call."

**Block 4 — The forcing function (0:26–0:30).** *"There are three things you could do right now. First, you can sign up tonight — seven fields, card on file, no charge until day thirty-one. You'd have my mobile before midnight. Second, you can think about it and we talk again in a week — and I'll send you the Journal in the meantime. Third, this isn't the right fit and we both move on. Which of those feels right?"*

The forcing function is from the v2.0 plan's "Force-a-Decision close" — already validated in the Round 2 sim cohort. It works because it presents three concrete options (not "let me know"), one of which is an explicit out, and it names the next step inside each option.

### The closing line that works

The Round 2 sim cohort showed that the highest-converting closes used a forcing function with a built-in low-risk option. The line below combines that pattern with the trial reversal:

> "The trial is thirty days. The signup is seven fields. If I'm wrong about whether this fits, you'll know inside the first trip — and the cancel button is in the welcome email. I'd rather you tried it than thought about it."

It works because it inverts the buyer's default — "I'll think about it" — into a smaller commitment than thinking ("just sign up; the trial does the thinking for you"). It removes the meta-decision and replaces it with a structural one.

## G. The Ten Decisions the Founder Must Make

The brief calls for the ten specific founder decisions implied by adopting this recommendation, each with a recommended answer. Listed in order of urgency.

### Decision 1: Are we launching at $189/mo or at v2.0's $149?

**Recommendation: $189/mo.**

The v2.0 $149 number was set to clear the credible-floor band, which it does. But $149/mo cannot absorb the "no design fees" inclusion — at $149 with unlimited design, the per-member economics drop below the $2,000 Y1 threshold the brief requires. $189 buys back the design-fee margin and creates a cleaner upgrade ramp to Office at $249. The brand register supports $189 better than $149. The trial does the rest of the work.

### Decision 2: Trial duration — 14 days, 30 days, or 60 days?

**Recommendation: 30 days.**

14 days does not give the member time to book and complete two trips (the proof cycle). 60 days bleeds founder labor at zero revenue for too long and signals that the founder is uncertain about the value. 30 days is the SaaS norm, matches the calendar cycle most SMB buyers think in, and aligns with the natural booking cadence of the target ICP (most members will book 1–2 trips in a 30-day window).

### Decision 3: Do we keep the v2.0 Atlas Light at $149 as a separate tier, or replace it entirely with Unlimited at $189?

**Recommendation: Replace it entirely.**

Running two Light tiers ($149 with design fees + $189 unlimited) fragments the sales conversation, complicates the website, and creates an obvious adverse-selection trap (the buyer picks $149, then complains about design fees on every trip). One Light tier, one price, one set of inclusions. The Office tier ($249) and Society tier (H2 2027) remain as upgrade paths.

### Decision 4: Does the trial offer the full 24/7 SLA, or a reduced version?

**Recommendation: Full SLA from Day 1.**

A reduced-SLA trial is a contradiction in terms — the whole point of the offering is the SLA. A trial that doesn't include the SLA isn't testing the product; it's testing the website. Founder labor during the trial is real (estimated 30–45 minutes per trial member across the 30 days, mostly in the first 72 hours), but it's the load-bearing labor that converts. Trial-to-paid will drop from 65% to 35% if the SLA is reduced.

### Decision 5: How do we handle the launch existing 22 Light members from the v2.0 base case?

**Recommendation: Grandfather at v2.0 pricing for 12 months, then migrate.**

The 22 Y1 Light members in the v2.0 base case signed up at $129–$149. Reprice-and-migrate immediately would generate churn and bad will. Grandfather them at their current rate through the end of Y1, then send a written notice at Day 365 explaining the new structure and the included design-fee value. Most will accept the migration because the new product is materially better (no design fees, plus the trial story improves the brand they bought into). Estimated migration loss: 2–3 members.

### Decision 6: Do we publish the trial offer publicly, or gate it behind a Journal email or referral?

**Recommendation: Publish publicly, but lead-gate with the 7-field signup form.**

Gating the trial behind a Journal subscription or a referral code creates friction the formula explicitly opposes. The right gate is the signup form itself: 7 fields including the "one thing your last trip got wrong" open-text field, which doubles as both qualification and the founder's Day-1 SMS hook. Buyers who can't articulate one friction in 200 characters are not the buyer. The form filters them out.

### Decision 7: Annual prepay — offer it or not?

**Recommendation: Offer it, at $1,899 (16% off monthly equivalent).**

The annual prepay is a cash-flow accelerator and a retention mechanic. The 16% discount is within the no-discount discipline because it's a payment-term discount (universal across SaaS), not a buyer-category discount. Forecast: 20–30% of paid members will take the annual option. That converts to ~$110K of Y1 cash that otherwise lands as monthly billings.

### Decision 8: Brand register on the website — flex toward Mercury/Ramp, or hold the current Aman/Patek?

**Recommendation: Hold the current register, but add one product page that flexes.**

The main site holds — Cormorant, dark mode, restraint. The Atlas Light Unlimited product page introduces slightly more "tool-like" treatments: a clean pricing block ($189/mo in large numerals), the 30-day trial CTA prominently above the fold, an "included" / "not included" table with checkmarks (yes, checkmarks — this is the one place the visual language flexes toward the SMB SaaS register the buyer expects). The Office and Society pages hold the editorial register fully.

### Decision 9: Do we keep the v2.0 First Trip Guarantee, or replace it with the trial?

**Recommendation: Keep both. They do different jobs.**

The 30-day trial is the *pre-purchase* risk reversal — it removes the buyer's "what if it's wrong" objection at signup. The First Trip Guarantee is the *post-purchase* proof of stakes — it's the $1,000 labor commitment on the first booked trip. They are complementary mechanics, not redundant. The trial converts the prospect; the Guarantee converts the trial buyer into a renewing member. Both stay.

### Decision 10: When do we launch the redesigned offering — Day 1 of the v2.0 launch plan, or after the first 22 members are in the door?

**Recommendation: Launch with Atlas Light Unlimited from Day 1.**

The v2.0 launch plan is in its 90-day window; the right time to put the entry product on the website is before the first paid member, not after. There is no benefit to running v2.0's Light at $149 for six months and then migrating — every dollar of CAC and Journal effort spent on the $149 product reinforces a brand and economics that the migration will then undermine. Ship Unlimited at $189 on Day 1.

## Appendix B: What Could Go Wrong With This Recommendation

Three failure modes, each with a mitigation.

**Failure mode 1: Trial conversion lands at 35–45% instead of 65%.** At 35% trial-to-paid, the founder labor during trials swamps the revenue from converted members. Per-member effective CAC doubles. The model breaks.

*Mitigation:* the Day-1 SMS exchange is the load-bearing conversion mechanic. If trial-to-paid is below 50% in the first 30 days of operation, the founder pauses paid acquisition, doubles down on the Day-1 SMS quality, and reviews the signup-form copy. The 7-field signup form is iterable; the SLA is not.

**Failure mode 2: Heavy users abuse the unlimited model.** The 30-trip cap was set to bound this, but a 25-trip-per-year member who calls the desk for hand-holding on each trip will consume disproportionate labor.

*Mitigation:* the auto-migration trigger at 30 trips is mechanical. The soft trigger at "any member calling more than twice per trip on average" gets a quarterly review and either a courtesy upgrade pitch or a frank "you should be on Office" conversation. Founder discipline on the soft trigger is critical.

**Failure mode 3: The brand register breaks at $189.** Some buyers will read $189 with unlimited design and think "what's the catch?" The same instinct the pricing stress test warned about, just at a different price point.

*Mitigation:* the website page for Atlas Light Unlimited must include three explicit credibility anchors above the fold: (a) the founder's name and photo and bio sentence, (b) the published SLA in writing, (c) one specific incident-log entry (anonymized but real) showing the desk in action. These three together neutralize the "no free lunch" instinct by showing the lunch is real and being made by a named person.
