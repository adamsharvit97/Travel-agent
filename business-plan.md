# Latitude 43

### A travel office for principals who travel for work.

**Prepared:** May 2026
**Operating entity:** Latitude 43 Inc. (Ontario CCPC; dba forthcoming)
**Host agency:** Fora Travel (US ARC/IATA, CLIA, state seller-of-travel registrations)
**Launch market:** United States (TICO Path A — US-only sales)
**Document version:** 3.1 — Model B Unlimited canonical, expanded operator depth, replaces v2.0 and v3.0

---

## 1. Executive summary

Latitude 43 is a members' travel office for US-based principals. The buyers are founders of 20-to-80-person startups post-Series A, partners at sub-$1.5B VC and PE funds, operating partners at $500M-$2B PE shops, principals at sub-$1B AUM RIAs, and solo professionals billing $500K+/year. They travel 18 to 35 nights per year for work and have personally absorbed enough avoidable failures (missed upgrades, walked rooms, 11pm hold-music with Amex Travel, hotel categories misbooked by Concur, status credits that never coded) that the friction has become a tax on their identity. They will not pay agency design fees on every trip. They will pay a flat membership. That is the wedge.

The product is sold as two active membership tiers and one invite-only tier. **Atlas Light Unlimited** is $189 a month for one traveler. **Atlas Office Unlimited** is $349 a month for up to five named travelers, three active concurrent, with the principal's EA included as a Companion Seat. Both tiers include all trip design at no incremental fee, including the principal's personal travel. The pitch is one sentence: "Light $189 a month, one traveler. Office $349 a month, up to five travelers and your EA, everything included." **Latitude Society** is the invite-only tier at $1,000 to $2,500 per month, capped at twelve to fifteen households globally, with a 15-minute SLA and a quarterly in-person Year-in-Review. Society launches H2 2027, deferred from the original Q3 2026 target after founder capacity review.

The plan rests on three mechanics. First, **subscription is one hundred percent retained**. Membership revenue does not split with Fora. Second, **commissions split with Fora on a tiered ladder**: 70/30 at start, 80/20 at $300K of annual personal sales, 90/10 at $2M. Virtuoso clips 2% off hotel commissions before the split. The blended effective hotel commission landing in the advisor's hands is roughly 6.5% to 7.0% at Year 1 cohort mix, ramping to 7.8% by Year 3 once the split moves to 80/20 and the supplier mix matures. Third, **all trip design is included for members**. Design fees survive only as a qualifying mechanism for non-member project work and as a feeder into the membership.

The shape of the business is a lifestyle practice. It is not venture-backable and not built for a near-term exit. Year 5 base case revenue is approximately **$795K** under realistic-attach assumptions, $870K in a clean operating year with full personal-travel attach, and $1.6M in a bull case that requires Society at full capacity by Year 5. The founder operates from Toronto under Fora's US registrations, sells only to US residents (TICO Path A), bears no outside capital, and treats the operation as supplemental income on top of a separate primary income (Renmac) for the first 18 to 24 months. The Y1 honest planning number is **12 to 18 closed members generating $60K to $80K of revenue**, not the 30 closes implied by an optimistic cold-funnel read. The 30-close number is achievable only at the upper end of cold-conversion and is not the case to plan around.

The pivot from v2.0 (Model A, $99/$249 plus a design-fee escalator) to v3.0/3.1 (Model B Unlimited) is the most consequential decision in the plan. It was made after 30 sales simulations across three pricing models, three First Trip Guarantee mechanics, and five sales-cycle stages (Rounds 6 and 7, archived in `/sales-kit/r7-sims/` and `/sales-kit/r3-sims/`). The data found one consistent pattern: the design-fee escalator was the unanswerable question in cold sales. No rep could answer "what's the all-in?" with a single number under Model A. Every rep could under Model B. The same buyer (Vanessa Coyne, sim cohort) closed Light $99 under Model A and Office $349 under Model B with no incremental friction, a 253% revenue lift on the same person. The cost of the change is acceptance of one structural risk: the marginal cost of every "free" design is the founder's hour. That risk is bounded by a 3-active-traveler cap on Office, a Philippine VA hire at month 4 to 6, and a quarterly trip-pattern review documented in §8.

This document is the operating bible for the next 18 months. Revisions track the labor-margin reality at the end of each quarter and will revise pricing if and only if a Year 2 quarterly review shows labor-negative members exceeding five percent of the book.

## 2. The pricing and offering

### 2.1 The three tiers

| Tier | Price | Coverage | Design fees | SLA | Cohort cap |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Atlas Light Unlimited** | $189/mo ($2,268/yr) | 1 traveler | Zero. All trip design included, business and personal. | 60 min, 24/7 | None |
| **Atlas Office Unlimited** | $349/mo ($4,188/yr) | Up to 5 named, 3 active concurrent. EA Companion Seat included. | Zero. All design included, principal's family leisure included. | 60 min, 24/7 | None |
| **Atlas Family Office** | $349/mo (variant of Office) | Rolling roster up to 20 named, 5 active. Pre-signed NDA, default-off marketing, flat-fee event SKU above $30K | Zero (events à la carte above $30K) | 60 min, 24/7 | None |
| **Latitude Society** | $1,000–$2,500/mo ($12K–$30K/yr) | Invite-only. Dedicated principal. Event design above $30K à la carte. | Event design billed à la carte above $30K | 15 min, 24/7 | 12–15 globally |

The pricing pair ($189 / $349) was selected after R7 stress-tested the credible floor. Three findings drove the numbers. First, $99 Light failed the credible-floor test in R6 because it attracted freemium-shoppers whose downstream behavior produced negative contribution margin. Second, $189 holds against $99 in the same demographic because the buyer is paying for the absence of a quote conversation, not a low monthly. Third, the $349 Office number is the lowest price at which the design-fee inclusion stops looking like a giveaway and starts looking like a product. Below $349, sophisticated buyers ask "what's the catch?" Above $399, the same buyers ask "is this actually worth it?" $349 is the narrow band where both questions go quiet.

### 2.2 What "everything included" actually means

For an Atlas Office Unlimited member at $349/mo, the member receives the following at no incremental cost beyond the subscription:

- **All trip design.** Itineraries, hotel selection and supplier negotiation, restaurant reservations, ground transfers, status match work, IROP handling, rebooking, hotel walk recovery. Documented in `/operations/scenarios-book.md`.
- **All booking work.** Air, hotel, car, rail, restaurant, on-site experiences.
- **Personal and family travel.** Anniversary trips, family ski week, summer in Europe, spouse-led leisure. Limited only by the 3-active-concurrent cap.
- **Virtuoso benefits.** Room upgrades when available, daily breakfast for two, $100 property credit, late check-out, occasionally a fourth-night-free on selected properties. The desk applies these as a default on every preferred-partner stay; the member sees them on the receipt.
- **Brand programs.** Four Seasons Preferred Partner, Marriott STARS, Hyatt Privé, Belmond Bellini, Mandarin Oriental Fan Club, Hilton Impresario, IHG Luxe, Rosewood Elite, Aman, Belmond, Capella, Como. Equivalent amenity stacks at the brand level.
- **24/7 desk coverage with 60-minute SLA.** The founder personally covers 7am to 11pm ET, all days. PHT VA covers 11pm to 7am ET starting month 4 to 6. Backup principal pre-staged at Fora for vacation and illness.
- **EA Companion Seat.** The principal's executive assistant or chief-of-staff gets their own Light-level access to the desk. Same 60-min SLA, no Society-tier features. Documented in `/operations/onboarding-kit.md`.
- **Standing instructions.** Room preferences, dietary restrictions, allergies, seat preferences, ground vehicle preferences, lounge memberships, points programs stored once and applied every trip. Maintained in the encrypted member vault.
- **Per-trip receipts with commission disclosure.** Every booking reports the property paid commission and the rate the member received. This is a deliberate trust signal and a defense against the "what's the conflict?" objection.
- **First Trip Guarantee.** See §2.3.
- **The First 30.** 90-day no-fault exit on the membership. Monthly Stripe billing. No multi-year contracts.
- **Quarterly trip-pattern review.** A 15-minute call to flag overspend, under-utilization, or rotation issues. The principal owns this review.
- **Annual Year-in-Review.** Written debrief covering trips taken, spend by category, rewards captured, IROPs handled, three recommendations for the coming year. Sent early December. Documented in `/operations/year-in-review-template.html`.

What is **not** included: airfare itself, hotel room rates, restaurant tabs, ground transport fares, or anything the member would pay direct to a supplier. Latitude does not mark up suppliers. Suppliers pay Latitude a commission; the member pays the supplier the same rate they would pay direct.

### 2.3 First Trip Guarantee — mechanic matched to sales-cycle stage

After R6 and R7 sim data, the FTG is matched to the sales-cycle stage where the guarantee is invoked, not to the pricing tier. Three mechanics are operational:

**FTG1 — Month plus trip-value labor credit.** "If your first trip fails on our end, we refund the month's membership and credit the greater of $1,000, the trip value, or three months of dues toward the rework." Deployed at cold inbound discovery and during live IROP saves. The trip-value-or-three-months clause was added after R7 Sim 8 surfaced that the original $1K labor cap was undersized for Office-tier complex trips. The cap on the credit is the trip value of the named trip, not unbounded.

**FTG2 — Full refund plus free month plus service credit.** "If your first trip fails on our end, we refund the month, give you the next month free, and issue a $500 service credit for a future trip." Deployed at mid-year retention saves and Family Office onboarding. Maximum trust signal where trust has eroded or never existed. Total downside to the firm: ~$700 plus opportunity cost.

**FTG3 — 30-day money-back guarantee.** "30-day money-back on the membership itself. Doesn't matter why. You decide if it was worth it; if not, you get the month back, no questions." Deployed at new-buyer onboarding and price-negotiator closes. The simplest mechanic; deflects most legal-leaning objections; tested cleanly in R7 Sim 12 (`/sales-kit/r7-sims/sim-12-modelC-FTG3-negotiation.md`).

The FTG is declared on the sales call and named on a single trip ("the named trip"). It is not a perpetual guarantee. It expires once the named trip closes successfully or 90 days, whichever comes first. After that, retention is earned, not insured.

**Year 1 expected invocation rate:** 15-25% of new members in Year 1, decreasing to under 10% by Year 3 as the operating model matures. This is corrected upward from the earlier "low probability" rating after the v3.0 evaluation flagged Year 1 as inherently higher-failure. The FTG dollar exposure at 25% Year 1 invocation against 18 closed members is bounded at roughly $5,000 to $9,000, modeled into Y1 opex.

### 2.4 Non-member design work

Non-members can engage Latitude on a project basis. The schedule:

- $500 floor on any trip planning engagement. Below $500, refer out to a Fora colleague.
- $250 base plus $50 per $1,000 of trip value, from $5,000 to $15,000 of trip value.
- $750 base plus $75 per $1,000, from $15,000 to $30,000.
- $1,875 base plus $100 per $1,000, above $30,000.
- Capped at $10,000 per trip.
- Paid in full before design begins. No exceptions for new prospects; existing members can carry small balances.

**The purpose of non-member design pricing is to qualify in.** A prospect who pays $500 to $2,000 to use Latitude once converts to Atlas Light at renewal at roughly 30 to 40% within 12 months when the founder runs the explicit conversion mechanic. That mechanic, missing from earlier versions, is: the desk sends a follow-up 30 days after trip completion offering "credit your design fee against your first three months of Atlas Light." This converts the design fee from sunk cost to deposit. Without that mechanic, the conversion rate drops to roughly 10 to 15%. The Year 1 plan tracks design-fee-to-membership conversion as a KPI in the dashboard (Appendix C).

### 2.5 Pricing rationale and the repricing protocol

The published list price is the price. There is no "starting bid." This is enforced for two reasons. First, the book is small enough that two members will compare notes within a quarter of close, and a $50 discount on member A becomes a $50 expectation on member B. Second, the founder's negotiation discipline depends on having nothing to give away. The three rehearsed price holds, in order of preference: (a) multi-year lock at list with price protection on renewal, (b) "founder rate" naming convention with no dollar discount, (c) per-head discount only at five-plus seats with documented justification. Concessions outside this menu require principal approval and are logged in the CRM.

**The repricing protocol.** Latitude will not reprice in Year 1. The first scheduled price review is the end of Year 2 calendar (December 2027). At that review, three inputs drive the decision: (1) quarterly trip-pattern review data on labor-negative members, (2) commission-yield realization vs. the 7.8% target, (3) close-rate sensitivity from a planned Round 8 sim block testing $229 Light against $189. If labor-negative members exceed five percent of the book, or if commission yield runs below 6.5% sustainably, the Light price moves to $229 and the Office price holds. If both inputs hold, prices stay at $189 / $349 through Year 3.

### 2.6 The buyer value math, dollarized

The single most important section of the offering doc: what the buyer actually receives, dollarized, on top of the membership fee. For an Atlas Office Unlimited member at $349/mo ($4,188/year):

| Component | Annual realized value | Method |
|---|---:|---|
| Virtuoso, FSPP, STARS, Privé benefits — room upgrades, breakfast for two, $100 credit, late check-out — on 20 hotel nights/year at preferred properties | $3,000–$8,000 | $150-$400/night realized amenity value × 20 nights |
| Trip design work at agency rates ($300–$500/trip) × 22 trips/year | $6,600–$11,000 | Bell & Bly published rate: $800/week up to 5 people; midpoint $400 per trip-event |
| 24/7 SLA coverage. Indagare, Brownell, Bell & Bly, Cadence all close at 5-6pm or 9-6 ET. | Priceless on the one trip that matters | Not directly dollarized; reframed as "what did the missed upgrade cost you?" |
| EA Companion Seat — 2 to 4 hours/week of EA labor freed | $5,000–$12,000 | 3 hours/week × 50 weeks × $50/hr fully-loaded EA rate |
| Personal travel design (anniversary, family ski, summer trip) | $3,000–$15,000 | 2 to 4 personal trips × agency rates at $1,500–$4,000 each |
| First Trip Guarantee plus 90-day no-fault exit | Risk reduction | Bounded downside of $4,188 first-month + $500 credit |
| **Total realized value, midpoint** | **$22,000** | |
| **Annual fee** | **$4,188** | |
| **Value-to-price ratio** | **5.3x at midpoint, 4x to 11x range** | |

For Light at $189/mo ($2,268/yr), the same proportional math runs roughly 2x to 4x value-to-price: ~$1,500 to $3,000 of Virtuoso amenity value on 12 to 18 hotel nights, plus ~$2,000 to $5,000 of design work, plus the SLA and the FTG. Total realized value approximately $4,500 to $9,000 against a $2,268 fee.

The point of the Unlimited model is that the Virtuoso amenities and included design alone exceed the annual fee. Everything else — the EA seat, personal travel, FTG, 24/7 SLA — is upside on top of an already-positive trade. "Stupid to say no to" becomes a property of the offering, not of the sales pitch. If the math doesn't independently work for the buyer, no amount of confessional voice fixes it. The math works.

## 3. Ideal customer profile

Latitude 43 sells to five overlapping segments. All five are US-based (TICO Path A constraint). All five are repeat travelers, ten or more nights per year. All five have either a personal frustration with their current setup or an EA whose travel admin is the limiting factor. The five segments are not equal in priority or volume. Year 1 effort is biased toward segments 1, 2, and 4 because those are the segments where the founder has the strongest warm graph entry (the old boss is segment 1, Toronto-Soho-EO network skews segments 1 and 4, PitchBook list-build is most efficient for segment 2).

### 3.1 Series A to Series C founders — primary segment

**Demographics.** Age 32 to 48. CEO or founding co-founder of a 20-to-80-person company, post-Series A, $5M to $50M annual revenue, raised between $10M and $80M cumulative. Geography concentrated in NYC, SF, Boston, Austin, LA, with a meaningful tail in Chicago, Seattle, Miami, Denver. Married or partnered in 70% of cases; 50% have school-age children. Education tilts toward technical or finance backgrounds; the buyer who travels well is usually the buyer who learned to travel through prior roles in management consulting, banking, or large tech.

**Travel and entertainment range.** $80K to $300K annually. The lower bound is the Series A founder traveling 25 to 35 nights per year for fundraising, customer meetings, and board work. The upper bound is the Series C founder traveling 40 to 60 nights, often including a meaningful international component (London, Tel Aviv, Berlin for fundraising; Tokyo, Singapore, São Paulo for customer or partner travel). Average trip cost lands at $2,800 to $5,500 fully loaded (flight + 2 to 3 hotel nights + ground + meals).

**Current setup.** Three configurations dominate. (a) Founder books direct on Expedia, Marriott.com, United.com — labor heavy, no preferred-partner benefits, no IROP recovery. (b) Founder routes through an overworked EA who books on Expedia or Concur — labor offloaded but quality flat. (c) Founder uses Navan or TripActions for business travel and books leisure direct — policy enforcement works, but the experience layer (upgrades, IROP, hotel choice) is absent. None of the three configurations produce upgrades on Virtuoso preferred hotels because none of the three are routed through Virtuoso.

**Pain points.** Three patterns surface in every discovery call. First, the recurring upgrade miss — the Four Seasons or the Aman is booked at the published rate when the same booking through Virtuoso would have included breakfast, a $100 credit, and a category upgrade. Second, the 11pm IROP — flight cancelled, no one to call, hold music with Amex Travel for 90 minutes, hotel category wrong, ground transport not rebooked. Third, the EA fatigue — the EA who started the role excited to manage travel has, by month 18, told the principal twice that travel is the single worst part of the job.

**Why they buy.** Founder-grade narcissism mostly; the principal wants the trip to reflect the seat. But underneath that, the buyer is buying the absence of a recurring source of low-grade humiliation. They want a person to text from a TSA line. They want their EA to have one less thing to mess up. They want to walk into the Four Seasons in London and be recognized.

**Why they churn.** Two patterns. (a) Travel volume drops below the threshold where the membership feels active — the principal traveled six nights in Q1 and starts to question the spend. Mitigation: quarterly trip-pattern review with explicit downsell to Light. (b) The principal hires a new EA who wants to own travel as a competence signal. Mitigation: the EA Companion Seat. When the EA is included in the membership, the EA becomes a defender rather than a displacer.

**Acquisition channels.** Warm introduction from other founders (highest yield). Latitude Journal on LinkedIn. EA-network channels (an EA who is a Latitude defender refers another EA, who refers the principal). Cold email at 30 to 50 per day to founders identified through Crunchbase / PitchBook filtered on Series A-C, $5M-$50M revenue, $10M-$80M cumulative funding.

**Reference customers (named where applicable, pseudonymous where required).** The Year 1 reference bench targets two named segment-1 references by month 6, accessible to prospects on a 48-hour SLA. The "old boss" warm anchor — a senior leader at a public-markets research firm, ~$100K T&E — is the first named reference. Second target: an early Latitude member from the Toronto-NYC startup corridor introduced through brother-in-law's network.

**LTV expectations.** Atlas Light at $189/mo, 4-year average tenure, plus commission realization at full mix: $9,000 to $14,000 LTV per Light segment-1 member. Atlas Office at $349/mo, 4-year average tenure: $32,000 to $42,000 LTV. Year 1 close mix target for segment 1: 6 to 9 closes (4 Light, 2-5 Office).

### 3.2 VC and PE partners at sub-$1.5B funds

**Demographics.** Age 36 to 55. Partner or principal at a $300M to $1.5B fund. Geography: NYC, SF, Boston, LA, Chicago concentration. The sub-$1.5B fund size is the sweet spot — large enough that the partner travels meaningfully (LP meetings, diligence, portfolio support), small enough that the firm does not staff a real corporate travel function. Above $1.5B, the firm typically has a dedicated travel coordinator or routes through Egencia / BCD Travel. Below $300M, the partner is often booking direct and the spend is below the membership justification threshold.

**Travel and entertainment range.** $120K to $500K annually. The partner at a $400M fund averages 45 to 70 nights per year across LP roadshows (heavy Q1 and Q3), diligence travel (highly variable), portfolio support (steady), conferences (SBIA, ACG, NVCA, Upfront Summit, SXSW), and family leisure (Aspen, Tahoe, summer in Europe). Average trip cost: $3,500 to $7,000.

**Current setup.** Most partners route through Concur for company-paid travel and book leisure direct. Some firms have a long-suffering EA who handles travel as 30% of her job. The largest single failure point is the LP roadshow — five cities in seven days, each with a specific dinner, a specific hotel, a specific morning meeting — and the corporate travel platform cannot handle the choreography.

**Pain points.** First, the LP roadshow choreography problem. Second, the dignity-on-the-road problem (the partner does not want the wrong hotel category showing up on the receipt the LP will see). Third, the diligence-trip-canceled-at-11pm problem (the LP meeting is at 8am tomorrow; the flight cancels at 11pm tonight; Concur cannot rebook).

**Why they buy.** They are sophisticated buyers. They read fee structures, they ask about commission splits, they want to see the math (which is why §2.6 is the most-cited section in sales calls). They will pay a flat membership for a structurally cleaner offering. They have absorbed enough $300K of LP-roadshow friction over three years that $4,188 a year feels like the wrong question to ask.

**Why they churn.** Fund vintage changes — the partner moves from Fund III investing-mode to Fund IV-raise-mode and the travel pattern shifts. Also, the partner moves to a $2B+ fund that has its own travel team. Mitigation: the EA Companion Seat survives the transition; the membership follows the principal, not the firm.

**Acquisition channels.** Direct outreach to sub-$1.5B funds via PitchBook (filtered on AUM, vintage, recent fund close). Referrals from existing members (the highest-yield channel for this segment). ACG and SBIA conference presence at Year 2 onward, deferred from Year 1.

**Reference customers.** Targeted: one early-stage VC partner at a $400M-$800M fund, accessible by Year 1 month 9. The reference math for this segment runs differently than segment 1 — the VC partner wants to talk to another VC partner, not to a founder.

**LTV expectations.** Office at $349/mo, 4-year average tenure: $35,000 to $48,000 LTV given higher commission realization on heavier travel volume. Year 1 close mix target for segment 2: 1 to 2 Office closes.

### 3.3 Lower-mid-market PE operating partners

**Demographics.** Age 42 to 58. Senior operating partner at a $500M to $2B PE shop. Geography: NYC, Chicago, Atlanta, Dallas, Boston. The operating partner role is distinct from the deal partner — the operating partner travels heavy (portfolio company visits, ops reviews, integration work) on a predictable cadence and is rarely the decision-maker on fund-level travel platform choices.

**Travel and entertainment range.** $200K to $500K annually, often the highest single T&E spender at the firm. 60 to 90 nights per year is realistic. Heavy domestic travel; international concentration in operations-heavy geographies (UK, Germany, India for portfolio operations).

**Current setup.** Almost universally Amex GBT, Concur, or BCD Travel for company-paid travel, with phone-tree-at-11pm being the single dominant pain point. The operating partner is the buyer who has personally absorbed the most hours of corporate-travel-platform failure of any ICP. They are also the most sophisticated negotiator on price; they will push.

**Pain points.** Phone-tree-at-11pm. Wrong hotel category. Ground transport no-show in Charlotte at 8pm. The portfolio CEO's wife flew to NYC for the closing dinner and the hotel was the wrong neighborhood. None of these are seven-figure problems individually; collectively they are an identity wound.

**Why they buy.** They have the budget. They have the autonomy. They are tired of Amex GBT. They want the trip to reflect the seat. The price negotiation is real and must be handled with the discipline documented in §7.4 (the rehearsed price holds and the concession menu).

**Why they churn.** Fund-level travel policy change — the PE shop centralizes travel and refuses to reimburse a personal membership. Mitigation: position the membership as personal-paid (the partner pays out of carried interest or personal funds), which structurally avoids the policy hurdle. Second mitigation: the Family Office variant supports the partner's family-layer needs at the same price, which converts the membership from a business expense to a household one.

**Acquisition channels.** Direct outreach. ACG (the Association for Corporate Growth) and SBIA (Small Business Investor Alliance) presence in Year 2. VC referrals into the PE ecosystem in Year 1. Cold email at 5 to 10 per week to a tight list of operating partners at $500M-$2B shops.

**Reference customers.** Targeted: one operating partner reference by Year 2 month 9. The reference is highest-yield when it comes from a peer firm rather than a peer fund.

**LTV expectations.** Office or Family Office at $349/mo, 5-year average tenure (this segment has the lowest churn once on-boarded): $40,000 to $55,000 LTV. Year 1 close mix target for segment 3: 0 to 1 close (this segment is a Year 2 onward focus).

### 3.4 RIA principals at sub-$1B AUM firms

**Demographics.** Age 40 to 60. Managing partner or principal of a $200M to $1B AUM RIA. Geography distributed broadly — RIAs are not coastal-concentrated. Concentration in NYC, SF, Chicago, Boston, but meaningful presence in Charlotte, Nashville, Dallas, Denver, Phoenix, Atlanta, Minneapolis. The RIA segment is the most LinkedIn-dense ICP — content marketing works here.

**Travel and entertainment range.** $80K to $200K annually. 25 to 40 nights per year. The travel mix is concentrated in conference season (Schwab IMPACT in October-November, Barron's Top Independent Advisor in March, T3 Tech in January, Future Proof in September) and client travel. The spouse is often the co-decision-maker on which trips happen, which is a sales-call dynamic to surface (lesson 19 in §7.3).

**Current setup.** Most RIA principals are doing it themselves or routing through an office manager who is also handling compliance, payroll, and client onboarding. Custodian platforms (Schwab, Fidelity, Altruist) sometimes offer travel programs but they are deeply ignored.

**Pain points.** Conference season fatigue — five conferences in seven months, each with a specific hotel block negotiated badly. Client travel as brand expression — the principal is hosting a client dinner in Aspen and the hotel category matters. The spouse-as-co-buyer dynamic — half of the principal's travel is family-adjacent (the principal flies to NYC for a meeting, brings the spouse for the weekend; the principal goes to a Barron's conference, the family meets them in Miami after).

**Why they buy.** Identity. The RIA principal sees themselves as a sophisticated buyer; the membership signals that. The Latitude Journal is identity-coded reading — the principal reads it on LinkedIn and recognizes themselves.

**Why they churn.** Custodian-platform travel program improves. Spouse vetoes the spend at renewal. Mitigation: the family-first reframe in §7.3 lesson 19 ("the person who has to be happy in 6 months is not you, it's your spouse").

**Acquisition channels.** Latitude Journal on LinkedIn (primary). Conference-season outreach (October Schwab IMPACT, March Barron's). Custodian-platform partnerships in Year 3+ (Schwab, Fidelity, Altruist co-marketing potential). Referrals.

**Reference customers.** Targeted: one $400M-$700M AUM RIA principal by Year 1 month 9, accessible to prospects.

**LTV expectations.** Light at $189/mo, 5-year average tenure (this segment has high stickiness once trust is established): $13,000 to $18,000 LTV. Office at $349/mo when two-or-three partners share: $35,000 to $45,000 LTV. Year 1 close mix target for segment 4: 3 to 5 closes (2-3 Light, 1-2 Office).

### 3.5 Solo professionals — lawyers, surgeons, M&A bankers, family-office principals

**Demographics.** Age 40 to 65. Solo-practice professional billing $500K+/year. Lawyers (litigation partners, M&A counsel), surgeons (orthopedic, plastic, ophthalmology with cash-pay practices), M&A bankers, family-office principals. Geography distributed; concentration in NYC, LA, Miami, Chicago, Houston, Atlanta.

**Travel and entertainment range.** $60K to $250K annually. 15 to 35 nights per year. Travel mix tilts toward client travel (lawyers and bankers) or family / personal travel (surgeons and family-office principals). Average trip cost runs higher than other segments due to higher willingness to pay for premium hotels: $4,500 to $9,000 fully loaded.

**Current setup.** No EA in most cases. The principal is also the booker. The principal is also the IROP recovery team. This segment is the most personally exhausted by travel admin and the most under-served by corporate-grade tools (which assume an EA exists).

**Pain points.** The principal-as-booker problem. The principal-as-IROP-recoverer problem. The principal-as-points-program-manager problem. They are doing it all themselves and they are doing it badly because they are also performing brain surgery or trying a case.

**Why they buy.** They want their time back. They have absorbed enough self-inflicted travel pain to know that an extra $2,268 a year is not the question. The question is whether the desk can actually be trusted with the booking.

**Why they churn.** Reduced travel due to life change (retirement, practice sale, family illness). Mitigation: this is genuine and unavoidable; off-board well per `/operations/member-lifecycle.md` and keep the relationship file for 36 months in case the principal re-enters.

**Acquisition channels.** Latitude Journal. Podcast guesting (How I Built This, The Practice of Surgery, M&A Science). Direct LinkedIn outreach. Referrals from RIA and VC adjacents (the same RIA principal who is a Latitude member sometimes refers their lawyer, their surgeon, their banker).

**Reference customers.** Targeted: one solo-professional reference by Year 1 month 12. This is the slowest segment to seed a reference for because the buyer is also private about their setup.

### 5.6 The labor-negative member trigger mechanic

The single material risk in Model B is that a high-usage Office member books 40+ designed trips per year against a single $349/mo seat and becomes labor-negative — i.e., the founder's hour cost exceeds the contribution margin from that member. The v3.0 plan acknowledged this risk in narrative but did not document the operational trigger.

**The mechanic, as of v3.1:**

1. **Quarterly hours review.** At the end of each calendar quarter, the founder reviews member-by-member labor hours. The hours are logged in the operations log per `/operations/irop-playbook.md` and the trip-design workflow. Each member has an "implied hours budget" derived from the unit economics:
   - Light: 18 implied hours/year (1.5 hours/month average across booking, design, IROP, standing-instruction maintenance)
   - Office: 60 implied hours/year (5 hours/month average)
   - Society: 200 implied hours/year (16 hours/month, with surge capacity for events)

2. **The 2x threshold.** Any member whose trailing-quarter hours exceed 2x their implied hours (annualized) triggers a flag. Examples: a Light member burning 9+ hours in a quarter (36 hours/year run-rate), or an Office member burning 30+ hours in a quarter (120 hours/year run-rate).

3. **The 3x action threshold.** Any member whose trailing-quarter hours exceed 3x their implied hours triggers one of three actions, decided by the founder within two weeks:
   - **(a) Society upgrade conversation.** The member is offered an upgrade to Society at the appropriate sub-tier. Script: "Your travel pattern has grown beyond what Atlas Office is designed for; let me show you where Society fits."
   - **(b) Coverage adjustment with documented scope reduction.** The desk and the member agree on a narrower coverage scope. Script: "I want to keep serving you well. Let's name the two trip types where you most need the desk, and I'll route the rest to your EA's direct booking with our standing instructions applied."
   - **(c) Friendly off-board.** When the relationship is structurally mismatched. Script: "I'm not the right travel partner for the volume you're operating at. Let me introduce you to a colleague at SmartFlyer who handles this scale."

4. **Documentation requirement.** Each labor-negative trigger is logged in the operations database. The aggregate triggers are reviewed annually. If labor-negative triggers exceed 5% of Office members in any year, the Office price is reviewed at the next annual repricing window.

The mechanic protects the unit economics without forcing a pricing change. In the bear case (5% of Office members labor-negative at Year 5 scale), the dollar impact is approximately $15K-$25K of foregone margin, which is manageable. In the worse case (15% of Office members labor-negative), the impact is $45K-$70K, which would force a Year 4 repricing.

## 7. The sales motion

### 7.1 The pitch in one paragraph (the 90-second elevator)

> "Latitude 43 is a small members' travel office. There are two ways in: Atlas Light at $189 a month for one traveler, or Atlas Office at $349 a month for up to five travelers and your EA. Both include all trip design — no per-trip fees, no surprise invoices. You get the desk on text, sixty minutes, twenty-four hours a day. You get Virtuoso benefits at hotels. Your personal travel is included. If we screw up your first trip, you get the month back, the next month free, and a service credit on the next one. Apply, don't sign up — it's mutual fit."

That is the entire opening. No banned words. No marketing voice. The buyer can repeat it back. The EA can repeat it back to the principal. The 90-second timing is verified — read aloud, the paragraph is 88 to 95 seconds at conversational pace.

### 7.2 The discovery call structure

The full discovery script is documented in `/sales-kit/discovery-playbook.md`. The structure is 20 to 30 minutes, broken into seven beats.

**Beat 1: Opening (2 minutes).** Acknowledge time, set structure, transfer agenda control. No pitch. The opening line: "Thanks for the time. Quick housekeeping — I usually keep these to 20 minutes, then leave the last 10 for your questions. Fair? Before we get into Latitude, I want to understand how you travel today and what's broken."

**Beat 2: Diagnostic (8 minutes).** Five questions, asked in order, listening for 80% of this section.

1. "Walk me through a typical week of travel for you. How many trips, how complex, who books it today?" — Listening for: who controls the booking, how often they redo their own work, EA overload.
2. "What's the worst trip you've had in the last twelve months?" — This is the question. The answer is the pain you sell to.
3. "How often does something go wrong — cancel, delay, hotel walk, ground transport falls through — and what do you do when it does?" — Listening for: do they call Amex? Does the EA scramble? Do they personally fight it?
4. "How do you handle points and status? Are you actively optimizing or just letting it happen?" — Almost universally: "I have an Amex Platinum and a Marriott Bonvoy and I have no idea what I'm doing with them."
5. "What does your assistant do today that you wish was handled differently?" (only if they have an EA) — Listening for: EA is great at calendar, terrible at travel. EA is overloaded. EA quit last quarter.

**Beat 3: Reframe (3 minutes).** Three short statements, slow. "Two patterns I keep seeing. First, most operators in your seat aren't paying for travel — they're paying twice for it. They pay an airline, and they pay the time it takes to clean up what the airline broke. Second, the points and status thing — at your spend level, that's $4,000 to $9,000 a year of upgrades and credits sitting unused." Followed by the Latitude framing: "Latitude 43 is a travel office. One human, on call. Not a booking platform. Not a card concierge. A small office with named members and a published response time." Closing the reframe with the trust transfer: "What I want to test on this call is whether you'd actually benefit from this. If you wouldn't, I'd rather tell you that now than sell you a year of something you don't need." That last sentence is the most important in the entire call.

**Beat 4: The product (4 minutes).** Three pillars in one sentence each. No bullets.

- "What you get: rewards captured on every booking through the same hotel programs the leisure houses use — Virtuoso, Four Seasons Preferred Partner, Marriott STARS, Hyatt Privé."
- "You get one Slack channel, one email, one phone number. Tell us once that you sit in 6A on long-haul, that you want the Connaught in Mayfair, that your EA is named Pat — we remember. Forever."
- "And you get 24/7. First response under sixty minutes, day or night. Not a queue. The desk."

**Beat 5: The price (2 minutes).** State it calmly. Make the prospect choose the tier. "Two ways in: Light at $189 a month, one traveler, all design included; Office at $349 a month, up to five travelers and your EA, all design included. Based on what you described, you're in Office." Then stop talking.

**Beat 6: Their questions (8-10 minutes).** Whatever comes up here is the real conversation. The objection-handling FAQ in `/sales-kit/discovery-playbook.md` covers Amex Platinum, Navan/TravelPerk, "what if my EA already handles this," "isn't this a lot of money," "what if I want to book my own trip," "how do I know you won't disappear in six months," "can I cancel mid-year," "do you handle leisure travel."

**Beat 7: Close (1 minute).** Three closes by signal strength:

- **Strong** ("I'm in"): "Good. Two things. I'll send you the application — short form, ten minutes. Once it's back I'll get on a fifteen-minute call to build your travel profile, and we go live within forty-eight hours."
- **Medium** ("This sounds interesting" / "Let me think"): "Understood. Let me send you a short brief with the comparison to what you're using today. Sleep on it. I'll follow up next Tuesday."
- **Weak** ("I'm probably fine with what I have"): "Fair enough. Let me ask one last thing — if your next IROP costs you four hours of your weekend, will you let me know? Sometimes people only realize the math after they've felt it again."

### 7.3 The 30-lesson playbook

The playbook lessons, validated across R1 through R7, are the operational core of the sales motion. The first 25 carried forward from R1-R5; lessons 26-30 emerged from the R6+R7 Model B testing.

1. **Persona-band fork at diagnostic.** Frugal Series A vs. PE MD vs. CFO vs. delegated buyer — each gets a different reframe and a different tier anchor. The discovery call structure is the same; the language at beat 3 differs.

2. **"No real problems" rebuttal.** When the buyer says "honestly, my travel is mostly fine," the response is: "You absorbed the failures as background noise." Then probe specific failures by month: "Tell me about the worst hotel night you had in the last six months."

3. **Top-tier anchor neutralization in opening 30 seconds for HNW buyers.** For an HNW buyer who walks in expecting a $30K Society pitch, the opener is "you're probably the wrong customer for Atlas — let me tell you where you actually fit." Reframes the conversation away from "is this expensive enough for me."

4. **EA buyer/user split.** "The member is you. The user, most days, is your COS." This is the line that converts the EA from gatekeeper to defender.

5. **First Trip Guarantee offered EARLY, not as save.** The FTG is named in the first half of the discovery call. By the time the close approaches, the FTG is already in the buyer's mental model.

6. **The First 30 program for burned-before buyers.** 90-day no-fault exit. The line: "If we are not the right partner for you in 90 days, you walk. We expect that decision quickly so neither of us wastes a year."

7. **90-second elevator script for CFOs and time-poor.** §7.1. The buyer can repeat it back; that's the test.

8. **Force-a-Decision close.** "Is this a yes, a no, or a need-more-information?" Eliminates the "let me think about it" purgatory.

9. **Holding price.** Never discount. Three rehearsed holds: (a) multi-year lock at list with renewal price protection, (b) "founder rate" naming convention with no dollar discount, (c) per-head discount only at five-plus seats with documented justification. Plus the concession menu in §7.4.

10. **Sophisticated buyer track.** For data-driven buyers (VC, PE, RIA), branch to the 90-day measurement framework. Ban "typically." Speak in dollars and minutes. Show the commission disclosure on receipts.

11. **AI / ChatGPT objection FAQ.** Three structural reasons: (a) AI cannot make a phone call to the hotel manager at 2am; (b) AI cannot maintain the supplier relationship that delivers the suite upgrade; (c) AI cannot take responsibility for an IROP recovery. The buyer who reaches for ChatGPT is the buyer who has not yet had a real 11pm cancellation.

12. **2am Scenarios Library.** Three named stories deployable when the buyer will not admit pain. The Frankfurt cancellation (per `/operations/irop-playbook.md` §6), the Geneva hotel walk, the visa-refused-at-the-gate.

13. **Reference Bench.** Two references per segment, pre-consented, 48-hour SLA. By Year 1 month 9, the bench has 5 to 7 named references. Reference pickup is the inflection point on segment-2 and segment-3 closes.

14. **No fabricated benchmarks.** "I'm new. I don't have a public benchmark. Two members will take your call this week." Transparency on book size is a power move when the book is small.

15. **"Layer above" framing for Navan and TMC defenders.** "We don't compete with Navan. We operate in the layer above it. Some of our members use Navan for company spend and Latitude for the trips that matter."

16. **"Disqualify up" to Society for HNW.** "$349 is the wrong product for you" flips the dynamic. The HNW buyer becomes a Society prospect rather than an Office buyer at full discount.

17. **Spouse line for bootstrapped founders.** "$189 isn't really for you. It's for Sunday night with [partner]." Convert the membership from a self-expense to a household one.

18. **Multi-EA coordination layer.** "We sell the seam between your two EAs." For principals with overlapping EAs (CEO + COO each with their own).

19. **Family-first reframe for married buyers.** "The person who has to be happy in 6 months is not you, it's [spouse]." The spouse is the underwriter on the renewal decision.

20. **Multilingual backup principal.** Pre-staged for Latam, Iberian, Asian families. The backup principal needs Spanish, Portuguese, or Mandarin coverage for specific Society and Family Office prospects.

21. **"Service contract on a scientific instrument" framing for technical buyers.** Biotech CEOs, engineering founders. "You wouldn't run a $20M instrument without a service contract. Your travel pattern is a similar instrument."

22. **Post-yes onboarding hook.** Book the EA's onboarding call before ending the founder's close call. Locks the seam.

23. **Atlas Family Office variant.** Rolling roster of 5 active from a pool of up to 20. Pre-signed NDA. Same price as Office. The variant exists for buyers whose travel structure is family-office-shaped without Society spend.

24. **The "concierge" word is banned.** Founders never use it; marketers do. If the buyer uses it, the response is "we're not concierge. Concierge implies someone behind a desk in a hotel lobby. We're a small office with named members."

25. **Confessional register, not triumphalist.** "We declined two of every five applications last year. This is intentional." Restraint is a sales asset.

26. **The "everything included" anchor (Model B-specific).** When asked "what does it cost?" the answer is one number. Never let the buyer think there is a second number coming. The escalator was the unanswerable question in Model A; under Model B, there is no escalator.

27. **The 10:47pm anchor.** Extract the buyer's own dollar value from a specific failure. "What did the missed upgrade actually cost you?" They quote their own number; you charge less.

28. **The "personal travel" reframe.** When a buyer balks at $349, walk them through their last anniversary trip and price it at agency rates. The membership pays for itself on one well-planned personal trip.

29. **The EA-as-buyer flip.** Sometimes the EA is the actual decision-maker. Speak to her directly. "This frees you, not him."

30. **The disqualification close.** For true sub-$80K T&E solos, gently steer to a referral and the Journal newsletter. They are not customers. They are future referrers.

### 7.4 Pricing holds and the concession menu

The list price is the price. Three rehearsed price holds, in order of preference:

- **Multi-year lock.** "Sign 24 months at list, locked." Costs the firm zero today and bounds churn risk.
- **Founder rate naming.** "I'll call you a founder rate member" — no dollar concession, all status.
- **Per-head discount at 5+ seats.** Office at 5+ named travelers: $349 holds; at 6+ travelers, $339; at 7+ travelers, $329.

The concession menu, in order of escalation if the buyer pushes hard:

- **Concession 1:** Price-hold on renewal at month 13 (the buyer's locked-in price for Year 2, even if list rises). Costs the firm $300-$600 in foregone price lift at the buyer level. This is the most-given concession.
- **Concession 2:** First month of design at $0 above what membership covers (a Society-tier service for Office price, in month 1 only). Costs the firm 4-8 hours of founder time.
- **Concession 3:** $500 onboarding credit. Used only for HNW buyers where the relationship density justifies the cost.
- **Concession 4:** Dollar discount on price. Used only with principal approval. Logged.

In 30 simulations, concessions 1 and 2 closed 8 buyers; concession 3 closed 2; concession 4 closed 1. The data favors holding price.

| Stage | Persona | FTG | Typical close | Notes |

| Cold inbound discovery | Series A founder, replied to cold email | **FTG1** | Office or Light | The FTG is offered early, before objection arrives |

### 7.6 Force-a-Decision close

The Force-a-Decision close is the answer to the "I want to think about it" purgatory. Script:

> "I appreciate the consideration. Let me ask plainly — is this a yes, a no, or a need-more-information? If it's a yes, I send the agreement now. If it's a no, that's also fine — let me know what I missed. If it's need-more-information, tell me what the information is and I'll have it to you tomorrow."

The buyer who answers "yes" closes that day. The buyer who answers "no" provides the diagnostic. The buyer who answers "need-more-information" gives the specific blocker, which is usually solvable in one email.

### 7.7 The reference bench protocol

By Year 1 month 9, the reference bench has 5 to 7 named members across segments. Each reference has:

- **A pre-consented call window.** "I will refer two prospects to you per quarter, by phone, at your convenience."
- **A 48-hour SLA on prospect outreach.** When a prospect asks for a reference, the desk introduces within 48 hours.
- **A documented compensation.** The reference earns a one-month membership credit per converted referral, capped at six months per year. Documented in writing.

The reference bench is the single highest-conversion sales asset in segments 2 (VC/PE) and 3 (PE operating partners). Cold outreach without reference access in those segments closes at under 5%. Cold outreach with reference access closes at 18-25%.

## 8. Operations

### 8.1 The desk model

Single channel for members: text/SMS to a dedicated line, with email as redundancy. No app to download. No portal to log into for routine requests. The member-portal HTML mockup at `/operations/member-portal-mockup.html` is the exception case — it holds itineraries, standing instructions, and the year-in-review archive, accessible only when the member chooses to look. The 76% buyer preference for messaging over phone (documented in research) drives the channel choice.

The desk operates across three shifts:

- **Shift A.** Toronto morning, 07:00 to 15:00 ET. Founder.
- **Shift B.** Toronto evening, 15:00 to 23:00 ET. Founder.
- **Shift C.** Overnight, 23:00 to 07:00 ET. PHT VA on call (Philippines daytime hours).

A written handoff goes into the operations log at every shift change: members in motion, open requests, pending IROPs, anything the next shift needs to watch. Per `/operations/irop-playbook.md` §7, no verbal handoffs. The founder is woken only for Tier 3 events; the threshold is documented and explicit.

### 8.2 The 60-minute SLA — enforcement and reality

The 60-minute SLA is the product. It is also the single largest operational risk. The v3.0 evaluation flagged that 7am-11pm coverage every day for the founder is not sustainable. The plan handles this with two specifics.

**The SLA flex clause in the MSA.** The 60-minute SLA is the *target*. The contractual language is: "First-response acknowledgement within 60 minutes for urgent matters; same-day resolution for routine matters; hard cap of 90 minutes during published founder coverage windows." This gives the founder operating slack on routine inquiries while holding the promise on the urgent ones.

**The PHT VA hire and cost.** The PHT VA covers 23:00-07:00 ET (the US overnight, which is PHT daytime — 11am to 7pm Manila time). Hiring plan:

- **Month 4-6:** First PHT VA hired at $1,500-$2,000/month, 40 hours/week. Sourced through OnlineJobs.ph or Magic with a 30-day trial. Founder shadows first 100 inquiries; recorded scripts and the IROP playbook serve as training material.
- **Month 9-12:** If member count reaches 15+, second PHT VA hired or first VA scaled to 50-hour weeks at $2,000-$2,500/month. The 56-hour-per-week overnight coverage requirement (8 hours × 7 nights) exceeds a single 40-hour VA; the plan budgets either a 1.4 FTE configuration or 2 part-time VAs from month 10 onward.
- **Year 2:** Two VAs steady, $3,000-$4,500/month combined. Annual cost: $36,000-$54,000.
- **Year 5:** Three VAs at $4,500-$7,000/month combined. Annual cost: $54,000-$84,000.

The VA cost line was missing from the v3.0 plan. It is added to the Year 1 opex projection in §14 ($18,000-$24,000 for months 4-12 of Year 1).

**The backup principal.** Pre-staged at Fora. A senior Fora advisor with overlapping ICP coverage who agrees to cover Latitude's desk during founder vacation (one week in month 6, one week in month 11) and during founder illness. The backup principal is paid an hourly retainer for on-call status ($150/hour committed, capped at 20 hours/year base) plus per-incident hourly billing for actual coverage. Annual cost: $3,000-$5,000.

### 8.3 IROP escalation tree

Documented in `/operations/irop-playbook.md`. Covers flight cancel, missed connection, hotel walk, lost bag, medical, weather, geopolitical, visa refusal. Each scenario has a documented escalation tree, a script, and an expected response time.

The tier model:

- **Tier 1 (Routine).** Contained disruption, obvious workaround. Desk acts independently. One member message at acknowledgement, one at resolution. Logged. No escalation. Example: 90-minute delay with comfortable connection buffer.
- **Tier 2 (Disruptive).** Itinerary broken, member's day or week materially affected. Desk acts; principal notified within 15 minutes by text. Member messages: acknowledgement, options, confirmation, mid-trip check-in, post-trip debrief. Example: flight cancellation, hotel walk.
- **Tier 3 (Critical).** Member at risk — physical safety, legal exposure, reputational consequence. Principal takes the desk personally. Voice contact within 15 minutes. Legal and insurance partners looped where relevant. Example: visa refusal at the gate, medical event abroad, political instability at destination.

The principal is woken only for Tier 3 events. Discipline matters: waking the founder for a Tier 2 erodes capacity to be sharp for a Tier 3 the next day.

### 8.4 Standing instructions data model

Each member has a standing-instruction file in the encrypted vault. The file structure:

- **Personal.** Name, DOB, passport details (image), Global Entry/TSA PreCheck/Clear, secondary ID, emergency contact, blood type, allergies (food and medication), dietary restrictions.
- **Preferences — air.** Preferred carriers in priority order, status by program, seat preference (window/aisle, long-haul-vs-short-haul), preferred airport for departure (e.g., TEB over EWR for NYC departures, JFK over LGA for international), aircraft type aversions (none, no regional jets, etc.), lounge memberships.
- **Preferences — hotel.** Preferred brands in priority order, status by brand, room category default, room location preference (high floor, away from elevator, no view of street), bed type, smoking/non-smoking, in-room amenities (pillow type, mattress firmness), specific properties in known cities ("when in London, the Connaught; when in Paris, the Ritz"), specific properties to avoid.
- **Preferences — ground.** Preferred ground vendors by city, preferred vehicle type, child seats if applicable.
- **Preferences — restaurant.** Allergies (repeated for kitchen), preferred cuisines, restaurants of standing reservation, restaurants to avoid.
- **Preferences — companions.** Spouse name and travel preferences, children's names and ages, EA name and contact, frequent travel companions.
- **Payment.** Stored card, secondary card, points programs and account numbers, expense routing.
- **Standing routes.** Routes the member flies regularly with documented workflow ("YYZ-JFK Monday morning, return Thursday afternoon — book this without asking").

Maintained in Notion (primary) with encrypted backup in 1Password Teams. Member can view at any time via the portal. The desk uses the file to pre-populate every booking. Updates are made by the desk on observation, then confirmed with the member at the quarterly review.

### 8.5 Member lifecycle from intake to off-board

Documented in `/operations/member-lifecycle.md`. The summary structure:

- **Discovery (week -2 to 0).** Intro call, application, fit assessment, invitation. The firm declines roughly two of every five applications. This is intentional.
- **Onboarding (week 1-2).** Welcome packet, travel profile completion, orientation call jointly conducted by principal and desk lead, first booking in week 2 with deliberate over-communication.
- **First 90 days.** Higher touch frequency. Proactive flags. The first IROP — statistically likely in this window — is treated as Tier 2 minimum regardless of severity, with founder notification. The 90-day check-in call (30 minutes, three questions) is scheduled.
- **Months 3-12.** Steady state. Monthly Reports digest. Quarterly trip-pattern review (15 minutes). Annual Year-in-Review in early December.
- **Renewal (60 days pre-anniversary).** The renewal conversation is conducted by the principal personally, 30 to 45 minutes. Honest review of the year, member's plans for the next twelve months, firm's recommendation on tier and renewal.
- **Off-boarding.** When a member leaves, conducted by the principal, 20-30 minutes. Clean export of the member file. The door is left open for 36 months: "If your circumstances change, we pick up where we left off."

## 9. Brand and voice

### 9.1 Visual identity

- **Typeface.** Cormorant Garamond display, Inter body. Restrained editorial typography.
- **Palette.** Navy #0E1A2B, ivory #F4EFE6, brass #B8924C. Restrained. No gradients. No photo overlays on text.
- **Register.** Patek Philippe / Aman / Loro Piana editorial. Not luxury-travel-brochure. Not concierge-app-startup.
- **Photography.** Sparse. Hotel-shot-by-the-hotel; if the desk has not stayed there, no photo. Photos are credit-cited.
- **Layout.** Asymmetric, white-space generous, table-driven where data carries the meaning. The website at `/preface-homepage-v3.html` and the design language at `/design-final-master.html` carry the visual system.

### 9.2 Voice rules

The voice in one sentence: confident, restrained, specific, dry. It does not sell. It does not perform. It does not announce. It states.

If a sentence could appear in a private bank's quarterly letter, it is on-brand. If it could appear in a travel agency's Instagram caption, it is not. Documented in full in `/operations/voice-guide.md`.

**Do.**
- Say **the desk**, not the team, not customer service, not concierge.
- Say **member**, not customer, not client, not guest.
- Say **apply**, not sign up.
- Say **open the desk**, not contact us.
- Say **the Journal**, not the blog.
- Say **brief us**, not request, not submit.
- Lead with the noun, not the adjective. "A property in Marrakesh," not "an exquisite Moroccan retreat."
- Name things. Names build trust. "AC 704" not "the morning flight." "Sarah K." not "your advisor."
- Use periods. Short sentences land harder than long ones with caveats.
- Cite numbers with units. "60 minutes." "$340." "Three days."

**Don't.**
- No adverbs of intensity: truly, genuinely, incredibly, deeply, absolutely.
- No exclamation marks. Ever.
- No emojis. Not in the product. Not internally if it might end up in front of a member.
- No leisure-luxe vocabulary: bespoke, curated, luxe, wanderlust, journey (the noun), experience (the noun), unforgettable, magical, dream.
- No startup vocabulary: excited, thrilled, stoked, vibes, drop, launch.
- No false intimacy: hey, hi there, friend, family.
- No urgency theatre: act now, limited time, don't miss out.
- No apologies for things that are not our fault. State what we did. Move on.

### 9.3 The newsletter content plan

The v3.0 evaluation flagged the twice-weekly Desk Diary + monthly 43rd Parallel as unrealistic for a solo founder also running the desk and a separate primary income. The cadence is corrected here.

**The Desk Diary.** Weekly on Substack and LinkedIn, ~500 words. Trip notes, hotel news, route news, mergers and acquisitions in hotels, a single observation from a recent trip. Cadence: Wednesday morning, every week. Realistic founder time: 2-3 hours per post. Annual time budget: 130-150 hours.

**The 43rd Parallel.** Quarterly, ~2,500 words. Long-form essay on travel as a lived experience for the principal. Identity-coded, not deal-coded. Cadence: end of March, June, September, December. Realistic founder time: 8-12 hours per post. Annual time budget: 32-48 hours.

**The Latitude Journal long-form bench.** Twelve evergreen long-form posts written across Year 1, drawn from the bank documented in `/sales-kit/journal-12-posts.md`. Used as sales-call follow-up assets and as reseed material when cadence slips.

Total Year 1 content time budget: 170-200 hours. This is roughly 8-10% of a working year and is the realistic ceiling for a solo founder also running the desk. The v3.0 plan's 75,000-word annual content goal (twice-weekly + monthly) was unrealistic; this plan corrects to roughly 40,000 words/year, which compounds the brand without breaking the operating model.

**The bank-twelve-first protocol.** Before launch, the founder writes twelve Journal posts and the first six Desk Diary posts. Banked content protects the cadence against the inevitable bad weeks. The v3.0 evaluation's "bank twelve, not six" recommendation is adopted.

### 9.4 Social distribution

- **LinkedIn.** Primary channel. Founder-voice, not brand-voice. Cross-posts the Desk Diary and the 43rd Parallel. Comments from the founder personally. Roughly 8-12 hours/week of LinkedIn time (reading, commenting, replying to DMs).
- **Twitter/X.** Secondary. Posts the Desk Diary excerpt and one observation per week. ~2 hours/week.
- **TikTok and Instagram.** Skipped in Year 1. The buyer is not on TikTok in their professional capacity. Instagram is reserved for Year 3+ when the founder has built a visual archive.
- **Paid social.** Skipped in Year 1. The v3.0 evaluation correctly flagged $6K of LinkedIn ad spend as the worst-yielding dollar in the plan. Redirect to the editor hire (Year 1 month 6) and tooling.

## 11. TICO and tax structure

### 11.1 The TICO three-path framework

Cannot sell travel to Ontario residents without TICO (Travel Industry Council of Ontario) registration. Three paths:

**Path A (Year 1 recommendation).** US-only sales. Toronto network as referral engine to US prospects. No TICO registration required. Sales contracts and Stripe accounts US-routed (Stripe USD account under Latitude 43 Inc.). Ontario-resident prospects are politely declined or referred out. The founder maintains residency in Canada and operates the business from Canada; no US permanent establishment.

**Path A operational mechanics:**
- All marketing materials state "Latitude 43 serves US-based principals."
- All applications have a "US residence" qualifier.
- Stripe payment routing is US-only.
- The Fora host relationship handles the US ARC/IATA/seller-of-travel compliance.
- The founder maintains a written log of any Ontario-resident inquiries declined.

**Path B.** Get TICO registration. Cost: $5,000-$15,000 initial bond plus annual renewal of $2,000-$3,500. Time-to-registration: 4-8 months. Defer to Year 3+ unless meaningful Canadian-resident demand emerges (>10% of inbound).

**Path C.** Partner with a TICO-licensed Canadian agency for Canadian-resident bookings. Revenue-sharing arrangement; the Canadian agency books, Latitude designs. Lower margin, but no bonding requirement. Optional Year 4+ if cross-border demand emerges.

### 11.2 W-8BEN-E and the Fora payment flow

The Fora payment flow runs USD → Latitude 43 Inc. (CCPC). Two tax instruments protect the structure:

**W-8BEN-E filed with Fora.** Establishes Latitude 43 Inc. as a Canadian-resident corporation eligible for US-Canada treaty benefits under Article VII (business profits) and Article XII (commissions). Avoids the 30% US withholding default. Filed at Fora MSA execution, refiled every three years.

**No US permanent establishment.** The plan caps US visits at 120 days per calendar year for the founder. This is well above realistic Year 1-2 travel (estimated 20-40 days/year) but is the threshold that triggers US permanent establishment risk under treaty rules. Society Year-in-Reviews hosted in NYC are counted against the 120-day cap, with a written approval workflow for any planned on-site engagement on US soil.

### 11.3 US permanent establishment risk matrix

| Activity | PE risk | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| Founder visits US <30 days/year | Negligible | Standard treaty residency holds |
| Founder visits US 30-120 days/year | Low | Standard treaty residency; document business purpose for each trip |
| Founder visits US 120-183 days/year | Medium | Risk of "substantial presence" under IRS test; tax counsel review required |
| Founder visits US >183 days/year | High | Triggers US tax residency; avoid |
| Latitude hires US-resident contractor | Low | 1099 contractor structure; no withholding obligation |
| Latitude hires US-resident employee | High | Triggers US payroll, state nexus; avoid in Y1-Y3 |
| Latitude rents US office space | Very high | Creates PE; avoid permanently under Path A |
| Society Year-in-Review hosted in NYC | Low | One-day events not PE-creating; document |

### 11.4 CCPC tax structure

- **Operating entity:** Latitude 43 Inc., Ontario CCPC.
- **Founder receives:** salary plus dividends. Dividends paid as eligible dividends from active business income, taxed favorably under the Canadian dividend tax credit.
- **Small Business Deduction:** Active business income up to $500K CAD/year qualifies for the SBD reducing federal corporate rate from 15% to 9%. Latitude Year 5 revenue is well within this threshold.
- **GST/HST.** Subscription fees from US members are zero-rated for GST purposes (exported services), but the founder must still register if revenue exceeds $30K CAD (~$22K USD), which Year 1 will. Annual filing required.
- **Cross-border tax compliance.** Budget $5K-$8K/year for a tax accountant fluent in both jurisdictions. Toronto-based small-business accountants are usually not. The accountant handles CRA, IRS Form W-8BEN-E filings, transfer pricing if relevant, and the GST/HST filings.
- **Currency risk.** All revenue in USD, all expenses in CAD. A 5% CAD strengthening = 5% revenue hit. Hedging is not realistic at this revenue scale. The plan acknowledges FX exposure explicitly and notes that the corrected base case is run at 1.36 CAD/USD; at 1.30, the CAD-equivalent revenue falls by 4-5%.

Mitigation: Latitude wins on principal-relationship density and on the named-advisor model. Athena's offshored model cannot replicate the "Sarah is on the desk" relationship. Defensive positioning: "They will book your trip. We will redesign it when it breaks."

The EA-as-Service category was missing from the v3.0 plan. It is the single largest blind spot the plan corrected.

### 13.3 The competitive thesis, summarized

Of the named competitors, exactly one (Bell & Bly) has the structural shape — "all design included + relationship density" — that competes directly with Latitude. Bell & Bly lacks the 24/7 SLA in their public materials. Every other named competitor has one or more of: business hours, design fee, no membership, TMC posture, software-not-service.

The "all design included + 24/7 SLA + principal-relationship" stack is structurally rare. The EA-as-Service category is the closest new threat. The competitive moat is not the offering itself (Bell & Bly can copy the offering); it is the principal-relationship density, the operator-honest content, and the 12-15-household Society cap that physically limits the firm's scale and signals seriousness to the buyer.

### 13.4 The Toronto-from-the-US arbitrage

A defensive moat that the v3.0 plan understated. Three structural advantages:

- **Cost-of-living arbitrage.** Founder runs profitably at burn levels (US$120-150K all-in annual personal cost in Toronto) that would be unsurvivable in NYC or SF. A US-based competitor at the same revenue stage carries 1.5-2x personal burn.
- **Tax arbitrage.** Ontario CCPC plus the US-Canada treaty position is structurally cleaner than a US C-corp for a single founder. The Small Business Deduction below $500K CAD of active business income drops corporate rate to 9% federal. The W-8BEN-E filed with Fora avoids 30% withholding.
- **Identity.** "Canadian operator serving US principals" is a positioning advantage. The reference set is Shopify, Wealthsimple, Hootsuite — Canadian companies that built US-serving products with cost and identity advantages. The Latitude operator narrative leans into this rather than hiding it.

## 15. The decision this plan represents

The pivot from v2.0 (Model A, $99/$249 plus design-fee escalator) to v3.1 (Model B Unlimited, $189/$349, all design included) is the most consequential strategic decision in the plan. It was made after 30 sales simulations across three pricing models, three First Trip Guarantee mechanics, and five sales-cycle stages. The data established four findings:

1. **Same buyer, higher tier under Model B.** Vanessa Coyne closed Light $99 under Model A and Office $349 under Model B with no extra friction — a 253% revenue lift on the same person. The buyer had not changed; the offering had.

2. **The escalator was the unanswerable question in cold sales.** No rep could answer "what's the all-in?" with one number under Model A. Every rep could under Model B. Cold-email and cold-call buyers refuse to engage with a pricing structure that has more than one number.

3. **The Virtuoso plus included design plus 24/7 SLA stack alone exceeds the fee.** §2.6 and §4 dollarize this directly. The buyer math runs 4x to 7x value-to-price at the midpoint. This makes "stupid to say no to" a property of the offering, not the sales pitch.

4. **The buyer's EA can sell Model B internally.** No EA in 30 sims successfully sold the Model A escalator internally; multiple EAs in Model B sims explained the membership to the principal in one sentence. The EA Companion Seat compounds this — the EA becomes a defender rather than a gatekeeper.

The cost of the change is acceptance of one structural risk: the marginal labor cost on every "free" design is the founder's hour. The risk is bounded by:

- The 3-active-traveler cap on Office.
- The PHT VA hire at month 4-6, scaling through Year 1.
- The quarterly trip-pattern review with the 2x / 3x labor-negative trigger (§5.6).
- The off-board mechanic for structural mismatches.

This document is the operating bible. Subsequent revisions will track the labor-margin reality at the end of each quarter and revise pricing if and only if labor-negative members exceed 5% of the book at the end of Year 2.

The 30-sim arc, the 5-stage sales cycle, and the FTG matrix together represent the most disciplined sales-design work the founder has done. The product is not yet proven against real buyers. The first real buyer is Year 1 month 1. Everything in this document is a hypothesis that real buyers will test.
