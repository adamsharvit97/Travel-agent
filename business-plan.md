### A travel office for people whose time costs more than their tickets.

**Prepared:** May 2026
**Operating entity:** Latitude 43 (Ontario corporation, dba forthcoming)

## 1. Executive Summary

There is a buyer in the middle of the US small-business economy who has no good option for managing their business travel. They are the founder of a Series A SaaS company, the partner in a $400M PE firm, the principal at a 22-person RIA, the operator of a multi-family office. They spend between $50,000 and $2,000,000 a year on travel and entertainment. They take fifteen to forty trips a year. They do not have an in-house travel manager and they will not get one. They are too large for the consumer tools, too small for the enterprise tools, and too time-poor for the do-it-yourself stack of Google Flights, Hotel Tonight, and a card concierge that never picks up.

This buyer used to be served, badly, by American Express Global Business Travel and CWT. Those two consolidated in September 2025, dropping the floor for accounts under roughly $5M in annual spend. They were briefly courted by Navan, whose stock went public in October 2025 at $25 and has since traded around $13 — a softening that has slowed Navan's enterprise sales motion and made mid-market accounts wonder whether their stack is being abandoned. They have read the Skift coverage from June 2025 on Concur fatigue. They have noticed that NDC content fragmentation has made the corporate booking tool worse, not better, year over year. They are, in short, ready to be sold to.

Latitude 43 is a members' travel office built to capture this buyer. We are not a TMC, a card concierge, or a leisure agency. We sell three things: rewards-program leverage (we book and certify the kinds of stays and fares that earn points and status the principal already cares about), convenience (one number, one inbox, one travel profile), and 24/7 service (60-minute first response, night or day). The product is sold as a membership — **Latitude Atlas** — in three tiers: $3,600/year for one traveler, $12,000/year for an office of five, $36,000/year for a firm of fifteen.

The economics work because of three structural choices. First, the founder operates from Toronto, on Eastern Time, with Canadian cost of living and Canadian healthcare — overhead a Manhattan-based competitor cannot match. Second, the business runs under Fora Travel as host, which provides ARC/IATA accreditation, Sabre GDS access, US payment processing, and a 70/30 commission split that improves to 90/10 at $2M GBV. Third, the product is priced on membership and fees, not commission, which means we are not held hostage to the supplier-by-supplier economics of any single booking.

The total addressable market is real and growing. Global business travel will pass $2 trillion by 2029 (GBTA). SMB business travel is $834B globally with roughly 75% of it unmanaged — a $625B pool of spend with no agency of record. We do not need a fraction of a fraction of that. The Year-5 base case net take-home is $171,370 against a bear of $32,200 and a bull of $1,567,500. The founder is full-time on this from launch, with a polished website, deck, and Journal at day one. This is not a side hustle. It is a deliberate, low-overhead, high-margin practice intended to either compound for a decade or sell into a larger advisory group in year five.

## 2. The Brand: Latitude 43

### 2.1 Origin

Toronto sits at 43.65 degrees north latitude. The name is geographic — a coordinate, not a metaphor. It is the kind of brand name that means nothing until you say it out loud in front of the right person, at which point it means precision, navigation, and a discreet acknowledgment that the office is somewhere specific and not, say, in the cloud.

The choice avoids the two failure modes that dominate travel-brand naming. It is not aspirational-noun ("Voyage," "Journey," "Compass"). It is not founder-vanity ("Smith Travel Group"). It is closer in spirit to the way professional service firms name themselves after streets, latitudes, and dates — Bridgewater, Two Sigma, Sequoia — than to the way travel agencies name themselves after sunsets.

### 2.2 Register and sub-product system

The house brand is **Latitude 43**. The sub-products are named with a single noun that signals function:

| Sub-product | Function | Audience |
|---|---|---|
| **Latitude Atlas** | The membership product — the annual contract | Clients |
| **Latitude Desk** | The 24/7 service line — phone, text, email | Clients |
| **Latitude Reports** | CFO/finance-team dashboards on T&E | Office and Firm tiers |
| **Latitude Journal** | Long-form content, published weekly on LinkedIn | Market |

The naming system is deliberately closed. We do not add a fifth sub-product on a whim. If we add private aviation brokerage in Year 3, it becomes **Latitude Air**, not "Latitude 43 Private Jet Solutions." Every sub-product is one word, every word is a thing the principal already understands.

### 2.3 Voice

Latitude 43 sounds like a chief of staff writing a memo to a principal she respects. Confident, restrained, declarative. No exclamation marks anywhere — not in copy, not in email, not in the auto-responder. No exuberance. Dry humor is permitted and occasionally welcome. We refer to ourselves as "the office" and to the 24/7 line as "the desk." We do not say "our team is excited to" or "we're thrilled to welcome you." We say "the office has confirmed your stay" and "the desk will be reachable through Sunday."

We use full sentences. We use the Oxford comma. We use US spelling when writing for US clients and revert to Canadian spelling only in internal documents. We never end an email with "Cheers." We do not use the word "luxury" in client-facing copy — the price already says it.

### 2.4 What we never do

- No lifestyle photography of beaches, planes, or smiling executives in convertibles.
- No "Top 10 hotels in Tokyo" listicles. Other people do that. We do not.
- No referral codes, no MLM-adjacent affiliate language, no "earn rewards by sharing Latitude with your network."
- No discounting. Ever. The price is the price. If a prospect cannot afford Atlas Principal, they are not the buyer.
- No public client names without explicit written consent.

## 3. The Market Opportunity

### 3.1 The total addressable market

Global business travel, after a long Covid hangover, returned to its pre-2020 trajectory in 2024 and has accelerated since. GBTA's 2025 outlook puts the global figure at $1.57 trillion in 2025, rising to $1.69 trillion in 2026 and crossing $2 trillion by 2029. Growth is approximately 7% CAGR globally and faster than that in North America.

| Year | Global business travel spend | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 2025 | $1.57T | GBTA 2025 Business Travel Outlook |
| 2026 | $1.69T (forecast) | GBTA |
| 2029 | $2.00T+ (forecast) | GBTA |

### 3.2 The SMB wedge

SMBs (under 500 employees) account for 26.1% of global business travel spend — approximately $410B in 2025 — and that segment is growing at a 7.1% CAGR, faster than the market overall. Roughly 75% of SMB business travel is "unmanaged," meaning the company has no agency of record, no negotiated rate program, no TMC contract. The principal books on Expedia or Delta.com and submits a receipt to Concur or Ramp.

This unmanaged SMB pool is approximately **$625 billion in annual spend with no professional intermediary**. Even at a one-tenth-of-one-percent capture rate, that is a $625M services market with very few credible incumbents.

### 3.3 Our wedge inside the wedge

Latitude 43 does not target all unmanaged SMB spend. We target the slice where T&E falls between $50,000 and $2,000,000 annually per company or principal — companies and partnerships that are large enough to feel the pain of unmanaged travel but too small to justify a corporate travel manager (typically a $130K–$180K all-in hire) or a TMC contract (typically $25K–$100K in implementation fees plus per-transaction fees that destroy the economics under ~$3M in spend).

| Segment | T&E range | Approx. US count | Latitude 43 target product |
|---|---|---|---|
| Founders, seed–Series B | $50K–$250K | ~35,000 | Atlas Principal |
| VC partners, sub-$2B AUM | $100K–$500K | ~8,000 | Atlas Principal or Office |
| PE deal teams, mid-market | $250K–$2M | ~3,500 | Atlas Office or Firm |
| RIA principals, $100M–$1B AUM | $75K–$600K | ~12,000 | Atlas Principal or Office |
| Solo consultants, partners | $40K–$200K | ~45,000 | Atlas Principal |

Conservative US addressable count: roughly 100,000 firms or individuals. At Year 5 base case we hold approximately 90 paying accounts. Penetration: less than one-tenth of one percent.

### 3.4 Why now

Four structural shifts make 2026 the right year to launch.

**Amex GBT / CWT consolidation (September 2025).** The combined entity now serves the Fortune 500 and the upper-mid-market. Account managers who used to take a $1.5M T&E client now require a $5M minimum. There is a cohort of accounts being orphaned in real time.

**Concur fatigue.** Skift's June 2025 long-form coverage documented what every CFO already knew: the Concur travel booking tool is worse in 2025 than it was in 2019. NDC fragmentation has hollowed out the content shelf. The CFO's compliance dream is the traveler's daily friction.

**Navan IPO underperformance.** Navan went public in October 2025 at $25 and has traded in the $12–$14 range for most of 2026. The market is signaling that the "automated TMC for SMB" thesis is harder than it looked. Mid-market buyers who were on the fence about Navan are now openly asking what comes next.

**NDC content fragmentation.** Airlines pulled premium content out of the GDS to push direct distribution. The result is that even a competent corporate booking tool now misses ~30% of the bookable premium fare inventory. The only way to actually see what is available is to have a human advisor with multi-channel access. That human is the product.

## 4. Ideal Client Profile

We pursue five primary segments. Each is large enough to anchor the business and narrow enough to write copy for.

### 4.1 Founders, seed through Series B

The typical Latitude 43 founder client runs a 15-to-80-person SaaS, fintech, or vertical software company headquartered in New York, San Francisco, Austin, Miami, or Boston. They have raised between $5M and $40M. The CEO travels for fundraising roadshows (two to four per year, each a one-to-two-week tour of LP and partner meetings), customer-acquisition trips (monthly), board meetings (quarterly), and the conference circuit (SaaStr, Money 20/20, RSA, HLTH, ShopTalk, depending on vertical).

They currently book on Google Flights, Expedia, and Delta.com. They have an Amex Platinum that they use mostly for the lounge access and the Centurion lounge guests. They have tried Navan and either rejected it ("too clunky") or use it only because their COO insists. Their EA, if they have one, handles 60% of bookings on the founder's behalf — usually badly, because the EA is a generalist and the founder is too proud to admit they need help. The founder's actual complaint is not the price of travel; it is that the last three trips had at least one avoidable failure (missed upgrade, wrong hotel category, no record of a status match).

They would pay $3,600/year for Atlas Principal because it removes a recurring source of low-grade humiliation and gives them a person to text from a TSA line. We acquire them through warm introduction from other founders, through the Latitude Journal on LinkedIn, and through a small number of EA-network channels (NYCEA, IAAP).

### 4.2 VC partners, sub-$2B AUM

The VC partner client is a general partner or principal at a sub-$2B-AUM venture firm — exactly the kind of firm too small to have a full-time office manager handling partner travel, but large enough that travel is constant. They take 25 to 40 trips a year: LP meetings, AGMs, portfolio company visits, due diligence trips, and the conference circuit (Upfront Summit, NewCo, AGC Partners, plus vertical conferences in healthcare or fintech).

They currently use a combination of personal travel preferences (a Hilton Diamond who only books Hiltons; a United 1K who only flies United metal) and ad-hoc bookings made by the firm's office manager or by the partner's spouse. They are obsessively loyal to one or two programs and would rather miss a flight than fly on the wrong metal. Their frustration is not booking — it is the inability to recover when something breaks. Cancelled segment in Frankfurt at 11pm local? They are standing in line at the United desk like a tourist.

They would pay $3,600/year for Atlas Principal or $12,000 for Atlas Office (if their two or three partners join) because it gives them a desk to call at 5am ET when they are at the gate in Heathrow. Acquisition is via direct outreach to firms in the $200M–$1.5B band using PitchBook lists, plus referrals from existing founder clients (the founder and the VC partner are often connected on LinkedIn already).

### 4.3 PE deal teams, lower-middle-market

A typical PE client is a fund with $300M–$2B AUM, doing four to eight new platform investments per year, with 10–25 portfolio companies in active management. Travel comes in spikes — a diligence trip is five people in a Tier 2 city for three days, two weeks in a row, then nothing for six weeks. Closing dinners run $40K. Portfolio company visits are scheduled and predictable. Add-on diligence is chaotic.

They currently have a part-time office manager handling travel for the partnership and nothing systematic for the associates and VPs. Junior staff book themselves and expense it. The lack of standardization shows up in two places: the senior partners get unpredictable service, and the firm has no idea what it actually spends on travel year over year. They cannot answer "what is our total airline spend" without three days of analyst work.

They would pay $36,000 for Atlas Firm because (a) Latitude Reports gives them the dashboard they have never had, (b) the desk handles the diligence-trip logistics that currently fall on associates, and (c) a named successor advisor means continuity when the office manager leaves. We acquire them through direct outreach, ACG and SBIA event presence, and through referrals from VC partner clients who know PE counterparts.

### 4.4 Financial advisors and RIAs

The RIA client is a principal at a $100M–$1B AUM independent firm — the segment that has detached from Merrill, Morgan, or UBS in the last decade and now runs as an independent practice with one to four advisors and a small support staff. They travel for client trips (high-net-worth clients increasingly expect to be visited, not the reverse), conferences (Schwab IMPACT in October, Barron's Top 100, Inside ETFs, Future Proof, T3 Tech, the wirehouse-alumni reunions), and prospecting events.

Their pain is conference logistics. Schwab IMPACT is in a different city every year, the hotel block sells out in 90 minutes, and the advisor wants the right hotel near the right venue with the right late-checkout for the closing reception. They currently use a personal travel agent left over from family vacation planning, who books their American Airlines first class but cannot get them into a sold-out IMPACT host hotel.

They would pay $3,600 for Atlas Principal or $12,000 for Atlas Office (the firm pays). Acquisition is via the Latitude Journal on LinkedIn (RIAs are the densest user base on LinkedIn after recruiters), via direct outreach during conference season, and via partnership conversations with custodian platforms (Schwab, Fidelity, Altruist) that may be willing to mention us in their advisor newsletters.

### 4.5 Solo professionals and consultants

The fifth segment is the solo high-end professional — the McKinsey alumna who now runs a $1.5M solo consulting practice, the IP litigator at a boutique firm, the executive coach with twenty clients, the keynote speaker doing 35 events a year. They have personal-grade travel needs at corporate-grade volumes, no support staff, and a six-figure travel spend that they currently manage from their phone in airports.

They would pay $3,600 for Atlas Principal because they value the asymmetry: a small fixed cost that converts a recurring source of stress into a phone number. They acquire faster than any other segment because their decision cycle is one person. Acquisition is the Journal, podcast guesting (one episode per quarter on shows like Modern Wisdom, How I Built This, The Knowledge Project), and direct LinkedIn outreach.

## 5. Positioning and Value Proposition

### 5.1 The three pillars

Latitude 43 sells on three claims. They are mutually reinforcing and we make all three, in this order, in every conversation.

**Pillar one: program leverage.** We book the stays, fares, and segments that earn the points and status the principal already cares about. We know which programs are worth what (Hyatt Globalist over Marriott Ambassador for most travelers; United 1K is harder than Delta DM but worth more in upgrade probability; Hyatt's Privé and Marriott's STARS give us booking codes the principal cannot access directly). We do not push our preferred chain — we push the principal's preferred chain, and we get more out of it than they get on their own. The benefits stack: Virtuoso amenities, Four Seasons Preferred Partner, Mandarin Oriental Fan Club, Belmond Bellini Club, Rosewood Elite, FHR (Fine Hotels + Resorts) cross-booked through the Amex Platinum, Hyatt Privé, Marriott STARS, IHG Luxury & Lifestyle, Hilton Impresario.

**Pillar two: convenience.** One travel profile, kept current. One desk to call. One Slack channel per Atlas Firm. The principal stops being the orchestrator of their own travel and becomes the consumer of it.

**Pillar three: 24/7 service.** A 60-minute first-response SLA. The desk is staffed by the founder during US ET business hours and on-call after midnight ET, with a Philippines-based VA covering the overnight window from Year 2. Backstop is Fora's global advisor network for the rare emergency outside our coverage envelope.

### 5.2 Why we do not pitch price

The principal does not buy this on price. The principal buys this on what owning it signals and what it is like to wear.

The expensive-watch analogy is exact. Nobody pays $5,000 for a Rolex Submariner to tell time. They pay it because of what it signals — they have arrived, they are particular, they appreciate craft — and because of what it is like to wear: the weight of it on the wrist, the feel of the bracelet, the click of the bezel. The watch is a daily, tactile reminder of a decision the wearer made about who they are.

Latitude 43 is the same. The principal who hires us is not buying the lowest-cost travel-booking service in the market. They are buying the experience of being the kind of person who has a travel office. They are buying the moment when they say to a peer at dinner, "Let me text my desk." They are buying the small, recurring relief of not having to remember whether the Hyatt House in Cambridge counts toward Globalist nights. The product is the feeling. The bookings are the byproduct.

This positioning has three operational consequences. First, our copy talks about hours saved and decisions removed, not dollars saved. Second, we never volunteer a price comparison. If the prospect asks "how much do you save me?" we say, "we are not the cheapest way to book a hotel, we are the cleanest way." Third, we never discount. The price is the qualifier.

## 8. Unit Economics

### 8.1 Per-client GBV by archetype

Gross booked value (GBV) is the dollar volume of travel booked through Latitude 43 in a year on behalf of one client. Commission yield is the percentage of GBV that converts to advisor commission before the Fora split.

| Archetype | Typical T&E | Of which we book | Mix | Estimated commission yield (pre-split) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Founder, Series A | $75K | $50K | 70% hotel, 30% air | ~6.5% on hotel, $50/ticket on air |
| VC partner | $250K | $200K | 50% hotel, 40% air, 10% ground | ~7% on hotel, $50-$100/ticket on air |
| PE deal team (5 pax) | $750K | $550K | 60% hotel, 30% air, 10% ground | ~7% on hotel, $50-$100/ticket on air |
| RIA principal | $150K | $110K | 60% hotel (conference-heavy), 40% air | ~8% on hotel (conference blocks), $50/ticket on air |
| Solo consultant | $90K | $60K | 50% hotel, 50% air | ~7% on hotel, $50/ticket on air |

### 8.2 Commission yield by booking type

| Booking type | Yield range | Our blended assumption |
|---|---|---|
| Hotel — preferred partner (Virtuoso, FHR, Privé, STARS) | 10–15% | 11% |
| Hotel — corporate negotiated | 0–8% | 4% |
| Air — domestic | $0 commission, $50 service fee | $50 |
| Air — international consolidator | 3–10% of net | 5% |
| Cruise (non-core) | 10–16% | 12% |
| Private aviation brokerage (Y3+) | 5–10% | 7% |
| Rail (Amtrak, Eurostar) | 0–5% | 2% |
| Ground transport (Blacklane, etc.) | 5–10% | 7% |

### 8.3 The Fora split

Fora's commission split is tiered by year-over-year advisor gross commission:

| Annual advisor commission | Fora split (advisor / Fora) |
|---|---|
| $0 – $300,000 | 70 / 30 |
| $300,000 – $2,000,000 | 80 / 20 |
| $2,000,000+ | 90 / 10 |

This matters more than it looks. At the bear case, the founder is at 70/30 throughout Year 5. At the base case, the founder crosses into 80/20 in Year 3. At the bull case, the founder is at 90/10 by Year 4, which is the single biggest accelerant in the model.

### 8.4 Atlas membership revenue model

Membership revenue is fixed, recurring, and 100% retained — Fora does not split membership fees (only booking commission).

| Tier | Annual fee | Year-5 base case account count | Annual recurring revenue |
|---|---|---|---|
| Atlas Principal | $3,600 | 50 | $180,000 |
| Atlas Office | $12,000 | 25 | $300,000 |
| Atlas Firm | $36,000 | 12 | $432,000 |
| **Total ARR (base case Y5)** | | **87 accounts** | **$912,000** |

### 8.5 Why fees plus membership beats commission-only

A commission-only advisor at our volume earns roughly $300K–$500K gross in Year 5 at the base case, of which Fora takes 20–30%. Yields fluctuate quarter to quarter as supplier mix shifts. There is no predictable cash flow, no contracted revenue, and no defense against a slow quarter.

A membership-plus-commission advisor at the same volume earns the same commission stream **plus** $900K of high-margin, predictable, contracted ARR. The membership revenue funds the 24/7 desk, the technology stack, and the founder's salary in every scenario. Commission becomes upside, not survival.

This is the structural reason Latitude 43 can deliver a service standard solo commission advisors cannot afford to deliver: the membership pays for the desk, not the bookings.

## 12. Regulatory & Cross-Border

### 12.1 TICO — Ontario regulator

Latitude 43 is operated from Toronto. The Travel Industry Council of Ontario (TICO) regulates travel agencies and travel wholesalers selling to Ontario residents. Because Latitude 43 markets exclusively to US residents, the founder is not selling regulated travel services to Ontario consumers and is therefore not required to register with TICO. This is a hard line: no Ontario or other Canadian client may be onboarded without prior consultation with a TICO-experienced lawyer and registration if required.

Operationally this means:

- No Canadian targeting on LinkedIn ads
- No Canadian zip codes in outreach lists
- A short "Service is offered to US residents only" line in the website footer
- If a Canadian inbound lead arrives, we politely decline and refer to a TICO-registered colleague

### 12.2 Fora's US registrations

Fora maintains the following on behalf of its advisors:

| Registration | Issuer | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| IATA | International Air Transport Association | Ticketing authority |
| ARC | Airlines Reporting Corporation | Settlement and reporting for US air |
| CLIA | Cruise Lines International Association | Cruise booking authority |
| Seller of Travel — CA | California AG | Required for US sellers booking to CA residents |
| Seller of Travel — FL | Florida DBPR | Same, FL |
| Seller of Travel — WA | Washington Department of Licensing | Same, WA |
| Seller of Travel — IA | Iowa Secretary of State | Same, IA |
| Seller of Travel — HI | Hawaii DCCA | Same, HI |

The advisor operates under these registrations. We confirm in writing with Fora that our membership product is covered under their registrations; if there is any ambiguity (membership fees are not strictly "travel sales"), we obtain a written legal opinion before billing.

### 12.3 Tax structure

The operating entity is a Canadian-controlled private corporation (CCPC) registered in Ontario. The CCPC invoices US clients in USD via Stripe (membership) and books US travel via Fora's US merchant of record (commission flows from Fora to advisor in USD, deposited to a USD bank account).

Key tax considerations:

- **Canadian corporate tax** — small business deduction applies up to $500K of active business income; combined federal-Ontario rate is ~12.2% on the first $500K, ~26.5% above
- **GST/HST** — services to US clients are zero-rated for GST/HST purposes (export of services); we register voluntarily to recover input tax credits on Canadian-side operations expenses
- **US tax** — no permanent establishment in the US (founder is not physically present in the US for substantial periods, no US employees, no US office); no US federal income tax obligation expected. We obtain an ITIN to enable W-9 reporting if anchor clients request it
- **State nexus** — minimal exposure given no physical or economic nexus in any single US state; revisit if/when we hire US-based associate in Year 4

We retain a Canadian cross-border CPA from month 1. Estimated cost: $4,800/year. Non-negotiable.
