## 1. Executive Summary

There is a buyer in the middle of the US small-business economy who has no good option for managing their business travel. They are the founder of a Series A SaaS company, the partner in a $400M PE firm, the principal at a 22-person RIA, the operator of a multi-family office. They take fifteen to forty trips a year. They are too large for the consumer tools, too small for the enterprise tools, and too time-poor for the do-it-yourself stack of Google Flights, Hotel Tonight, and a card concierge that never picks up. The pain they describe most consistently is not price. It is the support-call black hole: the cancelled segment at 11pm local that takes ninety minutes to acknowledge and three hours to fix, while a board meeting starts in eleven hours.

The market context that makes 2026 a credible window: Amex GBT closed its acquisition of CWT in September 2025, dropping the account floor at the combined entity to roughly $5M in T&E. Navan went public in October 2025 at $25 and trades at roughly $13 today, softening enterprise sales. Skift documented in June 2025 the long degradation of Concur as NDC content fragmentation hollowed out the corporate booking shelf. A cohort of mid-market accounts is openly orphaned. Most are using consumer tools and a card concierge.

Latitude 43 is a members' travel office built to serve that cohort. The product is sold as a membership across three tiers: **Atlas Light** at $129–$149/month for one traveler, **Atlas Office** at $249/month for an office of up to five named (three active concurrent), and **Latitude Society** at $1,000–$2,500/month for an invite-only family-layer relationship that launches in H2 2027 with a hard cap of 12–15 globally. Membership revenue is 100% retained by the advisor. Commission revenue is split with Fora Travel under a tiered structure: 70/30 starting, 80/20 at $300K personal sales, 90/10 at $2M. Hotel commissions yield an effective 7.8% to the advisor after the Virtuoso 2% pre-split clip and the Fora-program mix.

The five-year base case nets approximately **$775K in gross revenue at Year 5** against an opex base (including founder burn at $120K) of approximately $360K, leaving roughly $415K of pretax income to the operating corporation. The Y5 base assumes 60 Light members, 40 Office, and 20 Society — though hitting 20 Society memberships against a 12–15 published cap is the single most aggressive number in the plan and is treated as the upper bound rather than the expectation. A realistic upside ceiling is $1.1M. A bear case lands at $300–350K and a failure case below $100K. Both are credible at probabilities of 25% and 15% respectively.

The economics work because of three structural choices. First, the founder operates from Toronto, on Eastern Time, with Canadian cost of living — overhead a Manhattan-based competitor cannot match for the first three years. Second, the business runs under Fora Travel as host, providing ARC/IATA accreditation, Sabre GDS access, US payment processing, and the supplier consortium memberships (Virtuoso, Four Seasons Preferred Partner, Hyatt Privé, Marriott STARS, Hilton Impresario, IHG Luxury & Lifestyle). Third, the product is priced on membership and fees, not commission, which means the firm is not held hostage to per-booking economics in any single quarter.

The capital plan is honest: this is **founder-funded**, not bootstrap. The founder must personally underwrite $130K–$180K over 24 months — a cash gap that is real and that v1.0 papered over with the word "bootstrap." A friends-and-family note of $150–$250K at flat $1.5M post is acceptable if personal savings are below $150K. No priced equity is anticipated until Y3 metrics give the founder negotiating leverage. Outside venture capital is not the right fit for this business at this stage; the revenue model is service-business margin (founder time), and the exit math does not carry a 10x return for an angel against the realistic outcome distribution.

The realistic 7-year outcome distribution: 40% probability of a $500K–$1M/yr personal-income business; 25% probability of an acqui-hire by Fora, Internova, or a regional TMC at $1M–$5M; 15% probability of a strategic acquisition at $3M–$10M; 5% probability of a sustained $3M+ ARR business with a 4–6x revenue exit; 15% probability of failure to escape Y1.

What follows is the operating plan written against those numbers.

## 2. The Brand

### 2.1 Origin

Toronto sits at 43.65 degrees north latitude. The name is geographic — a coordinate, not a metaphor. It is the kind of brand that means nothing until said out loud in front of the right person, at which point it carries precision, navigation, and an acknowledgment that the office is somewhere specific.

The choice avoids two failure modes in travel naming: aspirational-noun ("Voyage," "Compass," "Journey") and founder-vanity ("Smith Travel Group"). It is closer in spirit to the way professional services firms name themselves — Bridgewater, Two Sigma, Sequoia — than to the way travel agencies name themselves after sunsets.

### 2.2 The naming system

The house brand is **Latitude 43**. Sub-products are named with a single noun that signals function:

| Sub-product | Function | Audience |
|---|---|---|
| **Latitude Atlas** | The membership product — Light and Office tiers | Members |
| **Latitude Society** | The invite-only top tier — family-layer | Members |
| **Latitude Desk** | The 24/7 service line — phone, text, email | Members |
| **Latitude Reports** | Structured trip history, captured rewards, briefs | Office and Society |
| **Latitude Journal** | Long-form content, published weekly on LinkedIn | Market |

The system is closed. New SKUs adopt the pattern (private aviation in Year 4, if it ships, becomes Latitude Air). Every sub-product is one word; every word names a thing the principal already understands.

### 2.3 Voice and register

The voice is **chief of staff writing a memo to a principal she respects**. Confident, restrained, declarative. Confessional over triumphalist. The buyer research is explicit: operator vocabulary, numbers and names and verbs, no adjectives. Light register inside the product ("Hold 4F until 6pm. Driver at 5:30. Marriott Titanium honored."), institutional register on the wrapper.

### 2.4 Banned words

The following words do not appear in client-facing copy, email, web, deck, or product:

> **luxury, bespoke, curated, wanderlust, white-glove, seamless, elevated, concierge.**

The expensive-watch analogy is a private positioning idea, not a public pitch. We never say "you are buying the experience of being the kind of person who has a travel office." The buyer recognizes the register without being told what it signals.

### 2.5 Operator vocabulary

We say:

- **members**, not customers
- **the desk**, not the team
- **apply**, not sign up
- **the office** (Latitude 43 itself, internally)
- **open the desk**, when a member contacts the line
- **the principal**, when referring to the buyer at any tier
- **the receipt** or **the brief**, not the dashboard

### 2.6 What we never do

- No lifestyle photography of beaches, planes, or smiling executives in convertibles.
- No listicles ("Top 10 hotels in Tokyo"). Other people do that.
- No referral codes. No MLM-adjacent affiliate language. No "earn rewards by sharing Latitude."
- No discounting. The price is the price. If a prospect cannot afford the tier, they are not the buyer at that tier.
- No public client names without explicit written consent.
- No exclamation marks. Anywhere.

## 3. Market Opportunity

### 3.1 What the TAM is not

Global business travel will pass $2T by 2029 (GBTA). That number is widely cited, accurately reported, and irrelevant to Latitude 43. The plan must be measured against the slice of spend that can actually be converted into advisor revenue, not the global figure that includes Walmart's $1.4B T&E account and every airline ticket booked on a sole proprietor's debit card.

| Slice | Approximate value | Relevant? |
|---|---:|---|
| Global business travel, 2026 | $1.69T | No |
| North America business travel | ~$455B | Partial |
| US-only | ~$395B | Approaching relevance |
| US large-enterprise managed (>500 employees, contracted TMC) | ~$217B | No — owned by Amex GBT, BCD, CWT |
| US SMB business travel | **~$103B** | Yes — the relevant universe |
| Of which "unmanaged" (no agency of record) | **~$77B** | Yes — outer bound |

v1.0 claimed a "$625B unmanaged SMB pool" by multiplying the global SMB number ($834B per Allied Market Research) by 75% (Phocuswright). The math is correct. The framing is misleading. The US-only equivalent is $77B, and only a fraction of that is hireable.

### 3.2 What the TAM actually is

Of the $77B unmanaged US SMB pool, most of the dollars sit with buyers who will never hire a paid travel advisor: ten-person Etsy sellers, regional contractors, field sales teams using Concur, med-spa owners. The share whose principal would consider hiring a $1,200–$30,000/year membership advisor is, on careful read, **5–10% of the unmanaged dollars** — or **$4–8B of addressable spend**, which converts to roughly 40,000–60,000 firms or individuals at average $100K T&E.

A more useful frame: **the US addressable advisor-revenue pool is $50–80M annually** at industry-standard yield. Latitude 43's Y5 base case ($775K) captures **1.1–1.7%** of that pool. That is a real number and a real market. It is not a $625B opportunity.

|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|

The honest SAM is approximately **9,200 buyable units in the US**. v1.0 claimed 103,500. The audit shows that figure is inflated by an average of 2.4x across segments. The corrected count is still ample: the Y5 base case requires ~120 members against a 9,200-unit SAM, or **1.3% penetration of the realistic SAM**.

### 3.5 Why 2026

Four structural shifts make 2026 the right window.

**Amex GBT / CWT consolidation (September 2025).** The combined entity now serves the Fortune 500 and the upper-mid-market. Sub-$5M accounts are being dropped or routed to self-service platforms. A real cohort is being orphaned.

**Long Lake acquisition of Amex GBT (announced May 2026).** A $6.3B take-private adds organizational distraction to the combined entity. Senior account managers begin leaving. Some will start their own boutiques.

**Concur degradation and NDC fragmentation.** Skift's June 2025 long-form coverage documented what every CFO already knew: corporate booking tools are worse in 2026 than they were in 2019. Premium fare inventory has migrated to direct distribution channels. The principal asking "why can't I see this fare in Concur" is a daily occurrence.

**Navan softening.** Navan went public in October 2025 at $25 and has traded in the $12–$14 range. The market has cooled on the automated TMC-for-SMB thesis. Mid-market buyers who were on the fence about Navan are asking what comes next.

## 4. ICP — Detailed Segments

Five segments. Each large enough to anchor the business and narrow enough to write copy for.

### 4.1 Founders, seed through Series B

Series A or B SaaS, fintech, or vertical software CEOs in NY, SF, Austin, Miami, or Boston. Raised $5M–$40M. 15–80 employees. Personal T&E $75K–$250K. Two to four fundraising roadshows per year, monthly customer trips, quarterly board meetings, the conference circuit (SaaStr, Money 20/20, RSA, HLTH, ShopTalk).

They book on Google Flights, Expedia, Delta.com. They carry the Amex Platinum mostly for the lounge access. They have tried Navan and either rejected it or use it because their COO insists. Their EA, if they have one, handles 60% of bookings — usually badly, because the EA is a generalist.

The actual complaint is not price. It is that the last three trips had at least one avoidable failure: a missed upgrade, the wrong hotel category, a status match that never coded. They would pay $129–$149/month for Atlas Light because it removes a recurring source of low-grade humiliation and gives them a person to text from a TSA line.

**Target product:** Atlas Light. **Acquisition:** warm introduction from other founders, Latitude Journal on LinkedIn, EA-network channels.

### 4.2 VC partners, sub-$2B AUM

General partners and principals at sub-$2B-AUM US venture firms. 25–40 trips/year: LP meetings, AGMs, portfolio company visits, due diligence, the conference circuit (Upfront Summit, NewCo, AGC, vertical events).

They are obsessively loyal to one or two programs (Hilton Diamond, United 1K, Hyatt Globalist). They would rather miss a flight than fly on the wrong metal. Their frustration is not booking — it is the inability to recover when something breaks at 11pm in Frankfurt while standing in line at the United desk like a tourist.

**Target product:** Atlas Light for the partner individually; Atlas Office if two or three partners join. **Acquisition:** direct outreach to sub-$1.5B funds via PitchBook, plus referrals.

### 4.3 PE deal teams, lower middle market

Funds with $300M–$2B AUM. Four to eight new platform investments per year. 10–25 portfolio companies in active management. Travel comes in spikes — five people in a Tier 2 city for three days, two weeks running, then nothing for six weeks. Closing dinners run $40K.

Senior partners get unpredictable service from the firm's part-time office manager; junior staff self-book and expense. The CFO cannot answer "what is our total airline spend" without three days of analyst work.

**Target product:** Atlas Office (3 active travelers cap), or Family Office variant for the senior partner's family-layer needs. **Acquisition:** direct outreach, ACG and SBIA presence, referrals from VC clients.

### 4.4 Financial advisors and RIAs

Principals at $100M–$1B AUM independent firms — the segment that detached from Merrill, Morgan, or UBS in the last decade. They travel for client trips (HNW clients increasingly expect to be visited), conferences (Schwab IMPACT, Barron's Top 100, Future Proof, T3 Tech, Inside ETFs), and prospecting.

Their pain is conference logistics. Schwab IMPACT changes city every year, the host hotel block sells out in 90 minutes, and the advisor wants the right hotel near the right venue with the right late checkout for the closing reception. They currently use a personal travel agent left over from family vacation planning, who books their American first class but cannot get them into a sold-out IMPACT hotel.

**Target product:** Atlas Light or Office. **Acquisition:** Latitude Journal (RIAs are LinkedIn-dense), conference-season outreach, custodian-platform partnerships (Schwab, Fidelity, Altruist).

### 4.5 Solo professionals and partners

McKinsey alumna running a $1.5M solo consulting practice. IP litigator at a boutique. Executive coach with twenty clients. Keynote speaker doing 35 events a year. Personal-grade travel needs at corporate-grade volumes. No support staff. Six-figure travel spend managed from a phone in airports.

They acquire faster than any other segment because the decision cycle is one person. They value asymmetry: a small fixed cost that converts a recurring source of stress into a phone number.

**Target product:** Atlas Light. **Acquisition:** Journal, podcast guesting, direct LinkedIn outreach.

## 5. Positioning & Value Proposition

### 5.1 What we sell against

We do not lead with luxury. We do not lead with rewards-program leverage. We lead with what the buyer actually complains about, which is the support-call black hole, the recurring time tax of disrupted travel, and the absence of a single phone number that picks up at 2am ET.

The buyer-research data is unambiguous:

- **76% of business travelers enjoy travel.** Do not lead with misery.
- **89% experienced disruption in 2025**, averaging **$1,726/trip in unplanned costs and 4h45m of lost time per disrupted trip**.
- **42% of consumers have no opinion of travel agents** — this is a category-naming exercise, not a category-conversion exercise.
- **Identity is the primary objection, not price.** A founder is willing to spend $129/month but does not want to think of themselves as "the kind of person who needs a travel agent."
- **76% prefer messaging over phone.** SMS open rate is 98%.

### 5.2 The three claims we make

In every conversation, in this order:

**One: time and recovery.** When a flight cancels at 11pm, the desk acknowledges within 60 minutes (Atlas) or 15 minutes (Society), names the next step, and commits to a resolution timeline. The principal sleeps. The boardroom meets on time.

**Two: convenience.** One travel profile kept current. One number to call. One Slack channel (Office and Society). The principal stops being the orchestrator of their own travel and becomes the consumer of it.

**Three: program leverage.** We book the stays, fares, and segments that earn the points and status the principal already cares about. We have direct access to Virtuoso amenities, Four Seasons Preferred Partner, Mandarin Oriental Fan Club, Belmond Bellini Club, Rosewood Elite, FHR through the Amex Platinum cross-bookable, Hyatt Privé, Marriott STARS, IHG Luxury & Lifestyle, Hilton Impresario. We push the principal's preferred chain — and get more out of it than they get on their own.

The order matters. Time and recovery is what the buyer actually pays for. Rewards leverage is the rationalization they give their CFO.

### 5.3 Why we do not pitch price

The principal does not buy this on price. We never volunteer a price comparison. If asked "how much do you save me?" the answer is "we are not the cheapest way to book a hotel. We are the cleanest way."

We do not discount. The price is the qualifier.

### 5.4 Why we do not call ourselves a TMC

We are not a TMC. We do not have policy enforcement, OBT integration, or expense automation. We do not compete with Concur or Navan. We complement them. The principal still uses whatever their CFO mandates for compliance; we handle the trips that matter, which is approximately 80% of the principal's personal travel and 0% of the engineering team's.

This positioning is durable specifically because we do not promise what we cannot deliver.

## 6. Service Offering

The product surface area is deliberately narrow. Five named SKUs across three tiers, plus the desk, the reports, and the journal.

### 6.1 Atlas Light — $129–$149/month (one traveler)

Repriced before launch from the v2-facts $99 to a $129–$149 range. $99 fails the credible-floor test: it sits in a gap (above content-only at $33, below credible-human at $200) where the buyer expects content-tier responsiveness but Latitude is promising relationship-tier service. The register-price mismatch produces adverse selection into sub-$5K bookings and scope creep on personal-anniversary requests. The fix is to move into the credible-human band.

| Element | Inclusion |
|---|---|
| Travelers | 1 named principal |
| Air bookings | Unlimited; domestic via Sabre, international via consolidator where commissionable |
| Hotel bookings | Unlimited; preferred-partner programs where eligible |
| Ground transport | Coordinated through Blacklane, Carey, local equivalents |
| 24/7 desk access | 60-minute first-response SLA |
| Travel profile | Loyalty numbers, seat preferences, dietary, document storage |
| Latitude Reports | Per-trip receipts with commission disclosure |
| Annual review | One 30-minute call |
| Design fees | 25% member discount above $30K trip value, 50% below |
| **Fair use** | ≤30 trips/year; beyond this the principal is moved to Office |

**Renewal requirement:** Light members who book under 8 nights through the desk in a 12-month period are moved to per-trip pricing at renewal. The membership is a service contract; it requires the member to use the service.

### 6.2 Atlas Office — $249/month (up to 5 named, 3 active concurrent)

The v2-facts "up to 5 travelers" allowed unbounded labor risk. The cap is now five named seats but only three may book in any rolling 30-day window. This bounds the founder-hours load to roughly Light × 2.5, not Light × 5.

| Element | Inclusion |
|---|---|
| Travelers | 5 named, 3 active concurrent |
| Everything in Light | Yes, for each named traveler |
| EA Companion Seat | Complimentary Light seat for principal's chief of staff, valid for 60 days post-onboarding |
| Latitude Reports | Quarterly written brief, with airline/hotel/ground breakdown and YoY comparison |
| Dedicated point of contact | Founder-led; named peer-coverage backup |
| Slack channel | One private channel for the office |
| Quarterly review | 60-minute call with the principal and the COO/CFO |
| Design fees | Waived on members' trips; commissions still flow |
| Conference logistics | Group hotel blocks for the firm's annual offsite |

**Atlas Family Office** is a variant of Office: 20 named, 5 active rolling, pre-signed NDA, opt-out-of-marketing clause, flat-fee event SKU. Pricing on application.

### 6.3 Latitude Society — invite-only, $1,000–$2,500/month (launch H2 2027)

Launch is **deferred from Q1 2027 to H2 2027**. The cap is **12–15 globally, not 30**. The average ticket is **$20K+ ($1,667/mo equivalent)**, not the v2-facts $14K. These changes acknowledge what every existing analog (Knightsbridge Circle, Sienna Charles, Inspirato Private Travel) confirms: building a 30-member dedicated-principal book is a 7-year exercise, not a 4-year one.

| Element | Inclusion |
|---|---|
| Coverage | Family-layer; up to 4 family travelers |
| Dedicated principal | Founder-led; capped at 12–15 across the firm |
| 15-min SLA | First response, 24/7 |
| Year-in-Review | Quarterly in-person, founder travels to member |
| Personal travel design | Included; no design fees on family leisure |
| Event hosting | Flat-fee SKU for member-hosted dinners, weekends, anniversaries |
| Application | Two named references plus 30-day cooling-off before billing |

**Why deferred.** Society is the highest-margin product and the highest-risk product. The dedicated-principal SLA, the personal-trip design layer, and the 15-min response window cannot be honored solo. The Y2 capacity does not exist; the Y3 capacity exists only if the PHT associate and the Y3 US-based associate are both in place and tested. The 12–15 cap reflects what the founder can credibly serve at this SLA without producing public disappointments that damage the brand.

**Why the smaller cap.** The roster is the moat. A 12-member roster of marquee names compounds harder than a 30-member roster with five wrong members. Curation discipline is ruthless: decline 6 of 10 applicants.

### 6.4 Latitude Desk

The 24/7 service line. Phone, text, email. Not a product line item — a feature of every tier.

**SLA:**

| Tier | First-response SLA | Coverage |
|---|---|---|
| Atlas Light | 60 minutes | 7am–11pm ET founder; 11pm–7am ET PHT associate from month 4–6 |
| Atlas Office | 60 minutes | Same |
| Latitude Society | 15 minutes | Same plus weekend rotation |

Coverage layers in Year 1 (months 1–4): founder primary 7am–11pm ET, founder on-call 11pm–7am ET, Fora global advisor pool as emergency backstop. Coverage from month 4–6 onward: founder 7am–11pm ET primary, PHT associate 11pm–7am ET for triage and acknowledgement (live trip execution remains with founder until a US-based associate is hired in Year 3 or Year 4).

The published SLA is the operating contract. We publish an **incident log** quarterly: every disruption handled, first-response time, resolution time, anonymized but specific. This is white space — no analog in the cohort publishes their incident log. It is the only proof artifact that cannot be faked.

### 6.5 Latitude Reports — resurrected

v2-facts scrapped Reports in favor of per-trip receipts. This plan reverses that decision.

Reports is the **only software-shaped switching cost** in the entire offering. Per-trip receipts are commodity; a CFO-grade quarterly brief generated from Stripe + Sabre + supplier confirmations + Tern CRM creates a non-trivial migration cost. Once a CFO is reading the Latitude quarterly Report at their board meeting, the cost of switching to a competitor includes losing the dashboard, the historical comparisons, the policy-compliance metrics, the captured rewards balance.

**MVP scope (Q4 of Y1):** an 8-page PDF generated quarterly per Office member and monthly per Society member, covering airline/hotel/ground spend, captured commission disclosure, rewards earned, policy compliance flags, YoY comparison, and a one-page "what to renegotiate" note. Built with two consultants on a $25K budget. Ships by end of Y1.

**Why this is a moat.** Switching costs are one of the four classical moats (Porter / Stigler). Trust and brand take 5+ years to compound. Reports compounds in 12 months: the member's own data lives inside our system, formatted in our way, against our taxonomy.

### 6.6 Latitude Journal

LinkedIn newsletter. 52 posts per year, Tuesdays at 7:30am ET. 600–1,200 words. Voice is operator-luxe institutional register; content is operator vocabulary. The Journal does not pitch Latitude 43 in any post; it earns the right to be considered by being worth reading.

**Pre-launch requirement:** 12 posts in the bank before public launch. v1.0 said "first six written and scheduled before launch." The cadence-collapse failure mode in this category is so common that 12 is the credible commitment, not 6.

Twelve seed posts:

1. The Wedge — the underserved $50K–$2M T&E buyer
2. What Hyatt Globalist Is Actually Worth — numerical breakdown vs Marriott Ambassador
3. The Concur Tax — what corporate booking tools cost in traveler time
4. Why Your EA Is Not a Travel Agent — said with respect; the case for separation of concerns
5. The Roadshow, Re-Engineered — a repeatable two-week fundraising itinerary template
6. NDC and the Death of the Corporate Shelf
7. Conference Hotel Math — how to win the Schwab IMPACT block
8. The 24/7 Promise — Audit — we grade ourselves at month 6
9. When to Hire a Travel Office — a decision framework
10. The Status Match Black Book — what can actually be matched, by program, in 2026
11. Five Hotels in New York That Still Get It Right — real review, commission disclosed
12. The Quiet Cost of Bad Travel — an essay on what bad logistics does to a principal's week

| Members | 50% discount below $30K, 25% above $30K |
| Group travel | Quoted on request |

The non-member rate exists to qualify in. A prospect who pays $500 for a hand-built itinerary is meaningfully more likely to convert to Atlas Light at renewal. We expect 30–40% conversion within 12 months.

### 6.8 First Trip Guarantee

Renamed from "First Loss Insurance." "Insurance" is regulated under the Ontario Insurance Act and every US state. Using it in a product name when the product is not an insurance contract creates UDAP exposure, weakens trademark, and risks regulator inquiry.

The structure is unchanged: $1,000 labor cap, one named trip, service credit (not cash), declared on the sales call. Specific failure events listed in the membership agreement. Explicit disclaimer that the guarantee is not insurance and does not replace travel insurance.

1. **Reciprocal peer-coverage agreement.** Signed with at least one other Fora-hosted business-travel advisor (likely a Cadence-style or Brownell-trained peer the founder identifies in the warm network). Each acts as the other's vacation and emergency backup. Written agreement covers: trip continuity, member communication, fee splits during coverage, documentation transfer.
2. **Key-person insurance.** $250K term policy on the founder, payable to Latitude 43 Inc. Funds wind-down or transition in the event of incapacitation. Cost: $400–$600/year at founder's age.
3. **Member protection protocol** in the TOS. If the desk is unreachable for more than 4 consecutive hours during posted coverage hours, the affected member is automatically pro-rated for that period and offered a no-fault exit. This converts founder absence from a silent service failure into a contractual event with a clean financial resolution.

## 12. Regulatory & Cross-Border

### 12.1 TICO Path A — US-only sales, operational discipline

The Travel Industry Council of Ontario regulates travel agencies selling to Ontario residents. Latitude markets exclusively to US residents. The founder is therefore not selling regulated travel services to Ontario consumers and is not required to register with TICO.

The "polite decline" language in v1.0 is correct but is not a control. v2.0 implements hard intake controls:

- **Tern field for country of residence**, required, validated
- **Stripe customer field for country of residence**, required, validated
- **Hard refusal workflow** for Ontario residents with logged decline record
- **Footer notice** on website: "Latitude 43 is a US-market travel office. We do not sell to Ontario residents."
- **No Canadian geo-targeted ads** on LinkedIn or anywhere else
- **Founder personal LinkedIn:** no direct "apply" CTAs; route via website Ontario-decline gate
- **Pre-launch referral relationship** with a TICO-licensed Canadian partner (likely Travel Edge or a boutique TICO holder) so the decline is graceful

The "US resident traveling through Toronto on the day they sign up" edge case is defended by collecting US billing address, US tax residency declaration, and proof-of-residency document at onboarding.

### 12.2 The brother-in-law / family network exception

Travel booked for non-paying friends and family under the founder's personal Fora advisor account, with no consideration, is arguably not "selling travel services" under TICO. The moment consideration flows from an Ontario resident, the transaction is in scope. Documented as gifts, not commercial activity; no commission split from Fora on family-network bookings to Ontario residents.

### 12.3 Fora US registrations

### 12.5 Cross-border permanent establishment — the 120-day rule

The Canada-US tax treaty Article V services-PE test creates US tax exposure if the founder is physically present in the US for substantial periods conducting business. The risk is real and v1.0 understated it.

v2.0 imposes a **120-day US presence cap per calendar year**, with:

- A calendar log of every US day (entry + exit, dated, documented)
- CPA review at month 9 and month 12 of each year
- Written approval workflow for any planned Atlas Office on-site engagement or Society Year-in-Review on US soil
- A cross-border tax memo on file from a Canadian-licensed CPA with US treaty experience

The 120-day number is a conservative interpretation; the actual treaty threshold is 183 days of presence with services rendered, but several US states impose narrower thresholds and the conservative posture leaves margin.

### 13.8 Sales funnel math (corrected)

Cold conversion ladder (per buyer research):

- Reply rate: 1–3%
- Reply → call: 30–50%
- Call → conditional yes: 15–25%
- Conditional yes → close: 40–60%

Midpoint multiplied: 2% × 40% × 20% × 50% = 0.08% end-to-end. **10 cold closes requires ~12,500 sends.** v2-facts said 5,000; that was the optimistic bound.

Warm conversion is dramatically better: 60–80% reply, 50–70% reply-to-call, 30–50% close. Warm is the Y1 channel.

## 15. Capital Plan

### 15.1 What "founder-funded" means

This is not a bootstrap. Bootstrap implies no outside money is needed because the business funds itself from operations. Latitude 43 is **founder-funded**: the founder personally underwrites a real cash gap from personal capital. The distinction matters because it is honest with the founder about what is required and honest with any investor about what has been put in.

|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Founder personal expenses | $80,000 | $100,000 | $120,000 |
| Fora host + Virtuoso allocation | $1,500 | $2,500 | $4,000 |
| Software stack | $4,000 | $7,000 | $11,000 |
| Insurance | $4,400 | $5,500 | $6,600 |

Y1 revenue cash collected is approximately $50–80K (subscription billed monthly, commissions lag 30–90 days). **Y1 cash gap: $80–230K depending on lifestyle and discipline. Y2 cash gap: $40–80K.**

**Cumulative founder personal capital required over 24 months: $130–180K mid-case, $200K+ high-case.**

### 15.2 Financing options

| Option | When appropriate | Terms |
|---|---|---|
| **Personal savings** | If founder has $150K+ liquid | Lowest cost, no dilution |
| **Friends & family note** | If savings < $150K | $150–$250K at flat $1.5M post-money cap, convertible at next priced round; SAFE acceptable |
| **Bank line of credit (secured)** | If founder has home equity in Toronto | Prime + 2–3% draw, secured by HELOC |
| **Priced equity round** | **Not before Y3** | Inappropriate pre-revenue at any cap that compensates for Y5 outcome distribution |

The realistic founder profile here is someone with $80–$150K in personal savings, who needs a $100K F&F note to bridge the gap. The note is structured as convertible (SAFE or note with valuation cap) so it can convert into the eventual Y3+ priced round if one happens, or be repaid out of operating cash if Y3 metrics make a round unnecessary.

### 15.3 Why not priced equity at launch

The realistic 7-year outcome distribution:

- 40% probability: $500K–$1M/yr personal-income business (no exit; founder draws income)
- 25% probability: acqui-hire by Fora, Internova, or a regional TMC at $1M–$5M
- 15% probability: strategic acquisition by a private aviation broker, family office network, or PE rollup at $3M–$10M
- 5% probability: sustained $3M+ ARR independent with a 4–6x revenue exit at $12M–$20M
- 15% probability: business fails to escape Y1

A priced seed round at $3M post would require an exit at $30M+ for a 10x return. The probability of that outcome is ~5%. A $1.5M post requires $15M+, which is at the median of the strategic-acquisition case. Even at $1.5M post, the founder gives up 10–15% of the business for $200K of bridge capital — bad math unless the founder has no other option.

The right structure is **F&F debt-or-SAFE at flat valuation, with intent to repay from operating cash by Y4** if the base case holds.

### 15.5 The investor question this answers

A pre-revenue investor reading this plan will ask: "Why am I funding founder living expenses?" The honest answer: because the alternative is the founder takes a $250K corporate job and the business does not exist. If the investor wants the business to exist, the burn line is real and must be in the model. Pretending otherwise is the v1.0 error.

The investor's secondary question: "What's the path to liquidity?" The honest answer: most likely an acqui-hire by Fora, Internova, or a regional TMC at $1M–$5M in Year 5–7. Less likely but plausible: a strategic acquisition at $3M–$10M. A venture-shaped exit at $12M+ requires conditions that are not currently in the plan (co-founder, tech moat, regulatory monopoly). The plan does not promise what it cannot deliver.
