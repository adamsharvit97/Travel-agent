# 07 — Pricing Viability (v3.0): Defending and Attacking Model B Unlimited

**Evaluator role:** McKinsey-trained operator. Pricing-decision lens.
**Subject:** The v2.0 → v3.0 pivot from $99/$249 + design fee escalator (Model A) to $189/$349 all-design-included (Model B Unlimited).
**Date:** 2026-05-24.
**Grade:** **B+**.

---

## One-paragraph verdict

Model B Unlimited is the right pricing decision for this founder, in this segment, at this stage — but it is the right decision for partially-right reasons, and the plan over-reads the 30-sim evidence to support it. The structural case for Model B is genuinely strong: one number is sellable, the EA can repeat the pitch, the Virtuoso-plus-design-plus-24/7 stack independently exceeds the fee at $349, and simplicity reduces founder cognitive load on every sales call. The labor-margin risk is real but bounded — the 3-active-traveler cap and quarterly review do meaningful work if enforced. Where the plan strains is in claiming the 30 sims "established" the price. They didn't. They established that **$189/$349 closes**, not that **$189/$349 closes better than $229/$399**. The optimum is probably $20–40/month higher on each tier and was never tested. The Model A escalator defenders' case is also stronger than the plan acknowledges (variable-cost protection, CFO-defensibility, category norm). The Model C three-tier case is weaker than it looks (Sim 12 shows the ladder gives buyers negotiation ammunition the founder has to work harder to neutralize). Net: ship at $189/$349 in Y1, but treat the prices as a Y1 floor and rerun a Round 8 sim block at $229/$399 before any 2027 reprice. The repricing protocol, multi-year discount strategy, and Society band placement need engineering the plan does not yet supply.

---

## 1. What the 30-sim evidence actually established (and what it didn't)

The v3.0 plan claims the pivot was "the result of 30 sales simulations across three pricing models, three First Trip Guarantee mechanics, and five sales-cycle stages" (§1, §15). The four findings the plan extracts are reasonable but the inferential leap is wider than the data supports.

### What the sims did establish

**Finding 1: Same buyer, higher tier under Model B.** Vanessa Coyne closed Light $99 under Model A and Office $349 under Model B with no extra friction (plan §15). This is a real signal. It says: when the all-in number is one number, the buyer's upgrade reflex is less constrained by the threshold of the first number. The buyer goes to the tier that fits the product, not the tier that fits the entry price. **This is the single most defensible finding in the dataset.**

**Finding 2: The escalator was unanswerable.** Across 30 sims, no rep could quote a clean all-in under Model A. Every rep could under Model B. The friction wasn't price level; it was price ambiguity. The buyer's question — "what does this actually cost me per year?" — has a one-sentence answer under Model B and a five-sentence answer under Model A. Sales velocity is a real product feature when the founder is the sales rep, and Model B's velocity advantage compounds across every cold call.

**Finding 3: The buyer's EA can sell Model B internally.** This is the most undervalued finding in §15. The decision-maker is the principal; the influencer-blocker is the EA. The EA can repeat *"$349 a month, everything included, 60-minute desk on text 24/7"* to the principal in one sentence. The EA cannot repeat the Model A escalator without notes. In a market where the EA is the gatekeeper for 60–80% of qualified prospects, EA-repeatability is a category advantage, not a nice-to-have.

**Finding 4: The Virtuoso-plus-design-plus-24/7 stack exceeds the fee.** Plan §4 dollarizes this at 4× to 11× value-to-price for Office, 2× to 4× for Light. The math is approximately right and is reproduced below in §3. The honest reframing: "stupid to say no to" is a property of the offering, not the pitch. This finding does not require sim data — it is arithmetic — but the sims confirmed buyers respond to it.

### What the sims did not establish

**They did not establish that $189 is optimal versus $229 or $249.** The sims tested $99 vs $189 vs $249 (across Models A, B, C). They did not test $189 vs $229. The plan's confidence that $189 holds is well-founded; the plan's confidence that $189 is the right price is not.

**They did not establish that Office at $349 is optimal versus $399 or $449.** Same gap. The Office price was chosen to maintain a clean $189/$349 step (32% premium over Light, clean ratio) and to anchor below the $400 SMB monthly threshold where CFO sign-off becomes a real procurement event. Both reasons are defensible. Neither reason is the same as "this was the best closing price."

**They did not establish that Model B beats Model C on revenue per qualified buyer.** Sim 12 — the Tomasz Werner three-tier negotiation — closed Plus at $249 list with no discount. That's a Model C win, not a Model B win. The sim's own evaluator concludes Model B is the cleaner offer "for this buyer profile" but allows Model C is "viable" with a more disciplined rep. The plan should be honest that Model C did not lose the sim block; it tied with Model B on revenue and lost on rep-load. Those are different findings.

**They did not establish retention.** Thirty sims are first-touch sales conversations. The labor-margin risk on Model B Unlimited manifests in months 6–18, not on the sales call. The sims tell you nothing about whether a high-usage Office member generates positive contribution margin in Y2. That has to be modeled separately, and the plan's §5.3 5% labor-negative assumption is asserted, not earned by the sim data.

**They did not establish CAC by tier.** Model B's higher entry price ($189 vs $99) should logically depress cold close rate at the top of the funnel — the $99 anchor is gone. The plan does not show whether the close rate on cold reply was higher, lower, or equal under Model B. Without that, the plan cannot say whether Model B *trades* a lower close rate for a higher ACV (defensible) or *improves* close rate and ACV simultaneously (the implicit claim).

### Inferential weight of 30 sims

Thirty is enough for a directional read on what a sales conversation looks like. It is not enough to underwrite a permanent strategic decision. A Round 8 block testing $229 Light + $399 Office vs $189/$349 against the same 5 sales-cycle stages × 3 FTG mechanics is the right next experiment. If $229 close rates within 10 percentage points of $189, the plan leaves $28,800 of Y5 revenue on the table by not running it.

## 2. Stress-test Light: $189 vs $169 vs $229 vs $249

The Light tier is the friction-heavy decision. It is the entry product, the first-time-buyer SKU, and the price point most exposed to price sensitivity.

### The four prices, side-by-side

| Light price | Monthly | Annual | Position | Cleared SMB threshold? | Read |
|---|---:|---:|---|---|---|
| $169 | $169 | $2,028 | Below One Medical → MDVIP gap | Yes ($200 instinct) | Reads "discounted from $199" |
| $189 (v3.0) | $189 | $2,268 | Clean under $200 | Yes (with margin) | Reads "considered" |
| $229 | $229 | $2,748 | Indagare Custom-adjacent | No — partner consult zone | Reads "premium-entry" |
| $249 | $249 | $2,988 | At Velocity Black floor | No — definite procurement | Reads "Office-tier first ask" |

### $169 — defending the lower price

The case for $169 is that it reduces the close-rate drag on the cold funnel and increases first-call yield. At a 1–3% reply rate on cold outreach, every percentage point of close-rate improvement is worth roughly 5 members per 5,000 outreaches. At $20/month × 60 Y5 members × 12 = $14,400 of foregone subscription, you'd need 6 incremental members across 5 years for $169 to outperform $189. Plausible in theory; the buyer profile in §3.1–3.5 is not a $20-sensitivity profile in practice.

**The argument against $169:** at $169, the brand register (navy, brass, Cormorant Garamond, "operator-luxe") is mispriced to its visual identity. The pricing-stress-test research established that under-pricing relative to the register triggers the "no free lunch" instinct in the buyer the brand is courting. $169 reads as a discount on $189, which is itself the implicit positioning of $99 Model A. The founder spent v2.0 → v3.0 walking away from the $99 anchor; reintroducing it at $169 partially undoes the work.

**Verdict on $169:** Worse than $189. Don't.

### $189 — the v3.0 position

$189 sits cleanly below the $200 SMB instinct threshold with $11 of margin. The annual is $2,268, which is 0.4–1.5% of the buyer's $80K–$300K T&E — a rounding error to the buyer, a meaningful subscription line to the founder. The price ladders to Office at $349 with a 85% step ($160 incremental) — large enough to feel like a different product, small enough that EA-driven upgrades happen.

**The argument against $189:** it may still be a touch too soft. The original stress-test argued the "credible human floor" runs $200–$400/mo (pricing-stress-test §1.4). $189 sits below that floor by $11. The buyer at $80K T&E who pays $189 is in the same monthly band as their Equinox membership; the brand register argues for higher.

**Verdict on $189:** Defensible at Y1 launch. Likely the floor, not the optimum.

### $229 — the steelman case for higher

$229 lifts Light above the $200 line into the partner-consult zone. This is normally bad for cold sales, but for the Latitude buyer profile, it might be neutral or positive: the v3.0 ICP segments (Series A founder, RIA principal, PE associate, surgeon) are not subject to a household partner consult. They are subject to a self-consult that says "is this worth it for me?" — and at $229, the answer is the same as at $189 if the buyer math runs.

$229 generates $40/month × 60 Y5 members = $28,800/year of incremental revenue against a possibly-trivial close-rate impact. If close rate stays flat, this is pure margin. If close rate drops 10%, the founder loses 6 members but gains $28,800 from the rest — a net of roughly +$15K Y5 revenue. **The math favors $229 unless close rate drops more than 20%.**

**Verdict on $229:** This is the optimum the plan never tested. Round 8 sims should price here.

### $249 — the aggressive case

$249 matches the original Atlas Office price under v2.0 and pushes Light into Velocity Black territory. The case for $249 is brand integrity: the $200–$400 band is where the visual register actually reads. The case against is that it removes the ladder — at $249/$349 the Office step is only $100 ($249 → $349 is 40%, much weaker upgrade math than $189 → $349 at 85%).

**Verdict on $249:** Wrong, but not by much. The upgrade-step issue is the deciding factor. If you reprice Light to $249, you reprice Office to $449 to maintain the step.

### The Light optimum

The plan's $189 is defensible at launch. The probable optimum is **$219–$229** based on the brand-register argument and the Y5 revenue math. **Recommendation: launch at $189, retest at $219 in Round 8 sims in Q4 2026, and reprice to $219 at the Y2 anniversary if Round 8 shows close rates within 15% of $189.**

## 3. Stress-test Office: $349 vs $299 vs $399 vs $449

Office is the higher-stakes pricing decision because it's the contribution-margin-leading SKU at Y5 (40 Office members × $4,188 = $167K of $592K Y5 subscription, with much higher per-member commission lift).

### The four prices, side-by-side

| Office price | Annual | Position | Step from Light | Read |
|---|---:|---|---:|---|
| $299 | $3,588 | Indagare Custom adjacent ($2,850/yr) | 58% step | Reads as "Light Plus" |
| $349 (v3.0) | $4,188 | Velocity Black + 35% | 85% step | Reads "different product" |
| $399 | $4,788 | Soho House CWH + 11% | 111% step | Reads "small business tool" |
| $449 | $5,388 | Soho House Every House zone | 138% step | Reads "office line item" |

### $299 — the soft case

$299 maintains a cleaner Light:Office ratio and reduces the procurement-event friction. It sits at the Indagare Custom price ($238/mo published, +$60 for the Latitude upgrade) and is the price most directly comparable to the strongest direct competitor.

**The argument against $299:** the Office buyer is structurally less price-sensitive than the Light buyer. The Office tier is purchased by founders with EAs, PE operating partners with assistants, RIA principals with co-partners. None of these buyers has the $200 instinct constraint. Pricing Office at $299 leaves $50/month × 40 Y5 members × 12 = $24,000/year of revenue on the table for negligible close-rate benefit.

**Verdict on $299:** Wrong. The Office buyer doesn't reward a discount on this product.

### $349 — the v3.0 position

$349 sits at the upper end of the named-relationship band ($200–$400 per pricing-stress-test §1.4) without breaching the procurement threshold ($500/month corporate-card sign-off). The 85% step from Light is large enough to make the upgrade conversation a real conversation, small enough that EA-driven upgrades close. The annual is $4,188, which is 0.8–2.1% of the buyer's $200K–$500K T&E.

**The argument against $349:** the buyer math says the realized value is $16K–$46K (plan §4). At a 4×–11× value-to-price ratio, the price has room to lift before the ratio collapses to the 2×–3× band where the value pitch stops landing. $349 may be 15–25% under-priced versus what the buyer would still close at.

**Verdict on $349:** Defensible. Probably the Y1 floor, not the optimum.

### $399 — the corporate-card case

$399 is the right answer if you believe the Office tier should be purchased on the company card, not the personal card. At $399/month the price is unambiguously a business expense; the EA codes it under "office services," not "founder lifestyle." This matters for retention — once the price moves to the company P&L, the renewal decision shifts from a personal-discretion call to an annual line-item review.

At $399 the annual is $4,788 (vs $4,188 at $349). The incremental $600/year per member × 40 Y5 members = $24,000/year. The realized value ratio drops from 4×–11× to 3.4×–9.6× — still well above any reasonable threshold for "stupid to say no to."

**The argument against $399:** breaches the $400 monthly threshold that the brief implicitly anchors against ("just under $400"). At $399 a CFO is more likely to be in the conversation, which slows the cycle and weakens the EA-can-sell-it-internally finding.

**Verdict on $399:** This is the probable Y2 or Y3 reprice target. Don't launch here.

### $449 — the aspirational case

$449 prices Office at the Soho House Every House register. The case is brand integrity: at $449 the product is unambiguously priced like a relationship, not a tool. The case against is that the close rate likely drops materially — $449 triggers a different procurement category, the EA cannot defend the price internally without a quarter of usage data, and the cold funnel close rate probably degrades 25–35%.

**Verdict on $449:** Wrong for Y1. Possible Y4+ if the brand earns it.

### The Office optimum

The plan's $349 is defensible. The probable optimum is **$379–$399**. The arithmetic favors $399 if close rate degrades less than 18%. **Recommendation: launch at $349, run Round 8 sims at $399 in Q4 2026, and reprice to $399 at the Y2 anniversary if Round 8 closes within 20% of $349 and the labor-margin reality on existing Office members holds positive.**

## 4. The labor-margin risk: fundamental flaw or bounded risk?

This is the central attack on Model B Unlimited. Under Model A, every "free" design hour the founder gave away was capped by the design fee structure — heavy-usage members paid more, light-usage members paid less. Under Model B, the marginal cost of every design hour is the founder's hour, and the price doesn't move.

### The exposure math

Atlas Office at $349/mo × 3 active travelers cap × ~18 trips/year/traveler theoretical max = 54 trips/year. At 2 hours per trip (intake + book + reconfirm + follow-up, before IROP work), that's 108 founder-hours per Office member per year. At the founder's blended billable rate of $200/hour (the floor for a credible solo practice per the pricing-stress-test), the labor cost on a heavy Office member is $21,600/year against $4,188 of subscription. The commission and personal-travel revenue brings the member to roughly $8,800 of total Y1 revenue — still labor-negative by $12,800.

This is the worst-case bound. It is not the expected case. Two things bound the realistic exposure:

**The 3-active cap.** The plan caps concurrent active travelers at 3. In practice, of an Office member's 5 named roster, 1–2 travel heavily, 2–3 travel rarely. The realistic load per Office member is closer to 25–35 trips/year total, not 54. At 30 trips × 2 hours = 60 founder-hours/year × $200/hour = $12,000 of labor against $8,800 of revenue — still labor-negative on the heavy members.

**The PHT VA at month 4–6.** The VA absorbs the routine portion of trip work. If the VA handles 60% of trip execution at $25/hour loaded (Manila PHT rates, ~$1,500/month for 20 hours/week), the founder's effective labor on a 30-trip-per-year Office member drops to 24 hours × $200 + 36 hours × $25 = $4,800 + $900 = $5,700 of total labor. Against $8,800 of revenue, that's $3,100 of contribution margin. Positive but thin.

### The 5% bear assumption in §5.3

Plan §5.3 asserts that 5% of members go labor-negative, generating $15–25K of impact at Y5. This is plausibly low for Year 1–2 and plausibly correct for Y3+. In Year 1, the founder doesn't know the usage distribution yet. The plan should publish a 15% bear case for Y1–Y2 with $30–50K impact, declining to 5% by Y4 as the off-board protocol matures.

### Fundamental flaw or bounded risk?

**Bounded, conditional on three operational mechanics being real:**

1. **The 3-active cap must be in the MSA, not in the founder's head.** v3 evaluation Lens 1 already flagged this. The cap is doing structural work. If it doesn't appear in the welcome email, the MSA, and the quarterly review template, it is aspirational.

2. **The quarterly trip-pattern review must trigger off-board conversations.** Plan §8 doesn't specify the trigger threshold. The mechanic in v3 evaluation Lens 8 (3.0× implied hours = trigger event) is reasonable. Without a documented threshold, the founder will discover labor-negative members in Q4 and have no playbook to address them.

3. **The PHT VA must hire on time.** If the VA hire slips from month 4–6 to month 9–12 (plausible — finding a Sabre-trained, US-business-hours-capable VA who can speak operator-luxe register is hard), the founder absorbs the entire labor load for 9 months. At 8–15 Y1 members that's survivable; at 30 it isn't.

The labor risk is real, bounded, and conditional. It is not a fundamental flaw. It is a fundamental operational risk that needs three pieces of infrastructure the plan partially documents.

## 5. The Model A escalator defenders' case

The plan is dismissive of Model A in §15. The defense exists and deserves a fairer hearing.

### Variable-cost protection

Model A's design fee escalator is a variable-cost hedge against heavy-usage members. The heavy traveler pays more, the light traveler pays less. Under Model B, the founder absorbs the cost of variability entirely. This is structurally analogous to the choice between an HMO (capitated) and a PPO (fee-for-service). HMOs win on simplicity and predictability for the buyer; PPOs win on labor protection for the provider. Both are viable; the choice depends on which side you're protecting.

For a solo founder with bus factor 1.0, the PPO argument is structurally stronger than the plan acknowledges. The founder is one twisted ankle from a margin disaster under Model B. Under Model A, every additional hour of design work generated a corresponding fee.

### CFO-defensibility

Where the buyer is a corporate-card holder with a CFO in the room, the Model A escalator was structurally easier to defend. The CFO reads "subscription + variable design fee per trip" as a known SaaS pattern (à la Salesforce + implementation services, or HubSpot + onboarding fees). The Model B Unlimited reads as a flat retainer, which some procurement teams resist because it's not benchmarkable. In the buyer profiles in §3, only the family-office and RIA segments have meaningful CFO involvement; for them, Model A may close better than the sims indicated.

### Category norm

The travel-advisory category norm is fees + commissions. SmartFlyer charges $500 floor + escalator. Bell & Bly charges $800/week. Indagare Custom charges $2,850/year + escalator on complex trips. Latitude under Model B is making a category-deviating bet. Category deviation is sometimes the winning move (Tesla on dealerships, Ramp on no-annual-fee) and sometimes the loser (every flat-rate health insurance startup of the 2010s). The plan should be honest that Model B is a category bet, not a category norm.

### Why the case still loses

The defenders' case is real but it loses to two facts:

**(a)** The buyer doesn't care about the founder's variable-cost protection. The buyer pays for predictability. Every dollar of variable-cost protection the founder banks under Model A is a dollar of predictability the buyer loses. In a market where Concur and Navan are the alternative, predictability is the structural advantage.

**(b)** The sim data, however limited, ran a controlled test of the two models. Vanessa Coyne closing Light $99 under A and Office $349 under B is the cleanest single data point: same person, higher tier, more revenue. The Model A defenders need to explain that data point. They can't.

Model A loses on the buyer-side argument even if it wins on the founder-side argument. The plan should have made this case more explicitly so the rejection is understood as a real trade-off, not an obvious win.

## 6. The Model C three-tier defenders' case

Model C (Basic $99 / Plus $249 / Office $499) was tested in Sim 12 and closed at list. The plan rejects it; the rejection is partially right.

### What Model C does well

**A ladder gives buyer agency.** Tomasz Werner in Sim 12 wanted a way to anchor downward without leaving the conversation. The $99 Basic tier gave him that anchor. He could push for "Plus at Basic pricing" precisely because Basic existed. In Model B, that conversation has nowhere to go — the buyer either takes $189 or walks. Buyer agency is a sales feature, even when the buyer ends up at the same price.

**The $99 Basic tier captures the freemium-adjacent buyer.** A founder doing 4–6 trips/year at $80K T&E is wrong for Light Unlimited but right for a booking-desk tier. Under Model B, that buyer is turned away (or referred to the non-member design schedule). Under Model C, they convert into a low-margin but real recurring relationship that may upgrade in Year 2–3.

**The middle tier is feature-defensible.** Sim 12's founder closed Plus by reframing the $150 Basic-Plus gap as "this is what design inclusion costs to deliver." That's the cleanest possible price defense. Model B Unlimited can't deploy this argument because there's no Basic to compare against.

### What Model C does badly

**The middle tier is feature-defensible but rep-fragile.** The Sim 12 evaluator notes the founder almost lost the deal twice and recovered both times by elite-level argument. Model C demands a more disciplined rep than Model B. For a solo founder doing every sales call themselves, this is acceptable. The moment the founder hires a salesperson (Y3+?), Model C scales worse than Model B.

**The $99 anchor is a long-term liability.** Every Plus prospect can point to $99 as a starting reference. Sim 12 shows the founder absorbing the pressure successfully, but across 100 sales calls, the $99 anchor wins some of them on price and loses others on the "Plus is the right product, you're just shopping" argument. Model B removes that anchor entirely.

**Three SLAs, three FTGs, three sales pitches.** Operating three tiers simultaneously is 50% more sales-script complexity for the founder. For a solo founder running primary income, this is not free.

### The verdict on Model C

Model C is the right pricing model for a multi-rep firm with a freemium-adjacent SMB segment. It is the wrong pricing model for a solo founder selling principal-grade service. The plan rejects it correctly, but the rejection should acknowledge that Model C scored within 10% of Model B on the sim outcomes — not below it.

## 7. Why simplicity wins for the buyer AND the seller

The strongest single argument for Model B is the one the plan understates: simplicity compounds.

**For the buyer:**
- The pitch fits in one tweet.
- The EA repeats it accurately to the principal.
- The CFO line-items it once and forgets it.
- There is no surprise invoice in month 4.
- The renewal decision is a one-bit decision (yes or no), not a five-bit decision (yes, yes but reprice, yes but tier-change, etc.).

**For the seller:**
- The discovery call doesn't include a fees walkthrough.
- The proposal is a paragraph, not a PDF.
- The contract is a Stripe link.
- There is no monthly trip-fee invoicing burden.
- The price disclosure is the same every time.

The compounding effect is real and unmeasured. The founder's cognitive load on every sales call is lower under Model B than Model A. Across 200 sales calls in Y1, that's tens of hours of saved founder cognition. The plan should claim this benefit explicitly. It is the second-strongest argument for Model B after the buyer math.

## 8. The Virtuoso + design + 24/7 stack as moat

Plan §4 dollarizes the stack. The arithmetic holds:

| Component | Annual realized value, Office |
|---|---:|
| Virtuoso/FSPP/STARS benefits on 60 hotel nights (3 travelers × 20) | $3,000–$8,000 |
| Trip design at $300–500/trip × 22 trips | $6,600–$11,000 |
| 24/7 SLA value (the one trip that matters) | Priceless |
| EA Companion Seat (2–4 hours/week of EA labor) | $5,000–$12,000 |
| Personal travel design (anniversary, family ski) | $3,000–$15,000 |
| **Total** | **$16,000–$46,000** |

Annual fee: $4,188. Value-to-price ratio: 3.8× to 11×.

This is the moat. It is structurally hard to replicate without (a) the Virtuoso seat, which Fora provides, (b) the 24/7 SLA, which requires either a solo founder with a backup or a real team, and (c) the included design, which requires the founder to absorb labor variability. Bell & Bly has design but not 24/7. Amex GBT has 24/7 but not Virtuoso-grade benefits. Indagare has both but at $238/mo Custom + escalator, breaking the simplicity. Cadence has none of the three. Brownell has design and Virtuoso but is Mon-Fri.

**The moat is the stack, not any individual layer.** A competitor can match any single layer; matching all three plus the simplicity register is structurally rare. This is the central defensibility argument and Model B Unlimited is the pricing architecture that operationalizes it. Model A could not — the design fee broke the simplicity. Model C could not — the ladder broke the value clarity.

This is the strongest pricing-decision argument and the one the plan should lead with.

## 9. Repricing protocols: when does Office go to $399?

The plan does not specify repricing triggers. This is a real gap. Three triggers should be documented:

**Trigger 1: Round 8 sim data.** Run a sim block at $229 Light + $399 Office in Q4 2026. If close rates within 15% of v3.0 prices, reprice at Y2 anniversary (Q4 2027).

**Trigger 2: Labor-margin reality.** End-of-quarter review of Office members: if cumulative labor on the top quartile of usage exceeds 2.5× the implied hours in the unit economics, reprice Office by $50 at next renewal cohort.

**Trigger 3: Virtuoso 2% clip change.** Per Risk #9, modeled at 2% with tolerance to 4%. If Virtuoso lifts the clip to 3% or higher, the effective commission rate drops from 7.8% to ~6.5%, costing roughly $45–60K of Y5 commission revenue. The right response is a price lift, not a margin absorption: lift Light by $20 ($209) and Office by $30 ($379) to restore the unit economics.

**Trigger 4: Bell & Bly capital event.** If Bell & Bly raises and prices up, follow them up within 12 months. If they price down, hold.

**Trigger 5: Y5 labor saturation.** If founder hours/week consistently exceed 60 by Y3, the choice is hire faster (which raises opex) or reprice up (which raises margin per member). The default should be reprice up.

**Format:** these triggers should live in a one-page repricing protocol document, refreshed annually at the Y-end review. Without it, the founder will face the reprice decision every year and re-litigate it from scratch.

## 10. Multi-year discount strategy

The plan does not address multi-year prepay. This is a small but real gap.

### The arithmetic

**Annual prepay:** offer at 10% discount. $189/mo × 12 = $2,268 list; $2,041 prepay. The discount costs $227/member; the benefit is (a) cash flow advance, (b) churn reduction (annual prepays churn at ~95% renewal vs. monthly at ~85–88%), (c) reduced Stripe processing fees by ~80%.

**Two-year prepay:** offer at 15% discount. $189/mo × 24 = $4,536 list; $3,855 prepay. The discount costs $681/member; the benefit is (a) larger cash advance, (b) the Y2 churn risk is converted to a refund-management risk, which is bounded.

### The strategy

For Y1: offer annual prepay at 10% discount on Light only (not Office). Office buyers are already paying $4,188 — pushing them to $4,500+ upfront introduces friction at the point of sale. Light's $2,041 annual prepay is small enough to clear a personal card without friction.

For Y2+: offer two-year prepay at 15% on Light, annual at 10% on Office.

**Verdict:** A 10% annual prepay discount on Light is a high-ROI move. The plan should add it.

## 11. The non-member design fee schedule: revenue line or vestigial mechanism?

Plan §2.4 lists the non-member design fee schedule:
- $500 floor
- $250 base + $50/$1K from $5K–$15K
- $750 base + $75/$1K from $15K–$30K
- $1,875 base + $100/$1K above $30K
- Capped at $10,000/trip
- Paid in full before design begins

The plan calls this "a funnel, not a margin product" and asserts a 30–40% conversion to Atlas Light within 12 months.

### Honest assessment

It is largely a qualifying mechanism. The 30–40% conversion claim is asserted, not engineered (v3 evaluation Lens 1 already flagged this). Without an explicit conversion mechanic — a 30-day post-trip nudge, a "your design fee credits toward Light if you join" mechanic, a quarterly newsletter-driven offer — the conversion will run at 10–15%, not 30–40%.

### What it should be

The schedule should be repositioned as a "front door for non-members," not as a revenue line. The mechanic should include:

1. **Design fee credit toward membership.** If the non-member converts to Light within 60 days of their first trip, 50% of the design fee credits toward the first 6 months of membership. This drops the membership decision from $189/mo to roughly $145/mo for the first half-year — a meaningful upsell hook.

2. **A 14-day rush surcharge.** Industry standard at 50% (Bell & Bly). Without it, the schedule rewards bad planning at founder expense.

3. **A higher floor at $750.** $500 at a $5K trip is roughly $125/hour at 4 hours of work — below the labor floor. $750 minimum at $5K, scaling per the existing schedule, lands at $187/hour — at the labor floor.

4. **The cap is fine at $10K.** Above that, the buyer should be in Society discussion, not à la carte.

The non-member schedule is partially vestigial as written. Modest engineering converts it into a real funnel mechanic.

## 12. Society repricing: $1,000–$2,500 — what determines placement?

The v3.0 plan keeps the Society band at $1,000–$2,500/month with an average of ~$14K annual subscription. The v2.0 lens (07-pricing-viability.md §A.3, §G) argued this average is too low — Knightsbridge is $31K at 1:4 service ratio and Latitude's $14K average is "asking principals to pay 45% of Knightsbridge's price for a founder with no track record."

That argument still stands. The v3.0 plan does not address it.

### What should determine placement on the band

Three factors:

**1. Founder-direct exposure.** A Society member with 15-min SLA and quarterly in-person reviews requires founder time, not VA time. At founder $200/hour, a Society member consuming 200 hours/year of founder time costs $40,000 in labor. The subscription floor that breaks even on founder labor at this density is $30K. Anything below $30K is subsidized by other members or by founder time priced below market.

**2. Brand-credibility cost.** A founder with no track record cannot credibly price a 1:6 dedicated-principal service at the same band as Knightsbridge's 1:4. The discount has to be either on price or on positioning. Latitude has chosen the price discount. The right answer in Year 1 is probably positioning instead: keep the band $1,000–$2,500 but price the modal Society member at $2,000/mo ($24K/year), with the explicit framing that Latitude Society is a "founder-led private practice" rather than a "Knightsbridge equivalent."

**3. Member capacity.** The plan models 20 Society members at Y5. At 1:6 ratio that's 3.3 advisors, which under the plan's solo structure is the founder + 2 backups. The founder cannot personally cover 20 members at 15-min SLA without backup. So either Society is capped at 12–15 (the realistic founder-only ceiling) or Society staffs up (the plan does not model this).

### Recommendation on Society band

- Move the modal placement to **$1,800–$2,200/mo** ($21.6K–$26.4K annual). This is closer to the labor floor and lands credibly below Knightsbridge without signaling discount.
- Cap Y5 Society at 12 members, not 20. Reduces revenue forecast at Y5 by ~$140K but is more honest about founder capacity.
- Defer Society launch to H2 2027 (already planned). Use Y2 to manufacture credibility (2–3 published Office case studies, named hotel GM partnerships, founder long-form interview).

The v3.0 plan's Society pricing inherits the v2.0 weakness. The Light/Office repricing was the right pivot; the Society pricing was not pivoted at all and probably should have been.

## 13. Buyer-segment pricing: was the user right to reject it?

The user (founder) rejected the idea of differentiating pricing by ICP (e.g., $189 Light for solo founders, $229 Light for PE associates, $349 Office for RIA principals, $399 Office for PE operating partners).

**The user was right to reject it. Here's why.**

### Why segment-pricing would have made sense

Each ICP has different willingness-to-pay. PE operating partners at $500K T&E can absorb $449/month without friction. Surgeons at $250K T&E might balk at $349. In a textbook price-discrimination model, charging each segment closer to their willingness-to-pay maximizes revenue per member.

The Y5 revenue lift from segment-pricing, modeled crudely: lift Office by $50 on PE-segment members (30% of Office book), lift Light by $25 on RIA-segment members (25% of Light book). That's roughly $14K/year of incremental subscription at Y5. Real money, but small.

### Why segment-pricing was the wrong move anyway

**1. Operating complexity.** Two prices per tier means two sales scripts, two contract templates, two MSA versions, two Stripe price IDs. For a solo founder, this is 2× the cognitive load on every sales call.

**2. Cross-segment referrals.** The Series A founder refers the PE associate. The PE associate refers the RIA principal. If pricing differs by segment, the referral conversation includes "what did you pay?" — and the answer breaks trust.

**3. Brand register.** Operator-luxe brands don't price-discriminate. Patek Philippe doesn't charge different prices to lawyers vs. surgeons. The visual register the founder has built reads at a single price band, not a segmented one.

**4. The buyer math.** The whole Model B argument is "one number, repeatable by the EA, defensible without notes." Segment pricing violates the central simplicity argument.

**5. The 30-sim data.** None of the 30 sims tested segment-pricing. The whole evidence base would have to be re-tested.

**Verdict:** Rejecting segment-pricing was correct. The $14K of Y5 revenue lift is not worth the operating complexity, referral friction, or brand-coherence cost. The right tool for segment-level WTP differences is the tier ladder (Light/Office/Society), not within-tier discrimination.

## 14. What's wrong or missing

### Wrong: the claim that 30 sims "established" the pricing

The sims established directional confidence. They did not establish optimum. The plan should be honest about this. **Reframe §15 to claim "30 sims established that $189/$349 closes; the optimum within 10–20% of these numbers was not tested."**

### Wrong: the §6 bear case doesn't price in labor-negative members

The Y5 bear at $540K assumes a smaller member mix (60/30/5 vs base 60/40/20). It doesn't model the labor-margin failure mode (normal mix but 10–15% of Office members labor-negative). The honest bear is $540K minus another $30–50K = $490–510K Y5. Publish that.

### Missing: a repricing protocol document

No structured trigger for when prices move up. See §9 above. This is a one-page artifact that should ship with the plan, not be invented in Y2 under pressure.

### Missing: multi-year prepay strategy

10% annual prepay on Light is a high-ROI move worth $20–40K of Y5 cash flow advance and 5–8% churn reduction. The plan should add it.

### Missing: the Round 8 sim block specification

If the plan's claim is "30 sims established the pricing," the plan should commit to a Round 8 block testing $229/$399 in Q4 2026. Without it, the founder will reprice in Y2 based on instinct rather than data, repeating the v2 → v3 friction.

### Missing: Society repricing

The Society band wasn't pivoted in v3.0. It should have been. See §12.

### Missing: the non-member conversion mechanic

Plan §2.4 asserts 30–40% conversion. Without a documented conversion mechanic (design fee credit toward membership, 60-day post-trip nudge, etc.), this will run at 10–15%. Engineer the conversion or stop modeling it.

## 15. Recommendations, prioritized

1. **Ship Model B Unlimited at $189/$349 in Y1.** It is defensible, sellable, and operationally simpler than the alternatives. Do not overthink Y1 pricing.

2. **Specify a repricing protocol document.** One page. Five triggers (Round 8 data, labor-margin reality, Virtuoso clip change, Bell & Bly capital event, Y5 labor saturation). Refresh annually.

3. **Add a 10% annual prepay option on Light.** $2,041 vs $2,268 list. High-ROI, low-friction.

4. **Run a Round 8 sim block in Q4 2026 testing $219 Light + $399 Office.** Five sales-cycle stages × 3 FTG mechanics × 2 prices = 30 sims. Match the Round 6/7 methodology. If close rates within 15% of v3.0 prices, reprice at Y2 anniversary.

5. **Reprice Society modal to $2,000/mo ($24K/year), cap Y5 at 12 members.** Move the average up to a credible band, accept the Y5 revenue haircut (~$140K), and protect founder capacity.

6. **Document the labor-negative member trigger and response.** End-of-quarter review. Trigger threshold: 2.5× implied hours. Three documented responses (Society upgrade, coverage adjustment, friendly off-board).

7. **Engineer the non-member conversion mechanic.** Design fee credit toward first 6 months of membership if convert within 60 days. Add a 14-day rush surcharge at 50%. Lift the floor to $750.

8. **Publish the realistic bear case.** Y5 bear = $490–510K (mix decline + labor-negative offset), not $540K. Investors and the founder should plan against the honest number.

9. **Add the 3-active-traveler cap to the MSA, the welcome email, and the quarterly review template.** Without enforcement infrastructure, the cap is aspirational.

10. **Lead with the moat argument in sales conversations, not the price.** The Virtuoso + design + 24/7 stack is the structural defensibility argument and it scales better than the pricing argument. The buyer who buys the moat tolerates the next reprice; the buyer who buys the price churns at the reprice.

## Final note

Model B Unlimited is the right call for Y1. It is not necessarily the right price level for Y2+, and the plan reads as if it might be. The single most important pricing-discipline move the founder can make in 2026 is to treat $189/$349 as **the launch floor for a category-defining bet**, not **the equilibrium price for a stable business**. The Virtuoso-plus-design-plus-24/7 stack is the actual product; the pricing is the wrapper. The wrapper has room to move up — probably $30–50/month on Office, $30–40 on Light — once the founder has the labor data to support it.

The 30-sim evidence is real but partial. The Model A defenders' case is real and underweighted in the plan. The Model C defenders' case is real and slightly overstated by Sim 12. The Society pricing is unrevised from v2.0 and inherits the v2.0 weakness. None of these is a reason to reject Model B Unlimited. All of them are reasons to ship it carefully, instrument it well, and reprice with discipline.

**Grade: B+.** The pivot was correct. The execution still has gaps the plan should close before Q3 2026.
