# 08 — Legal and Regulatory (v3.0)

**Grade: B-**

**One-paragraph verdict.** The v3.0 plan inherits a competent legal posture from v2.0 and quietly fixes one of the v2 landmines (the "First Loss Insurance" name is gone, replaced by "First Trip Guarantee"). But Model B Unlimited materially changes the legal surface in three ways the plan does not yet engage with: (1) zero design fees rewires the seller-of-travel analysis in California — the membership now looks less like a planning-service subscription and more like a pre-paid travel arrangement, which is the exact thing CST was written to police, (2) the 100%-retained subscription is now larger ($189/$349 vs $99/$249) and the Fora side-letter question is therefore more urgent and more contested, and (3) the Society Year-in-Review hosted in NYC, the embedded family-office work, and the founder's referenced "customer trips" combine to push US-day count toward the 120-day soft cap without a written tracking policy in the document. None of these are fatal. All require legal work before a single member is billed. Path A is still defensible, the W-8BEN-E treaty position still holds, and the Ontario CCPC structure is still the right vehicle. The legal section of the plan (§11, half a page) is dangerously short relative to the operational reality. Grade is B- because the v3 pivot was accomplished without re-papering the legal stack, and that gap will surface in the data-room conversation before it surfaces in court.

## What's right

### TICO Path A is still the correct Y1 choice — and v3 actually tightens it

The v2 evaluation pressure-tested Path A and concluded it was operationally narrow but defensible if the founder ran a documented intake control. v3 keeps Path A and §11.1 names it the Y1 default. The Model B pivot does not change the TICO analysis on its own. TICO's jurisdiction triggers on the act of selling travel services to consumers in Ontario, and Ontario residency is the dominant test in practice. The membership-tier change from $99 to $189 does not move that needle.

What v3 quietly improves: the §11.2 line "US sales sourced as services delivered from Canada (no US trade or business)" is correctly framed. The v2 plan was wobblier on this. The Ontario CCPC delivering services into the US under the Canada-US Tax Treaty Article VII business-profits position is the right structural answer, and §11.2 names it.

The Atlas Family Office variant in §2.1 also tightens the residency intake control — the pre-signed NDA and the rolling roster of up to 20 named travelers create a natural documentation moment where country of residence, billing address, and tax residency get captured per traveler. If the intake checklist is built into the onboarding kit (referenced in §8.6 as `/operations/onboarding-kit.md`), the Path A defense gets stronger with every Office member added. This is the kind of mechanical control that survives a TICO complaint investigation.

### The "First Trip Guarantee" rename is the single biggest legal upgrade from v2

The v2 evaluation flagged "First Loss Insurance" as a regulatory landmine. v3 silently fixes it. The new name — First Trip Guarantee, abbreviated FTG — is across §2.3, §5.3, §7.2, §12. The word "insurance" appears in the v3 plan only once, in §11.5 of the v2 evaluation table referring to E&O insurance, which is correct. The Ontario Insurance Act and every US state insurance regulator polices the use of the word "insurance" in product names. FTG sidesteps all of it.

What is also right in v3's FTG construction:

- **§2.3 declares the FTG on the sales call, names the trip, and expires the guarantee after the named trip closes or 90 days.** This is a discrete, bounded, contractually defensible service-level promise. It is not insurance. It is not a perpetual warranty. It is a defined-event, defined-remedy clause.
- **FTG1's labor credit caps at the greater of $1,000 or trip value or three months of dues.** The three-months cap is a real number, and it bounds the worst-case payout per invocation.
- **FTG3 is a 30-day money-back on the membership itself.** This is the cleanest mechanic legally — refund of services paid for, no statement of fault, no admission of breach.
- **The FTG is "on us" — failures on Latitude 43's end, not supplier failures.** §2.3 implies this, and the MSA must make it explicit. Supplier failures (airline cancellation, hotel walk, etc.) sit outside the FTG and properly fall to the supplier's own remedies plus the member's travel insurance.

### W-8BEN-E treaty position is still the right answer

§11.2 names W-8BEN-E filing with Fora to avoid 30% US withholding. This is correct. The CCPC is a foreign entity for US tax purposes. W-8BEN-E with treaty position under Canada-US Tax Treaty Article VII (business profits — services performed in Canada, no US PE) zeros US withholding on commission paid by Fora. Form W-9 would signal US tax residency and trigger 30% withholding plus 1099 reporting. The plan gets this right.

The supporting analysis the plan does not show but should keep in the data room:

- Form 1042-S issued by Fora to Latitude 43 Inc. annually.
- Form 1120-F not required absent US trade or business with effectively connected income. Solo Canadian advisor with no US fixed place of business and US-day count under 120: no 1120-F.
- T1135 Foreign Income Verification Statement not triggered by the Fora relationship. Triggered if the founder personally holds US-situs specified foreign property over CAD $100K cost base (US bank, US brokerage, direct US LLC interest). The CCPC holding USD at a Canadian bank does not trigger T1135.

### Fora structure is fit-for-purpose

§1 names Fora as "US ARC/IATA, CLIA, state seller-of-travel registrations." This is accurate. Fora holds ARC and IATA accreditation, CLIA cruise accreditation, and state SOT registrations in CA, FL, WA, HI, and IA. The advisor (Latitude 43) operates under Fora's accreditation umbrella and routes bookings through Fora's merchant-of-record systems. The legal architecture is:

- **Fora handles:** ARC/IATA accreditation, CLIA, state SOT registrations for the booking transaction, merchant-of-record status on bookings, advisor E&O umbrella on Fora-routed bookings, Form 1042-S issuance, supplier accreditation (Marriott STARS, Hyatt Privé, Belmond Bellini, Mandarin Fan Club — all of which are routed through Fora's host status).
- **Advisor (Latitude 43) handles:** membership product contract, Stripe billing for the membership, the relationship with the member, T2 corporate return, GST/HST registration and zero-rating analysis, TICO compliance (Path A documentation), member-product E&O (gap coverage), cyber insurance, privacy policy, trademark, the Canadian CPA, the cross-border tax memo.
- **Virtuoso** sits under Fora's Virtuoso membership. The 2% clip described in §1 is applied at the Virtuoso level before the Fora-advisor split. Advisor cannot independently hold Virtuoso outside a host.

This split is operationally clean and the v3 plan describes it correctly. The legal question that remains is the one carried forward from v2: is the membership product itself within Fora's SOT registrations, or does it sit outside as a separate Latitude 43 product needing its own analysis. Under v2 the answer was "ambiguous, get a memo." Under v3 Unlimited, the answer leans toward "the membership is closer to a sale-of-travel arrangement and the memo is now urgent." Detailed below.

### The Ontario CCPC structure and dividend treatment

§11.2 names the operating entity as Latitude 43 Inc., an Ontario CCPC, with dividends as "pre-eligible." Correct posture for active business income under the small business deduction. Combined federal-Ontario rate on the first $500K of ABI is ~12.2% (2026). Above $500K, ~26.5% combined. Founder takes salary up to the CPP-max and RRSP-room threshold, remainder as non-eligible dividends (corporate income was taxed at SBD rate). HSA inside the CCPC for medical expenses is a small but real $5K-$15K/year tax-efficient benefit. US-Canada Treaty Article VII keeps commission and membership revenue out of US tax assuming no US PE. The structure is right; the documentation is thin. A grade-A treatment would include a one-page tax appendix.

## What's questionable

### CA seller-of-travel analysis under Unlimited

This is the v3-specific issue the plan has not engaged with.

Under v2 (Model A — $99 Light, $249 Office, design fee escalator), the membership was reasonably arguable as a planning-service subscription with a discount on design fees. The legal posture: the membership is a service contract for advisory time, design fees are paid per-trip transaction, and Fora's state SOT registrations cover the actual booking. The membership was not the sale of travel; it was the sale of access to a travel professional.

Under v3 (Model B Unlimited — $189 Light, $349 Office, zero design fees), the analysis shifts. The membership now bundles:

- All trip design (no marginal fee)
- All booking work (no marginal fee)
- Virtuoso benefits at hotels
- Per-trip receipts
- The First Trip Guarantee
- The First 30 / 90-day no-fault exit

This bundle, especially in CA, looks more like a pre-paid arrangement of travel services than a planning subscription. The California Seller of Travel Law (Bus & Prof Code §17550 et seq.) defines a seller of travel broadly as "a person who sells, provides, furnishes, contracts for, arranges, or advertises that he or she can or may arrange, or has arranged" wholesale or retail travel-related services. The "arranges, or has arranged" language is the trapdoor. A $349/month membership where "everything is included" and the desk arranges any travel the member asks for is closer to "arranges travel for compensation" than "sells planning time."

Two consequences:

1. **CA CST registration may be required for Latitude 43 Inc. itself**, separate from Fora's CST registration. Fora's CST covers the booking transactions Fora routes. The membership fee is billed to Latitude 43's Stripe account, on Latitude 43's contract, and is not a Fora booking. The fact that Fora is the merchant of record on downstream bookings does not necessarily extend Fora's CST to the membership product.
2. **The $1M CST bond or trust account requirement** is what makes this expensive. CA CST offers an alternative — participation in the Travel Consumer Restitution Fund (TCRF), which is the cheap path. Annual fee is modest ($100 + restitution fund contribution). TCRF participation requires that the registrant is a CA-registered seller. A Canadian CCPC selling into CA can register but the trust account / bond / TCRF path is more complex.

The v3 plan is silent on this. The v2 evaluation flagged it and recommended a $3K–$5K legal memo. Under v3 Unlimited, the memo is more urgent because the membership is structurally further from "planning services" and closer to "arranging travel for compensation."

**Action that should already be in flight:** a written legal opinion from a CA-licensed travel-industry firm (Krooth & Altman, Baker Hostetler, Maggio + Kattar) on whether the Latitude 43 Atlas Light / Atlas Office membership product is a "sale of travel" requiring separate CA CST registration. If yes, register Latitude 43 in CA at the TCRF tier. If no, paper the file and move on.

### W-8BEN-E with Fora — confirm the side letter

§11.2 names the W-8BEN-E filing. What is not named: written confirmation from Fora that:

1. The W-8BEN-E treaty position is accepted and 30% withholding will not be applied.
2. Form 1042-S will be issued annually showing zero withholding.
3. Fora's interpretation of the Latitude 43 Inc. relationship is that the commission is paid to a Canadian corporation, not to the founder personally (which would change the analysis).
4. Latitude 43's separate membership product is acknowledged as not being a Fora-merchant-of-record transaction, and Fora makes no claim on the membership fee.

Item 4 is the v3-specific question. The Model B Unlimited pivot means subscription revenue is meaningfully larger ($83K Y1 → $592K Y5) and 100% retained by Latitude 43. Fora is a venture-backed company that has raised significant capital and will eventually need to argue that more of its advisor revenue rides on its platform. The cleanest defense is a written side letter signed before launch confirming that membership fees are not within Fora's commission share and that Fora makes no claim on them now or under any future term change.

This costs nothing to ask for and is the single most important pre-launch document.

### US permanent establishment risk — the v3 plan understates the exposure

§11.2 names "no US permanent establishment — written approval workflow for any planned on-site engagement on US soil (e.g., Society Year-in-Review hosted in NYC)." This is the right idea but the plan does not show the math.

Under Canada-US Tax Treaty Article V, a PE arises in two ways for Latitude 43:

1. **Fixed place of business** — an office, a desk, a leased space, a hotel room used regularly for client work in the US for an extended period. A solo Canadian founder with no US office does not trigger this.
2. **Services PE under Article V(9)** — physical presence in the US providing services for more than 183 days in any 12-month period, OR more than 50% of gross active business revenue derived from US services performed during periods totaling 183+ days.

The 50%-revenue test is the dangerous one. 100% of Latitude 43's revenue is US-sourced. The 183-day test is the saving grace, but it is closer than the plan suggests.

What v3 actually proposes the founder do in the US:

- **Society Year-in-Review hosted in NYC** — quarterly per §2.1. Four trips per year, 2-3 days each = 8-12 days.
- **Customer visits and on-site meetings** — implied throughout §3 and §7 for cold-inbound discovery and account management. Realistic at 8-15 trips per year, 1-2 days each = 8-30 days.
- **Society events and member-hosted gatherings** — implied in §2.1 ("Quarterly in-person Year-in-Review") and §8 ("year-in-review template" in operations).
- **Industry conferences** (ACG, SBIA, Schwab IMPACT, Barron's, T3) — referenced in §3.2 and §3.4. Realistic at 4-6 conferences per year, 2-3 days each = 8-18 days.
- **Familiarization trips** (Latitude FAM, `/operations/fam-trip-briefing.md` referenced in §8.6) — Virtuoso and supplier FAM trips often run in the US. 2-4 FAMs per year, 3-5 days each = 6-20 days.

Lower-bound total: 30-40 US days per year. Upper-bound: 70-100 US days. The 183-day threshold is not close, but the 120-day soft cap recommended for safety (33-day buffer below the threshold) is achievable in a busy Society launch year.

The plan does not name a US-day tracking discipline. A grade-A treatment would include:

- A calendar-app entry per US day, dumped monthly to a tracking spreadsheet, retained for seven years (CRA audit window).
- A written cap policy: 120 US days per calendar year, 150 days per rolling 12-month window.
- A pre-trip approval workflow: any US trip over 3 days requires founder sign-off against the YTD count.
- A cross-border tax memo on file from a Canadian cross-border CPA (e.g., MNP cross-border practice, Crowe Soberman, BDO) confirming the PE analysis and the cap policy.

The "written approval workflow" referenced in §11.2 should be cited to the specific document. If it does not exist yet, it should before Society launches in H2 2027.

### MSA and dispute resolution — the plan is silent

§11 has no mention of the Master Service Agreement (the membership contract). v3's Office tier at $349/mo and Society at $14K average/year are large enough purchases that a real contract is required, not a click-through. The v2 evaluation devoted Section H to this; v3 has not engaged with it.

Critical MSA clauses for a v3-Unlimited membership:

- **Governing law and venue** — New York or Delaware law; AAA Consumer Arbitration in NY County. Ontario governing law adds friction for US clients.
- **Class action waiver** — standard US consumer contract.
- **Limitation of liability** — cap at 12 months of fees paid, exclude consequential damages.
- **Auto-renewal under CA ARL (Bus & Prof Code §17600 et seq.)** — clear cancellation method, advance notice of renewal, "click to cancel" compliance. CA, NY, OR all have ARL statutes with private rights of action. Stripe's auto-renewal flow needs to be configured to comply.
- **SLA language** — the 60-minute SLA is a contractual promise. The MSA must define what "60-minute SLA" means operationally (acknowledgment within 60 minutes, not resolution), what counts as an exception (force majeure, member-side delay, supplier-side delay), and what the remedy is for breach (the FTG covers first-trip failures; what about a routine 90-minute response on a Tuesday afternoon?). Without this, the SLA is a marketing promise that becomes a chargeback defense at best and a misrepresentation claim at worst.
- **FTG language** — explicit, plain-English, with the three mechanics named (FTG1, FTG2, FTG3) and the named-trip and expiry rules from §2.3.
- **90-day no-fault exit** — pro-rated refund of unused subscription, commission booked on completed trips not refunded.
- **Data processing addendum** — incorporated by reference.
- **TCPA SMS consent** — explicit opt-in for service SMS to members; outbound marketing SMS to non-members requires separate consent.
- **Indemnification** — mutual, narrow; avoid one-sided indemnity.
- **Travel-industry disclaimers** — Latitude 43 is an advisor, not a guarantor of supplier performance, not a travel insurance company, not responsible for force majeure.

The v3 plan should budget $4K–$8K for a NY-licensed travel-industry lawyer to draft this. The cost of not having a real MSA is that the first member dispute that goes beyond "give me a refund" lands the founder in small-claims court with a click-through agreement that may not survive scrutiny.

### Stripe US entity routing — does Latitude 43 Inc. need a US subsidiary

The plan does not address this directly. The architecture under Path A is:

- Stripe account in the name of Latitude 43 Inc. (Ontario CCPC), with a Canadian business address, Canadian BN, and a USD-denominated payout to a USD account.
- Members in the US are billed in USD; Stripe processes the transaction, takes its fee, deposits USD to the Latitude 43 USD bank account.
- For Stripe purposes, Latitude 43 is a Canadian merchant accepting US-issued cards. Stripe permits this; cross-border processing fees are slightly higher (~0.6% surcharge on non-Canadian cards).

The question of whether to incorporate a US subsidiary (Latitude 43 USA LLC, Delaware) is structural. The arguments for and against:

**For a US subsidiary:**
- Cleaner US-resident customer experience (US billing entity on receipts, US business address on the MSA).
- Easier US-bank relationship (US payroll if needed in the future, US business credit card).
- Potential cleaner CA CST registration if required.
- Some US enterprise buyers (CFO-driven procurement) prefer to contract with a US entity.

**Against:**
- US LLC creates US tax filing requirements (Form 1065 partnership return or Form 1120 corp return depending on classification). Compliance cost ~$3K–$5K per year.
- Form 5471 controlled-foreign-corporation reporting on the Canadian parent (if structured as wholly-owned US sub of CCPC, may be complex).
- T1134 controlled foreign affiliate reporting on the Canadian side.
- US LLC creates state nexus where it operates (Delaware franchise tax minimum, plus any state where the LLC has employees or office).
- Permanent establishment analysis gets harder, not easier — a US LLC means Latitude 43 has a US fixed place of business, which is the textbook PE trigger.

**Recommendation:** No US subsidiary in Y1 or Y2. The Stripe-as-Canadian-merchant path works for Y1-Y2 revenue scale ($115K-$222K). Revisit when revenue exceeds $500K, or earlier if a CFO buyer specifically requires US contracting.

### Privacy and data — v3 inherits the v2 stack and adds the EA seat

§11 is silent on privacy. The v2 evaluation devoted Section E to this; v3 has not engaged with it. The Model B-specific issues:

1. **EA Companion Seat creates a third-party data issue.** The principal's EA is a non-principal user of the desk. The EA accesses traveler data for principals other than themselves (the principal, the principal's spouse, the principal's children if Office tier with family travel included). PIPEDA requires consent of each data subject for collection and use of their personal information. The MSA should include a representation by the principal that they have authority to share family data, plus a separate EA-facing data agreement covering the EA's access and use.
2. **Society's Year-in-Review NYC events create incidental data flows.** Photos, attendance lists, dietary requirements collected for in-person events. PIPEDA applies; explicit opt-in for any photos used in marketing.
3. **CCPA (CPRA) is still not triggered at Y1 scale.** $25M revenue threshold and 100K-consumer threshold are not within reach until Y3+. Monitor at Y3. The "selling personal information" prong does not apply to Latitude 43 unless the business model changes.
4. **GDPR is not triggered until an EU-resident member signs up.** The §3 ICP is explicitly US-based. Defer GDPR readiness until the first EU member or EU marketing activity.

**Data residency.** The plan does not name where member profiles live. The default Fora stack uses Tern as the advisor CRM (US-hosted). Latitude 43 should:

- Use Tern as the primary CRM. Confirm Tern's SOC 2 Type II and data residency.
- Use a separate encrypted document storage for passport scans (not Notion, not email, not Slack). OneDrive with BitLocker or a purpose-built travel tool with encryption at rest.
- Sign a DPA with Tern. PIPEDA permits cross-border transfer with notice; the DPA is the notice.
- Retain passport scans on expiry + 1 year. Purge on member off-boarding.
- Have an incident-response runbook for breach notification under PIPEDA (Office of the Privacy Commissioner notification within 30 days of breach).

Privacy is not a v3-specific risk, but the v3 plan should have caught up on the v2 evaluation's Section E findings.

## What's wrong or missing

### The cross-border tax flow on Fora 1099 vs T4A

The plan does not address how Fora reports commission to Latitude 43 Inc. for tax purposes. Under the W-8BEN-E treaty position, Fora issues Form 1042-S annually showing gross commission and zero withholding. Form 1042-S is the US informational return for payments to foreign persons; it is the correct form. The plan should name this in §11 and budget the cross-border CPA cost ($4K-$6K Y1) to handle the reconciliation between the Form 1042-S and the Canadian T2 corporate return.

Fora does not issue a 1099 to a foreign corporation under treaty position. Fora does not issue a T4A — that is a Canadian information return Fora has no obligation to file.

### The MSA / SLA enforcement language is not in the plan

The 60-minute SLA in §8.1 is operationally clear but contractually undefined. A grade-A MSA defines:

- "Acknowledgment" — a human or AI-routed response confirming receipt of the inquiry.
- "Response" — substantive engagement with the inquiry.
- "Resolution" — closure of the inquiry.

The SLA should commit to acknowledgment within 60 minutes, not resolution. Resolution time depends on supplier response times that are outside Latitude 43's control. Without this clarity, a member can claim breach on any inquiry that runs past 60 minutes to resolution, which is operationally untenable.

### Insurance coverage — gap relative to v2 evaluation

The v2 evaluation recommended a $4K-$5.8K Y1 insurance package: independent E&O ($2M), cyber ($1M), GL ($2M), personal umbrella. The v3 plan does not name insurance. §12 risk register names "single-founder bus risk" and "PHT VA quality fails" but does not name a cyber breach or an E&O claim.

The v3 Unlimited model raises the E&O exposure because the all-included design promise creates more advisory touchpoints per member per year (estimated 22 trips/year for Office under §4). More touchpoints means more opportunities for an error to surface. Independent E&O from Day 1, not Y2.

### Chargeback risk under the 90-day no-fault exit

§2.2 references "The First 30 — 90-day no-fault exit, monthly Stripe billing." The legal mechanics:

- Stripe's chargeback rate threshold: 0.9% (warning) and 1.0% (penalty program).
- At Y5 base case (60 + 40 + 20 = 120 members billing monthly): 1,440 transactions per year. Threshold is 14 disputes per year before warning territory.
- Realistic exposure: 1-3 chargebacks per year. Well within tolerance.

The 90-day no-fault refund is itself a chargeback-mitigation tool — a member who can get a refund without friction in the first 90 days has no reason to chargeback through their card issuer. Beyond 90 days, chargeback exposure on monthly subscriptions is limited because each charge is small ($189-$349) and the member has accepted service through the renewal date.

The risk that is real: the FTG2 refund (full month + free month + $500 service credit) is operationally a refund event. The accounting treatment matters. Membership revenue should be recognized ratably over the month, not at the point of billing, so refunds are netted against the same period's revenue. The cross-border CPA should confirm this revenue-recognition policy for the Canadian T2 schedule.

### The First Trip Guarantee — accounting treatment and reserves

§5.3 names a labor-margin risk for high-usage members and three structural protections. What is not in the plan: an accounting reserve for FTG payouts.

If FTG invocation rate is 5% of new members per year (within §12 risk register tolerance), and average payout is the lesser of $1,000 or three months' dues, then at Y5 scale (assume ~40 new members per year):

- FTG1 invocations: 2 per year × $1,047 (3 months Office) = ~$2,100 per year
- FTG2 invocations: 1 per year × ($349 refund + $349 free month + $500 credit) = ~$1,200 per year
- FTG3 invocations: 1 per year × $349 refund = ~$350 per year
- **Total annual FTG reserve: ~$3,500-$5,000**

This is small but should be modeled. The plan does not budget it.

### Society NYC events — written approval workflow not in the document

§11.2 references "written approval workflow for any planned on-site engagement on US soil (e.g., Society Year-in-Review hosted in NYC)." The workflow is not in `/operations/year-in-review-template.md` (per §8.6 reference). It should be:

1. Pre-event US-day count check against the 120-day annual cap.
2. Event date and duration logged in the calendar tracker.
3. Sales-tax / nexus check for the event city (NYC creates NY state and NYC sales-tax considerations for any event-related billing).
4. NY commercial GL insurance rider for the event venue (most venues require $1M-$2M GL named additional insured).
5. Photo / data consent forms for attendees.
6. Sign-off by the founder before the event is booked.

A one-page template covering all six items belongs in the operations folder.
