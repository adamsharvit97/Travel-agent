# The Latitude Journal — Weeks 1 through 12

A 12-week launch sequence for the founder's LinkedIn newsletter. Operator-grade voice. No listicles. No hashtag confetti. The reader should finish each post feeling they have learned something they did not previously know about the business of moving senior people around the world.

Each post follows the same structure: title, dek, hook (the first 2–3 sentences that appear in the LinkedIn feed preview), full draft, hashtags (3–5 max), image direction.

## Week 1 — Why your Amex Platinum Concierge is failing you

**Title:** Why your Amex Platinum Concierge is failing you
**Dek:** The card concierge was designed to book Carbone, not to rescue you from a missed Frankfurt connection. A field report.
**Hashtags:** #BusinessTravel #FounderOps #TravelManagement

**Hook**

I have spent the last decade watching founders and partners discover, usually around the third week of a hard quarter, that the Amex Platinum concierge is not the white-glove emergency desk they thought they were buying. It is a switchboard. A polite, well-trained, almost-always-American switchboard, but a switchboard. When your flight from Newark to Munich cancels at 11:42 pm and Lufthansa's app has already routed you into a 1,400-person rebooking queue, the concierge cannot rebook you. They can only call the same Lufthansa number you can call.

**Full draft**

This is not a complaint about Amex. The card is a brilliant product. The Centurion Lounge network is the single best amenity ever stapled to a piece of metal. The travel insurance — particularly the trip-delay reimbursement, which pays out for delays of six or more hours, up to $500 per ticket — is materially more generous than any consumer card I am aware of. I keep my Platinum. So does every senior person I work with.

The misunderstanding is about scope. The Platinum concierge was built, structurally and contractually, around four things: dinner reservations, theatre and sports tickets, gift sourcing, and what the industry politely calls "lifestyle requests." The job of the agent on the other end of the line is to be the person who, at 4 pm on a Friday, gets you the corner table at Carbone, the two tickets to the Knicks game, and the same-day delivery of a $1,200 watch to a hotel concierge in Paris. They are very good at this. They are also, structurally, not the person who solves a travel emergency.

Here is what actually happens when you call the Platinum line for a travel emergency:

The agent answers. They ask for the reservation. You explain that your flight has cancelled and you need to be in Munich by 9 am for a board meeting. The agent, who has been on the desk for somewhere between six months and three years, opens up the Amex Travel booking tool, which is a thin layer over a wholesale GDS, and looks at the same Lufthansa availability you can see in your United app. They identify the same three options you have identified. They offer to book one of them. You agree. They book it. You hang up. Total time: somewhere between 18 and 35 minutes, depending on the desk's queue depth.

What they cannot do, structurally:

They cannot call the Lufthansa station manager at Newark, who is the only person on the planet who can short-list you for the protected-fare seat the airline holds back from the GDS during irregular ops. The station manager does not pick up phone calls from a card-concierge desk. They pick up phone calls from a named TMC agent with a Sabre PCC code and a fifteen-year supplier relationship.

They cannot reach into the hotel's central reservations override layer and waive the cancellation fee on tonight's Andaz Munich room because you are no longer arriving until tomorrow. The card concierge has no authority over a hotel's revenue management system. A Virtuoso-credentialed advisor with a direct line to the hotel's director of sales can usually get that fee waived in one phone call.

They cannot get you into the upgrade list at the Park Hyatt Vienna using a STARS-rated booking, because the Platinum desk does not transact through World of Hyatt's preferred-partner program. STARS is a B2B channel reserved for advisors who deliver enough room-night volume to be coded into Hyatt's CRM. The card concierge is not in that CRM.

They cannot extend your United Polaris connection to allow you to stop in Frankfurt for two hours to take a customer call, because that requires a manual fare construction with a married-segment override, which is a Sabre desk task that the Amex tool does not surface to its agents.

I list these specifics not to make a competitor look bad. I list them because the people I work with — founders, GPs, RIA principals — are almost universally surprised by how narrow the card-concierge scope actually is. They have, reasonably, assumed that the most expensive consumer card in North America comes with the most expensive consumer travel desk. It does not. It comes with a very polite reservations layer.

The structural reason is operational. The Platinum concierge is staffed by Amex Travel Related Services, which prices for volume and ROI on the card itself. The unit economics of card concierge — millions of cardholders, a small percentage of whom call in any given month — require an average handle time under twenty minutes. A real travel emergency takes ninety. The desk is not built for ninety-minute calls.

The fix, if you spend $80,000 or more a year on travel, is to put a named human between yourself and the airline. Someone with a Sabre PCC. Someone with hotel-side override authority. Someone whose first response to "the flight just cancelled" is not to open the same app you have open, but to call the station manager directly.

Keep the Platinum. Keep the lounges. Keep the trip-delay reimbursement. Just stop expecting the concierge to be the emergency room. It is the maître d'.

## Week 2 — The 2 a.m. Frankfurt test

**Title:** The 2 a.m. Frankfurt test
**Dek:** A method for evaluating any travel partner in 90 seconds. Built around the moment that actually matters.
**Hashtags:** #BusinessTravel #ExecutiveTravel #FounderOps

**Hook**

Here is the only diagnostic that matters when you are choosing a travel partner. Ignore the website copy. Ignore the awards. Ignore the supplier-relationship boasts. Ask one question: *what specifically happens, minute by minute, if I miss a connection in Frankfurt at 2 a.m. on a Tuesday in February.* Then time how long it takes them to answer. Fifteen seconds means they have rehearsed it. Thirty means they are improvising. Sixty means they have never thought about it.

**Full draft**

I call this the 2 a.m. Frankfurt test, but it can be the 4 a.m. São Paulo test or the 11 pm Singapore test. The pattern is the same. Pick the hardest hour in the hardest airport on the hardest day of the week, and ask the partner what they do.

A good answer has six components. I will walk through each.

First, *who picks up*. A good partner can tell you the name of the person on the night desk. Not a department. A person. Or, in a smaller firm, the founder's mobile. A bad partner says "our after-hours desk is staffed 24/7" and then cannot tell you where it is staffed from or what time zone the agents are on. A worse partner makes you call a different number than your daytime contact, with a different code.

Second, *the first response SLA*. A good partner says "you'll hear back in writing within 60 minutes, sometimes faster." That is a specific commitment. A bad partner says "we'll respond as quickly as possible." Two of the four largest luxury travel agencies in the United States — both of them with marketing copy that promises 24/7 coverage — have no published SLA anywhere on their site, and bury the after-hours number in a privacy policy.

Third, *the supplier they call first*. A good partner names a person at the airline. "I would call the Lufthansa station manager at FRA, whose direct line I have." A bad partner says they would call "Lufthansa." That difference is operational competence.

Fourth, *the hotel side*. A good partner says "I would call the night manager at the Hyatt Regency FRA, where you have a STARS code, and have them hold your room and forgive tonight's no-show fee." A bad partner says "we would find you a hotel." The difference is whether the room is held and the fee is forgiven before you walk in.

Fifth, *what they tell you to do in the meantime*. A good partner gives you a single instruction: "Go to gate Z. I'll text you in eight minutes with the rebooking." A bad partner asks you what you want to do.

Sixth, *how the next morning is handled*. A good partner has already, by the time you wake up, rebooked your ground transfer in Munich, notified the next day's hotel of your late check-in, and sent a one-paragraph summary of what changed. A bad partner waits for you to call them.

I have run this test on roughly forty travel partners over the years — TMCs, advisories, card concierges, friends-of-friends. The results sort cleanly. Maybe four firms have all six components fluent. Maybe ten firms have three or four. The remainder fail at component one: they cannot tell you the name of the person on the night desk because they do not know.

There is a structural reason for this. Most travel businesses are built around the day shift. The night desk, if it exists, is either outsourced to a consortium-shared call center in Manila or routed to a different brand inside the same corporate parent. The economic incentive is to pool overnight volume across multiple agencies; the operational consequence is that the person who answers your 2 a.m. Frankfurt call has no relationship with your daytime advisor and no memory of your travel profile.

The 2 a.m. test is, in this sense, a test of whether the firm you are hiring is one firm or whether it is a daytime brand with an overnight pass-through. The membership advisory model I work in solves this by simply not pooling. The advisor on your contract is on call. Their mobile is in your phone. The 60-minute SLA applies whether it is 2 pm in Toronto or 2 am in Frankfurt.

The test takes ninety seconds to run. It is the highest-signal interaction you will have during a sales process. If a prospective partner cannot pass it, the rest of the pitch is decoration.

## Week 3 — What a TMC actually charges you (and what they hide)

**Title:** What a TMC actually charges you (and what they hide)
**Dek:** Three years of procurement disclosures, six fee categories, and the line items most clients never see.
**Hashtags:** #TravelManagement #CFO #Procurement

**Hook**

Travel management company pricing is, by industry convention, opaque. The "fee structure" you sign on the master services agreement is rarely the all-in cost. After eighteen months of reading procurement disclosures from universities, state agencies, and a handful of mid-market companies, here is the actual line-item breakdown of what a TMC costs. The number that matters is at the bottom, and it is not the number that appears on the marketing PDF.

**Full draft**

A TMC contract usually has six fee categories. Most clients see two of them.

*Category one: the agent-assisted transaction fee.* This is the headline number. Industry range, per Business Travel News's TMC fee primer, is $25 to $35 for a domestic agent-assisted ticket and $50 or more for international. Dartmouth College's published ALTOUR contract puts the per-transaction fee at $26 domestic. This is the only fee most travel managers can quote from memory.

*Category two: the online booking tool fee.* If your travelers self-serve through Concur, Deem, GetThere, or Rearden, the TMC charges a separate, lower per-transaction fee — industry range $7 to $15. The catch: most TMCs charge an additional "OBT licensing" or "platform" fee on top of the per-transaction charge, which is a monthly or annual line item often calculated per active user.

*Category three: the after-hours fee.* The 24/7 desk is rarely included in the day-shift transaction fee. Most TMCs charge an additional $15 to $35 per after-hours call, plus a higher per-ticket fee for tickets issued outside business hours. If your travelers call the after-hours desk twice a month each, and you have twenty travelers, the after-hours line item alone can run $7,000 to $17,000 a year.

*Category four: the implementation fee.* One-time charge to set up your account, configure the OBT, build your traveler profiles, integrate with your expense system, and onboard your travel manager. Industry range: $10,000 to $50,000 depending on complexity. This is usually amortized into the first-year contract or waived if the contract is large enough, but it is real money.

*Category five: the management fee, or its disguised cousin, the volume rebate.* For larger programs, the TMC may charge a flat management fee — say, $5,000 to $20,000 a month — in exchange for a dedicated account team. Alternatively, the TMC may waive the management fee but retain a share of the supplier override commissions it earns on your bookings. This is the line item where the TMC's economics actually live.

*Category six: pass-through charges.* GDS segment fees on certain contracts. Credit card merchant fees if you use a corporate card the TMC has to process. Visa and passport service charges. Risk-management feed subscriptions. Any duty-of-care platform license — SafeToGo, International SOS, World Travel Protection — passed through with or without a markup.

A mid-market program that books 3,000 tickets a year through a TMC, half agent-assisted and half online, with twenty travelers and a modestly active after-hours desk, will pay something like:

| Line item | Annual cost |
|---|---|
| Agent-assisted tickets (1,500 × $28 weighted) | $42,000 |
| Online tickets (1,500 × $11) | $16,500 |
| After-hours calls (~480 × $25) | $12,000 |
| OBT licensing (20 users × $25/mo) | $6,000 |
| Implementation (amortized over 3 years) | $7,000 |
| Risk management platform pass-through | $4,800 |
| **Total visible fees** | **$88,300** |

Then there is the part most clients never see: the supplier override commissions. When a TMC books a hotel through its consortia channel — Virtuoso, Signature, FROSCH's Ensemble — it earns 10 to 16 percent commission on the room rate. When it books an airline ticket through a preferred-fare contract, it earns a back-end override of 1 to 3 percent. On a $2 million annual program, supplier overrides can generate another $80,000 to $150,000 of TMC revenue, none of which appears on your invoice. Some TMCs share this transparently; most do not.

The structural point is this: the headline transaction fee is somewhere between 35 and 50 percent of what the TMC actually earns from you. The rest is in the after-hours surcharges, the OBT licensing, the pass-throughs, and especially the supplier overrides. When you negotiate your TMC contract, you are negotiating the 35 percent. The 65 percent is opaque by design.

The membership model exists because of this asymmetry. If you charge a fixed annual fee — $3,600 a year for a single traveler, $12,000 for a small office, $36,000 for a firm of fifteen — and you book all tickets, all hotels, all transfers, with no per-transaction surcharge, you have collapsed five of the six fee categories into one number on the homepage. The supplier overrides still exist; you still earn them. But the buyer is buying clarity, not arbitrage.

What this means for you, as the buyer: when you are evaluating a TMC, ask for the all-in cost projection for a 12-month period using your last year's actual transaction volumes. Make them produce a number with six line items, not one. If the partner cannot or will not, the partner has answered your question.

## Week 4 — The case for a named human

**Title:** The case for a named human
**Dek:** The pod model versus the dedicated advisor. The hidden economics behind which one you actually get.
**Hashtags:** #BusinessTravel #CustomerExperience #Operations

**Hook**

The single most consequential question to ask a prospective travel partner is also the most uncomfortable: who, specifically, is going to be on the email reply when I write in at 11 pm. Not a team. Not a department. A name. If the answer is "your dedicated team of experienced advisors," the answer is the pod model. The pod model is fine — it is how most TMCs are built, and it has been the industry default for thirty years. It is also not what most senior buyers actually want, and it is almost never what they think they are buying.

**Full draft**

The pod model works like this. A travel management company assigns your company to a pod of three to six agents, plus a senior account manager who oversees the relationship. When you email the team inbox, the next available agent picks up the request. They have access to your traveler profile, your policy, and your prior trip history through the GDS, the OBT, and the TMC's internal CRM. If the agent who handled your last trip is on lunch, the next agent picks up. If they are on vacation, a backup agent picks up. Continuity is structural, not personal.

The advantages of the pod model are real. It scales. Three agents can cover one another seamlessly across time zones. The agent on shift has access to the same data as the agent who was last on your account. Vacation coverage is automatic. The risk of a single point of failure — one agent quits, one agent gets sick — is distributed.

The disadvantages are also real. The agent who picks up does not remember that your wife's name is Mira and that you always book the corner room at the Mandarin Oriental Geneva because the radiator under the window sometimes leaks. The agent does not remember that your favorite seat on the United 787 to Munich is 6A. The agent does not remember that you prefer to arrive at LaGuardia ninety minutes early because security at Terminal B is unpredictable. All of this exists in your traveler profile, in theory. In practice, none of it gets retrieved on a 90-second handle-time call.

The named-human model is the opposite. One advisor handles your account. They know your travel patterns by memory, not by lookup. When you write in at 11 pm, the email goes to a single inbox, and a single human answers. If the advisor is on vacation, a documented secondary picks up — but the secondary is one person, not a rotation. The relationship is one-to-one.

The cost of the named-human model is the cost of the human. A senior corporate travel advisor with fifteen years of experience commands $90,000 to $140,000 a year in base compensation in a US TMC. To maintain a one-to-one model, the advisor's book has to be small enough that they can hold the detail in their head. For a senior corporate book, the realistic ceiling is somewhere between 12 and 20 active accounts, depending on travel intensity.

This is the structural reason the pod model is the industry default. The math does not work otherwise. If a TMC is charging a per-transaction fee of $28 and a management fee of $5,000 a month, the gross revenue per account is too thin to support a dedicated advisor. The pod model lets a TMC spread one advisor across four or five accounts, which is what the unit economics require.

The membership model breaks this constraint. If a client pays $3,600 or $12,000 or $36,000 a year as a flat membership fee, the advisor's economic unit becomes the account, not the transaction. A book of fifteen accounts at an average $12,000 each is $180,000 of revenue. That is enough to support one advisor making $100,000 in compensation, with margin left for the platform, the supplier relationships, and the overnight backstop. The named-human model becomes economically rational at exactly the membership-pricing band.

What this means in practice: when you write in, the response comes from the same person every time. When the trip is rebooked at 2 am, the rebooking advisor is the same advisor who built the original itinerary, which means they know that the Tuesday morning meeting in Munich is the one that cannot be missed and the Wednesday afternoon meeting in Berlin is the one that can. When you change jobs, the advisor moves with you. When the advisor goes on vacation, you get a hand-off email naming the secondary, with context, in advance.

There is one further consequence worth naming. The named-human model produces a different kind of trust than the pod model. The pod model produces trust in the brand. The named-human model produces trust in the person. The two are not the same thing. The pod model is what you want when the brand is what you are buying. The named-human model is what you want when the trip is what you are buying.

For a founder, a GP, a partner — for any senior buyer whose travel is high-stakes and idiosyncratic — the trip is what they are buying. The brand is a wrapper. The named human is the product.

## Week 5 — The upgrades you have already earned but cannot see

**Title:** The upgrades you have already earned but cannot see
**Dek:** Virtuoso, FHR, STARS, and the B2B booking codes that quietly upgrade you when an advisor uses them.
**Hashtags:** #LuxuryTravel #BusinessTravel #HotelIndustry

**Hook**

If you booked a five-night stay at the Bulgari Hotel Paris last year through the hotel's own website, you paid the same nightly rate the hotel offers to walk-ins. If you booked the same five nights through a Virtuoso-credentialed travel advisor, the rate was identical — and you also received: a complimentary upgrade at check-in subject to availability, daily breakfast for two, a $100 hotel credit, early check-in and late checkout where possible, and a welcome amenity from the general manager. The B2B booking code did not raise the price. It quietly added $800 of value to a stay you were already going to book.

**Full draft**

There are three main hotel-side B2B programs that travel advisors use to book luxury and upper-upper-upscale stays. They overlap and they compete, but the principle is the same: the hotel chains and the individual properties pay a structured premium to advisors who deliver enough room-night volume to justify a relationship. The premium is paid in two currencies: commission to the advisor, and amenities to the guest.

*Virtuoso.* The largest of the three, with about 1,300 advisor agencies worldwide and 2,500 preferred hotel partners. Virtuoso membership is invitation-only. Hotels in the Virtuoso program publish a "Virtuoso rate" — often, but not always, identical to the best available rate on the hotel's website — and bundle in the Virtuoso amenity package: complimentary upgrade at check-in based on availability, daily breakfast for two, an experience credit ($100, $150, or more depending on the property), and a written welcome from the GM. The advisor books through Virtuoso's proprietary platform, which transmits the booking with a tracking code so the hotel knows the guest is a Virtuoso booking.

*Four Seasons Preferred Partner.* A smaller, Four Seasons–specific program. Advisors who are credentialed get a similar amenity package on Four Seasons stays: upgrade at check-in subject to availability, daily breakfast for two, a property amenity (usually a $100 food and beverage credit or spa credit), and complimentary early check-in and late checkout where the property can accommodate. The Preferred Partner code is entered at the booking level in the Four Seasons reservation system.

*FHR — Fine Hotels and Resorts, the Amex Platinum program.* Available to Platinum cardholders booking through Amex Travel. The amenity package includes a fourth-night-free benefit on stays of four or more nights at participating properties, a guaranteed room upgrade subject to availability at check-in, daily breakfast for two, complimentary Wi-Fi, and a unique property amenity. FHR is a strong consumer program, but it has two limitations relative to Virtuoso: it requires the booking to flow through the Amex Travel desk (not your dedicated advisor), and the property selection is narrower — about 1,700 hotels versus Virtuoso's 2,500.

*STARS — World of Hyatt's preferred-partner program.* Similar structure for Hyatt properties. Advisor codes earn amenity packages and, importantly, place the guest into the property's preferred-guest upgrade queue. STARS bookings are coded in Hyatt's CRM, which is why the booking-channel choice affects upgrade probability on arrival.

There are also smaller, brand-specific programs: Rosewood Elite, Mandarin Oriental Fan Club Prestige, Belmond Bellini Club, Aman Privé Reserve, Peninsula Preferred Privileges, Ritz-Carlton Stars, Park Hyatt Privé. Each operates on the same principle. Booking-channel choice affects what the guest receives at check-in.

There are two things most senior buyers do not know about these programs.

First, the rates are usually identical. There is a persistent misconception that "Virtuoso rates" or "Preferred Partner rates" are higher than the hotel's own website rate. They are not, in the overwhelming majority of cases. Hotels are contractually prohibited from disadvantaging the B2B channel — they want the booking, they want the volume, they want the relationship. The amenity package is an additional value layer on top of the same rate.

Second, the amenities are stackable with most hotel-loyalty status benefits. If you are a World of Hyatt Globalist and you book a stay through a STARS advisor, you get the Globalist upgrade, plus the STARS welcome amenity, plus the breakfast inclusion (which you also get as a Globalist), plus any property-specific Globalist amenity. The two channels do not cancel each other out. They compound.

The reason all of this exists is volume aggregation. A single luxury traveler books maybe ten to twenty hotel stays a year. A Virtuoso-credentialed agency books ten thousand. The hotel pays the advisor channel the amenity package and a 10 to 16 percent commission because the channel delivers volume the hotel cannot reach alone. The amenity is the marketing budget the hotel was always going to spend on you; it is just paid through a different counter.

What this means for you, practically: if you are booking five-figures of hotel spend a year and you are doing it through the hotel's website or through your card concierge, you are leaving money on the table. The number is not theoretical. On a $5,000 hotel stay at a Four Seasons property, the breakfast for two ($240 of value over four nights), the food credit ($100), and the upgrade (often a category jump worth $300 to $800 per night) can add up to $1,400 of additional value. On a year of business travel that includes ten luxury stays, the cumulative value is in the five-figure range. None of it costs you a cent extra at the booking. It costs you only the booking channel.

## Week 6 — TEB vs. LGA: the executive's runway calculus

**Title:** TEB vs. LGA: the executive's runway calculus
**Dek:** The unspoken trade-offs between Teterboro, LaGuardia, JFK, and Newark for the New York traveler.
**Hashtags:** #BusinessTravel #PrivateAviation #Aviation

**Hook**

The four New York airports — LaGuardia, JFK, Newark, and Teterboro — are not interchangeable, and the trade-offs between them are not the ones most senior people optimize for. Most travelers default to LGA for proximity, JFK for international, and Teterboro for private. The actual calculus is more nuanced, and the small differences add up to hours per year, multiple meetings missed or made, and a non-trivial annual expense delta.

**Full draft**

I should declare what this post is not. This is not a private aviation pitch. I do not sell jet hours. I book commercial tickets through Sabre at the same wholesale rates anyone else does, and I help clients buy commercial first when that is the right answer. The reason to write about Teterboro alongside LaGuardia and JFK is that for a meaningful share of senior New York travelers, the question of which airport to use is the question of how much of the trip is actually worth what they think it is worth.

*LaGuardia, the daily-grind airport.* The completed 2022 redevelopment turned LGA from a national punchline into one of the best mid-sized airports in North America. Terminal B and Terminal C are both new, with clean security lanes and reasonable lounge access (Centurion, Sky Club, United Club, Chase Sapphire). The structural advantage of LGA is access: from Midtown Manhattan, with no traffic, you are at the curb in 25 minutes. With Tuesday morning traffic, 45 minutes. Compare to JFK at 55 minutes no-traffic, 90 minutes with traffic. LGA is, almost without exception, the right answer for a domestic flight to Chicago, Atlanta, Boston, Washington, Charlotte, Denver, San Francisco, Los Angeles. The cost is that LGA's perimeter rule limits non-stops over 1,500 miles (with the Saturday and Denver exceptions), so for a flight to the West Coast you are constrained to specific carriers and times.

*JFK, the international gateway and the long-haul domestic.* JFK is where you go when you are flying internationally — Europe, Asia, the Middle East, South America — or when you are taking a premium domestic transcontinental on a carrier whose flagship product lives at JFK (Delta One, JetBlue Mint, American Flagship). The downside of JFK is the airport itself: Terminal 4 and Terminal 5 are tolerable, but Terminals 1, 7, and 8 are aging, and the ground-transport experience is the worst of the four. The trade-off is the product. A Delta One Suite from JFK to Los Angeles is a materially better seat than a Delta first-class recliner from LGA.

*Newark, the underrated option.* EWR's Terminal A is now, post-2022 redevelopment, one of the best domestic terminals in the country. For travelers based in Hoboken, Jersey City, the Upper West Side, or anywhere west of Fifth Avenue, EWR is materially closer than JFK and competitive with LGA. United's Polaris lounge is the best business-class lounge in the New York region. The structural disadvantage is the East River — for anyone east of Fifth Avenue, the bridge or tunnel adds 20 minutes that LGA does not require.

*Teterboro, the time machine.* Teterboro is a private-aviation-only airport in Bergen County, New Jersey, 12 miles from Midtown. The structural advantage is the elimination of the security and boarding overhead: you arrive 10 minutes before wheels-up, walk from the car to the aircraft, and depart on the timetable you set. The structural disadvantage is the cost. A light jet (Citation CJ3, Phenom 300) from TEB to Chicago is roughly $14,000 to $18,000 one-way for a 4–6 hour mission depending on routing. A mid-size jet (Citation XLS, Hawker 900XP) is $20,000 to $26,000. A super-mid (Challenger 350, Citation Latitude) is $26,000 to $34,000. The fully-loaded hourly rate ranges from $4,500 to $9,500 depending on the aircraft category and the operator.

The calculus on Teterboro, for senior travelers, comes down to two questions. First, how many people are you taking. A four-person executive team flying TEB to ORD on a Hawker 900 at $22,000 round trip is a $5,500 per-seat cost. The same four people in business class on United from EWR to ORD round trip is $1,600 per seat. The TEB premium is real but not as obscene as the headline number suggests when amortized across seats. Second, how time-sensitive is the trip. If you need to be in Chicago at 9 am, leave at 11 am for a New York commitment, and be back home that night, no commercial schedule supports it. Teterboro does.

A few additional small things that matter:

The Teterboro Million Air FBO has the best lounge of the three FBOs at TEB; the Meridian FBO is the most efficient on the ramp; the Signature FBO is the closest to the entry and the best when you are running late. If you are flying on Wheels Up or NetJets, you are going to whichever FBO the operator has standing arrangements with that day, but if you are chartering directly, the FBO choice affects your experience.

The LaGuardia Marine Air Terminal — the smaller terminal on the south side of the airfield, between Terminals B and C — is now the second-best secret in the New York airport system. Delta's LGA Sky Club here is materially less crowded than the Sky Club in Terminal C.

JFK Terminal 4 has a 25,000-square-foot Centurion Lounge as of late 2024, which has changed the calculus for Platinum cardholders making international connections through JFK.

The Westchester County Airport — KHPN — is a third option for travelers in Greenwich, Stamford, and the Fairfield County corridor. JetBlue and American operate from HPN. It is unlovely but extremely fast.

None of this is hot-take material. It is the kind of detail that compounds across a year of business travel into hours of time saved or wasted. The choice between LGA and EWR for a Tuesday morning flight to Atlanta is a 30-minute decision that, multiplied by 40 round-trips a year, is 40 hours.

## Week 7 — What founders get wrong about travel ops

**Title:** What founders get wrong about travel ops
**Dek:** Six patterns I see weekly. Each one is a category of decision the founder is making without realizing it.
**Hashtags:** #FounderOps #StartupOps #BusinessTravel

**Hook**

After enough conversations with founders about how they manage their own travel, the patterns become predictable. They are not mistakes in any embarrassing sense. They are the consequence of treating travel as something to be solved with an app, when it is actually a category of operating decision that compounds across the company. Six patterns, in order of how often I see them.

**Full draft**

*Pattern one: outsourcing travel to the EA without giving the EA the tools.* This is the most common pattern. The founder has hired an executive assistant, usually their first operational hire, and the EA is now responsible for "managing the calendar," which by extension includes booking flights and hotels. The EA does not have a corporate travel account, a TMC relationship, or any B2B program credentials. The EA books through Expedia, Google Flights, or the founder's Amex card via the consumer airline website. The result: the founder gets the public rate, the EA spends 90 minutes per booking, and there is no policy infrastructure for what happens when a trip changes. This is a $60,000–$80,000-a-year EA spending an hour a day on transactions a $30,000-a-year membership advisor would handle in eight minutes. The math is bad in both directions: the EA is doing the wrong work, and the founder is paying the public rate.

*Pattern two: confusing card concierge with travel ops.* Covered last month, but the short version: the Amex Platinum concierge is a reservations layer, not an operations layer. They cannot solve a missed connection in Frankfurt. They cannot get you the FHR rate stacked with your World of Hyatt Globalist upgrade. They cannot reach the hotel night manager. The founder assumes the most expensive card in their wallet comes with the most capable desk, and it does not.

*Pattern three: optimizing for the wrong unit.* Founders optimize for ticket price. The cost of business travel is not the ticket price. The cost is the time-cost of the trip not going well: the missed meeting, the lost upgrade, the four hours of customer-service phone calls after a cancellation, the $300 cab from EWR to JFK at 11 pm. A $1,800 business-class ticket that gets you to Munich rested for a 9 am board meeting is cheaper than a $640 economy ticket that has you arriving exhausted and rebooking after a cancellation. The cost-per-meeting-delivered is the right unit. The ticket-price unit is the consumer unit.

*Pattern four: not knowing the company's travel spend.* The founder cannot say, within $50,000, what the company spent on travel last year. This is true at companies up to about 100 people. The expense reports live in Brex, Ramp, Mercury, or Expensify; the company books vary by tool. The spend is real and material — for a 25-person company, often $250,000 to $600,000 a year — but it is not on a dashboard. The pattern is to find out the spend only when the board deck is being built.

*Pattern five: misallocating the policy.* "We use business class for flights over six hours" is a policy. "We use premium economy for flights over four hours" is also a policy. "We use the lowest available fare" is a policy. Most early-stage companies do not have one, which means the policy is whatever the founder did last week, and the rest of the team is guessing. The lack of a policy is not a cost-control issue. It is a fairness issue. The new hire flying coach to a customer in Dallas while the founder flies first to the same conference creates resentment that lasts longer than the trip.

*Pattern six: not pre-positioning for irregular operations.* The founder has no plan for what happens when a flight cancels. The default plan is: pull out the phone, call the airline, wait on hold, accept whatever the agent offers. A better plan is: have a single number to call that has your traveler profile loaded, your priorities understood, and the authority to rebook in three minutes. The cost of building this plan is approximately one hour during onboarding with an advisor. The benefit is collected over every disruption for the rest of the founder's career.

What all six patterns share is the assumption that travel is a transactional commodity — something to be efficiently transacted — when it is actually an operational commitment. The founders who get this right tend to make one of two decisions: either they hire an internal travel manager once the company is large enough (usually 75 to 100 people), or they outsource to a membership advisor before that. The companies that do neither spend money inefficiently and time inefficiently, and the inefficiency compounds.

The post is not about cost. It is about leverage. The founder's time, the EA's time, the team's time, the meeting that was supposed to happen, the customer who was supposed to be there. Travel is, when done well, the operational substrate beneath the deals that get done. It deserves the same care as any other operational substrate. Most founders give it less.

## Week 8 — Why your EA hates booking your travel

**Title:** Why your EA hates booking your travel
**Dek:** A note to founders, GPs, and partners who think travel is a calendar problem. It is not. It is an emotional one.
**Hashtags:** #FounderOps #ExecutiveAssistant #Delegation

**Hook**

If you are a founder or a partner whose executive assistant books your travel, I want you to take a moment and consider what the EA actually does when you ask them to "find me a flight to Chicago Tuesday morning, return Wednesday night, preferably business class." The request is one sentence to you. To the EA, it is a 45-minute block of work, a constant exposure to airline-side rejection, and a recurring source of professional anxiety they will never tell you about.

**Full draft**

I have spent a lot of time talking to executive assistants — at startups, at funds, at law firms, at family offices. The pattern is consistent. The EAs I respect most all share a quiet hatred of travel booking, and the reason is structural.

When you ask the EA to book a Tuesday flight to Chicago, they begin by opening four or five tabs: United, American, Delta, the corporate booking tool if there is one, your Amex Travel page. They check fares across all four. They cross-reference your loyalty status. They check the calendar to see if any of the times conflict with other meetings. They check your travel preferences — aisle, window, no red-eye, premium cabin over four hours. They draft a proposed itinerary, copy it into an email, and send it to you.

You reply 90 minutes later: "Can you check if there's a later flight? I want to do the morning at the office."

The EA repeats the process. The new flight is available, but it now conflicts with your wife's dinner reservation, which the EA has to gently flag. You reply: "Move the dinner." The EA emails the restaurant. The restaurant is fully booked for the new time. The EA emails your wife. Your wife is not happy. The EA navigates the not-happy.

Meanwhile, the original flight, which was on a low-availability discount fare, has now sold out. The next-available fare is $400 higher. The EA absorbs that information silently, books it, and updates the trip.

Then, on Monday at 6 pm, United emails the EA: the flight has a 90-minute schedule change. The EA reviews whether the new time works. It does not — it conflicts with your Wednesday morning call. The EA calls United. The agent says the flight can be moved to a later departure, but the later flight requires a same-day change fee. The EA, who is not on your account at United, cannot waive the fee. The EA escalates to your office's travel manager, who does not exist. The EA pays the fee on your corporate card.

On Tuesday at 4 am, you text the EA: "What gate?" The EA, who is asleep, wakes up, checks the app, replies. Then they cannot fall back asleep.

Every step of this, the EA absorbs the friction. The not-quite-right itinerary. The bumped fare. The annoyed restaurant. The annoyed spouse. The waived fee they do not have authority for. The 4 am text. None of it is large in isolation. All of it compounds across 80 to 200 trips a year.

The EA is, in this sense, doing two jobs they were not hired for. The first is dispatch: matching your time, your preferences, and your status across multiple airline and hotel inventories. This is a job that can be done well by software, when the software has access to a consolidated booking environment with B2B credentials. The EA does not have this. The EA has consumer tools.

The second is emotional buffering: protecting you from the frustrations of the booking process, and protecting the airline from you when those frustrations bleed through. This is a job that, when done well, is invisible to you. The EA does it for free, and it costs them sleep and steady attention.

When I take over a founder's travel from their EA, the EA's first reaction, almost without exception, is gratitude. Not because the EA was bad at the job — most EAs are extraordinarily good at the job. It is because the EA can now spend that 45-minute block of work on the things they were actually hired for: managing the calendar, prepping briefings, handling expense reports, running the small operational machine that makes the founder effective. The travel booking moves to a partner whose only job is travel, who has B2B credentials, who has waiver authority, who can call the airline desk and ask for a favor. The EA still receives the itineraries and confirms them. The EA still handles the spouse-and-dinner half. But the dispatch and the buffering are no longer their problem.

The pattern I want to name is this: when you delegate travel to your EA, you have not delegated travel. You have delegated the symptom. The work is still being done, and it is being done by the wrong person on the wrong tools. The EA is too senior for it, the tools are too consumer for it, and the cost is being paid in time the EA is not spending on the work you actually hired them for.

The right delegation is to someone whose only job is travel, and who has the infrastructure to do it without burning a 45-minute block on every itinerary change. The EA's job is to keep your day running. Let them do that job.

## Week 9 — The hidden cost of Concur

**Title:** The hidden cost of Concur
**Dek:** What a corporate online booking tool actually costs in time, leakage, and the quiet erosion of the traveler's day.
**Hashtags:** #TravelManagement #Concur #CFO

**Hook**

SAP Concur is the dominant corporate online booking tool. It is in approximately two-thirds of mid-market and enterprise travel programs in North America. It is also, by the time you have used it for a year as a senior traveler, the most reliable source of low-grade frustration in your professional life. The frustration is structural and worth diagnosing.

**Full draft**

Concur was built as a procurement-side tool. The buyer is a travel manager at a 2,000-person company who needs to enforce policy, track spend, capture leakage, and produce reports that satisfy the CFO. The tool's job is to make sure that when a traveler books a flight, the flight is within policy, the cost is captured in the right cost center, and the receipt makes it into the expense report. From the travel manager's perspective, Concur is excellent at its job.

From the traveler's perspective, Concur is a tax. The interface is built for a desktop browser in 2014. The search experience requires entering a lot of fields. The fare display does not surface the actual seat map or the cabin amenities. The booking confirmation arrives in three separate emails. The cancellation process requires either calling the corporate help desk, or navigating a flow that the traveler does once and then never quite remembers. The mobile experience is significantly worse than the desktop experience, which is itself significantly worse than what any consumer airline or hotel app offers.

The hidden cost is in three layers.

*Layer one: the time the senior traveler spends inside Concur.* For a senior person whose hourly economic cost is somewhere between $150 and $1,000, ten minutes spent inside Concur per booking, multiplied by 30 to 80 bookings a year, is $750 to $40,000 of fully-loaded cost the company is paying for the traveler to use the tool. The tool's stated purpose is to save the company money on travel. It does so, in aggregate, but it does so by transferring the time-cost from the travel manager (who would otherwise process the booking) to the traveler.

*Layer two: the off-channel booking leakage.* Senior travelers, faced with the friction of Concur, eventually do what humans do: they route around the tool. They book on their personal card. They use the United app. They send the EA a text and ask the EA to handle it. The booking, when it happens this way, is no longer captured in the corporate travel program. It is "leakage." Industry studies (including BCD's annual Move report and Amex GBT's traveler surveys) consistently estimate leakage at 10 to 30 percent of total travel spend, depending on the maturity of the program. Concur charges the travel manager for the tool, then loses 10 to 30 percent of the visibility into the program the tool was bought to manage.

*Layer three: the negotiated-rate degradation.* When a traveler books off-channel, they almost never receive the negotiated corporate rate the company has spent procurement cycles securing with airlines and hotels. The corporate rate is in Concur. The public rate is on the airline's website. Off-channel booking, in aggregate, costs the company the corporate-rate discount on every leaked transaction.

The total cost of Concur, to a company with $1 million in annual travel spend, is something like:

| Line item | Annual cost |
|---|---|
| Concur licensing (per-user fees) | $4,000 – $12,000 |
| Per-transaction OBT fees through the TMC | $5,000 – $15,000 |
| Senior-traveler time inside the tool | $10,000 – $40,000 |
| Leakage on off-channel bookings | $50,000 – $200,000 |
| Negotiated-rate degradation | $20,000 – $80,000 |
| **Total annual cost (fully loaded)** | **$89,000 – $347,000** |

The total cost-to-benefit calculation depends on the size of the program. For an enterprise with $20 million in travel spend, the policy enforcement and reporting benefits of Concur are material enough to justify the cost. For a mid-market company with $1 to $5 million in spend, the math is much closer. For a startup with $200,000 to $800,000 in spend, Concur is almost certainly the wrong tool — the friction cost exceeds the policy-enforcement benefit, and the program is small enough that human-mediated booking through an advisor is cheaper.

The alternative is not "no tool." The alternative is to compress the booking layer back into the human layer. A traveler emails or texts the advisor: "Tuesday morning Chicago, return Wednesday night, business class, prefer United." The advisor confirms the booking in 8 minutes, charges the company's corporate card via a virtual-card workflow, and pushes the booking confirmation into the expense system. There is no Concur tab. There is no leakage. The negotiated rate (whether the corporate rate or the advisor's B2B-channel rate) is captured by default, because there is no off-channel option.

This is not the right answer for every company. A 500-person company with a sophisticated procurement function and 200 travelers needs the policy-enforcement infrastructure Concur provides. But for the founder-led company, the small fund, the boutique advisory, the family office — the persona for whom the senior traveler is the traveler, and the policy is "do not be stupid" — the human-mediated booking layer is cheaper, faster, and produces more compliance, because there is no off-channel option to leak to.

The Concur question is the wrong question. The right question is: what tool produces the lowest fully-loaded cost across licensing, fees, traveler time, leakage, and rate-degradation. For the buyer this newsletter is written for, the answer is rarely Concur.

## Week 10 — What I'd do with $50K of T&E if I were a Series A founder

**Title:** What I would do with $50K of T&E
**Dek:** An annual travel budget for a Series A founder, line-itemed. Not aspirational, not theoretical. The actual playbook.
**Hashtags:** #FounderOps #StartupOps #TravelManagement

**Hook**

If you raised your Series A in the last 18 months and you have $50,000 a year to spend on travel — yours, plus a co-founder, plus the occasional team trip — here is what I would do with that money, line by line, in order of return on each dollar. The list is not in the order most founders think about it. It is in the order that compounds.

**Full draft**

The premise. A Series A founder, raised $10–$25M, runs a 12–25 person company, travels 25–40 times a year, has a co-founder who travels a similar amount, and budgets roughly $50,000 a year for the travel portion of T&E (separate from team-wide travel, conferences, customer dinners). The $50,000 is the founder-and-co-founder line.

Here is how I would allocate it.

*Line item 1: airline status and loyalty positioning. $0 marginal, but real.* Pick one airline alliance — Star Alliance via United, SkyTeam via Delta, or oneworld via American — and concentrate 80 percent of all flights through that alliance for one year. Earn the mid-tier elite status (Platinum on United, Platinum on Delta, Platinum on American). This is free, in the sense that it does not change the cost of any individual ticket, but it changes the per-trip experience materially: confirmed upgrades on domestic flights, lounge access on international, preferred boarding, free same-day changes, free award redemptions. The single biggest mistake Series A founders make in travel is to be promiscuous across airlines for one cheaper flight at a time, then earn no status anywhere. Discipline produces the asymmetric return.

*Line item 2: business class on long-haul. ~$18,000 of the $50,000.* If you take three to four overseas trips a year (Europe, Asia, the West Coast counts as borderline), the business-class differential is roughly $3,000 to $5,000 per round trip over premium economy. Your sleep quality and your meeting quality on landing are non-negotiable. Premium economy is a downgrade from business class in every dimension. Coach on a 7-plus-hour flight is malpractice for a Series A CEO heading to a Tier-1 customer meeting. Budget for it explicitly.

*Line item 3: domestic premium cabin on flights over 3 hours. ~$8,000 of the $50,000.* The first-class fare on a 3.5-hour New York-to-Denver flight is usually 1.5 to 2x the coach fare, not 4x. The upgrade probability with mid-tier elite status is around 30 to 50 percent on most United, Delta, and American transcontinentals — meaning roughly a third to a half of these flights upgrade for free. Budget the cash for the rest.

*Line item 4: hotel quality on critical trips. ~$10,000 of the $50,000.* For the five or six trips a year that are genuinely high-stakes (board meeting, fundraise, key customer), book the hotel that gets you to the meeting fully resting. Not the four-star down the street. The five-star three blocks from the office. The $200 or $300 differential is the cheapest insurance you will ever buy. For the other 20 to 30 trips, the Hyatt Place is fine, and you bank the Globalist nights.

*Line item 5: airport lounges and Clear. ~$1,000 of the $50,000.* Amex Platinum (or Centurion) covers Centurion, Sky Club via Delta, and Priority Pass — most lounge needs. Add Clear ($199/year, sometimes $99 with a card subsidy) for the security queue. Global Entry ($120 for five years, free with Platinum). These are nearly trivial in cost and material in time saved.

*Line item 6: the membership advisor. ~$3,600 of the $50,000.* This is the line item this publication is about, so I will be brief. The membership advisor exists to convert your 80 to 200 hours of annual travel administration into 30 minutes. The advisor's job is to book what you tell them to book, to remember your preferences so you never have to repeat them, to be the human on the other end of the line at 2 a.m. when something breaks, and to apply the B2B booking channels (Virtuoso, STARS, FHR) you cannot apply yourself. The cost is the dignity premium and the time-arbitrage premium combined.

*Line item 7: the contingency and the "do not skimp" envelope. ~$6,000 of the $50,000.* For when a flight gets cancelled and you need to take a $1,500 last-minute ticket to make a meeting. For when a hotel runs over budget because the only available room is the suite. For when a car service is the right answer and not the cab. The point of having this envelope is to remove from yourself the friction of every individual spending decision. The envelope absorbs the variability. If you do not spend it down, it rolls to the next quarter.

*Line item 8: the trip post-mortem. $0, ten minutes.* After every trip, take ten minutes to ask: what worked, what did not, what would I change. The compounding return on this practice is the highest of anything on this list, and almost no founder does it.

The total is $46,600. The remaining $3,400 is float. The list does not include leisure travel, family travel, or team off-sites — those are different budgets.

The thing this list is trying to optimize for is not money saved. The thing it is optimizing for is the founder's time, attention, and energy across a year. A Series A founder has somewhere between 70 and 90 days of meaningful customer or investor travel a year. If each of those days is the right cabin, the right room, the right ground transfer, and the right backup plan, the entire year's strategic output is materially better. If half of those days are spent on the wrong plane in the wrong seat with a missed connection and a hotel room three blocks too far away, the year's strategic output is materially worse.

The dollar cost of getting it right is somewhere between $40,000 and $55,000 a year. The dollar cost of getting it wrong is harder to measure, but easier to feel.

## Week 11 — The 60-minute rule

**Title:** The 60-minute rule
**Dek:** What service-level agreements actually mean in travel, and why the 60-minute SLA is the only one that is structurally honest.
**Hashtags:** #BusinessTravel #CustomerService #Operations

**Hook**

Every travel partner with a website claims to be available 24/7. Almost none of them publish a service-level agreement. The reason for the omission is operational: a written SLA is a written commitment, and a written commitment exposes the gap between the marketing copy and what the night desk can actually deliver. The 60-minute SLA is the only one I have found that is structurally honest.

**Full draft**

A service-level agreement is, in its simplest form, a promise about response time. In the SaaS industry, an SLA is usually expressed as uptime — 99.9 percent, 99.99 percent. In the travel industry, the equivalent is the first-response time. When a client writes in, how long until a human acknowledges the message.

The travel industry has historically avoided publishing first-response SLAs for three reasons. First, the response time depends on the channel: a phone call gets faster acknowledgment than an email. Second, the response time depends on the hour: 11 am is faster than 3 am. Third, and most importantly, the response time depends on the staffing reality, and most agencies do not staff overnight in a way that supports a sub-hour SLA.

A 60-minute first-response SLA means: within 60 minutes of a client message arriving at the desk, a human at the agency has acknowledged the message, named the next step, and committed to a resolution timeline. The acknowledgment is not the resolution. The 60 minutes is for the acknowledgment.

Why 60 minutes and not 15, or 5? Because the unit of meaningful travel decision-making is rarely faster than 60 minutes, and most travel problems are not improved by a 5-minute acknowledgment with no plan attached.

Consider a flight cancellation at 11 pm in Frankfurt. Inside the first 60 minutes:

The advisor has acknowledged the message (1 to 5 minutes if they are awake, 10 to 30 if they are not).

The advisor has pulled up the original booking and checked the airline's rebooking options through the GDS (5 to 10 minutes).

The advisor has cross-referenced the rebooking options against the client's next-day commitments — which the advisor knows because the advisor built the itinerary (5 to 10 minutes).

The advisor has called the airline's station manager or duty desk to ask about the protected fare that does not show up in the GDS (10 to 20 minutes; sometimes longer in actual disruption).

The advisor has sent the client a recommendation with one or two options, the trade-offs, and a request for the go-ahead (5 minutes).

This sequence takes 30 to 50 minutes when it runs well. The 60-minute SLA is the realistic ceiling, with some buffer for the night-of-disruption case where the airline desk has a queue.

Why an SLA shorter than 60 minutes is dishonest, in my experience: a 15-minute or 5-minute SLA implies the advisor is at the desk and available immediately, every time. The only way to staff this is to have multiple advisors covering overlapping shifts around the clock — which is what a large enterprise TMC does, by pooling the night shift across many client accounts. The cost of this staffing model is the pod model: the advisor on the night shift is not your advisor, and they do not know you or your itinerary.

A 60-minute SLA, by contrast, is honest about the trade-off. The advisor on your contract is on call. They are not staring at a screen waiting for your message. When the message arrives, they receive a notification on their phone. They wake up if it is night. They respond within 60 minutes. The acknowledgment is from the named human, not from a pool.

What the 60-minute SLA covers: the first response. What it does not cover: the resolution. A complex rebooking on a Tuesday at 2 a.m. when the airline's IT system is in a soft fail state can take three hours to resolve. The 60-minute SLA does not promise resolution in 60 minutes. It promises that the client knows, within 60 minutes, that the advisor is on the problem.

What this looks like in practice. A client texts at 11:14 pm: "Flight just cancelled, on the way to baggage claim." At 11:31 pm, the advisor texts back: "Got it. Pulling rebooking options now. Stay near gate A26, I'll have an answer by midnight." At 11:54 pm, the advisor texts again: "Lufthansa 401 tomorrow at 9:30 am gets you to Munich at 11:15, which keeps your noon meeting. Hotel tonight: held a room at the Hyatt Place FRA, walk-in distance from arrivals. Confirm and I'll book." At 11:56, the client confirms. At 12:04 am, the advisor sends the rebooking and the hotel confirmation. Total time from disruption to resolution: 50 minutes. First-response time: 17 minutes.

The published SLA is 60. The delivered response is usually faster. This is the standard.

The reason I write about this is that almost every prospective client I speak with assumes the 24/7 marketing language they have seen on competitor websites carries the same meaning. It does not. The 24/7 desk at most large TMCs is staffed by a rotating night pool with no SLA, no named advisor, and no obligation to act within a specific window. The contract usually says only that the desk is "available." Availability is not a commitment. Availability is a phone number.

A first-response SLA is a commitment. The 60-minute mark is the threshold where the commitment is structurally honest — where the named human, on call, can reliably deliver. Anything shorter is the pod model. Anything longer is not 24/7.

## Week 12 — How to read a hotel rate code

**Title:** How to read a hotel rate code
**Dek:** A short glossary of the alphabet soup that determines what you actually receive at check-in.
**Hashtags:** #LuxuryTravel #HotelIndustry #BusinessTravel

**Hook**

The five-letter code on your hotel reservation confirmation determines, more than any single piece of information you receive, what kind of room you get, what amenities are bundled, what the cancellation policy is, and how the property will treat you on arrival. Most senior travelers have never noticed the code. It is, however, the most consequential string of characters on the document.

**Full draft**

A hotel reservation, when it is made through the hotel's central reservation system or through a global distribution system like Sabre or Amadeus, carries a rate code. The code is usually three to seven characters. It tells the hotel which rate plan the reservation was made under, which determines pricing, inclusions, cancellation, and — implicitly — the priority the reservation gets in upgrade queues and special-request workflows.

A brief, non-exhaustive glossary of codes you will commonly see, broken down by category.

*Public retail rates.* These are the rates available to anyone with an internet connection.

- **BAR / RAC / RACK.** Best Available Rate, also called rack rate. The hotel's standard published rate. No discount, no inclusions, fully flexible cancellation. This is what a walk-in pays.
- **AAA / CAA.** AAA or CAA discount, typically 5–10 percent off BAR, with the same cancellation terms.
- **AARP / SENIOR.** Senior-citizen discount, similar shape.
- **GOV / GOVT.** Government per-diem rate, typically capped by the GSA per-diem schedule.
- **MIL / MILITARY.** Military rate, often substantial discount but tightly verified.

*Loyalty rates.* These require the booker to be a member of the hotel's loyalty program.

- **MEMBR / MEMBER.** Member rate, usually 5 percent off BAR for loyalty members, often the lowest publicly-bookable rate.
- **HONORS / HHONORS.** Hilton Honors discount.
- **HYR / WOH.** Hyatt member rate (older Gold Passport vs. newer World of Hyatt code).
- **BONVOY / MR.** Marriott Bonvoy member rate.

*Negotiated corporate rates.* These require a contract between the hotel (or chain) and the company booking.

- **CORP / CORP1 / CORP2.** Negotiated corporate rate, sometimes capped by company name. The discount varies by contract but is often 8–15 percent off BAR, with negotiated inclusions.
- **NEGOT.** Generic negotiated rate.
- **TMC / SBT.** Travel-management-company-negotiated rate, available only when booked through the TMC's channel.

*Consortia and B2B advisor codes.* These are the codes that quietly add the amenity packages described in Week 5.

- **VIRT / V1A.** Virtuoso. Adds the Virtuoso amenity package — upgrade subject to availability, breakfast, property amenity credit, welcome from GM.
- **FHR / RP1.** Fine Hotels and Resorts (Amex Platinum). Adds the FHR amenity package and, on stays of 4+ nights, the fourth-night-free benefit.
- **FSPP.** Four Seasons Preferred Partner. Adds the Four Seasons-specific amenity package.
- **STARS.** Hyatt's preferred-partner program code. Adds the World of Hyatt-specific amenity package.
- **AMR / AME.** Amex Travel program rate (similar to but not identical to FHR).
- Brand-specific codes: **ROSE** for Rosewood Elite, **AMAN** for Aman Privé Reserve, **BELM** for Belmond Bellini Club, **PEN** for Peninsula Preferred Privileges, **MO PP** for Mandarin Oriental Fan Club Prestige.

*Promotional and package rates.* These are rate plans that bundle inclusions and impose restrictions.

- **AP / ADV / ADVNC.** Advance Purchase rate, usually non-refundable, 10–20 percent off BAR.
- **PKG.** Generic package rate. May include breakfast, parking, or other inclusions; cancellation terms vary.
- **SUITE.** Suite-specific package rate, usually with inclusions valued at $50–$200 per night.

*Group and block rates.* These are rate plans tied to a specific group booking.

- **GRP / BLOCK.** Group rate, available only with the group's confirmation code. Cancellation terms are typically restricted.
- **CITP.** Citywide group rate (conferences, conventions).

What this matters for, practically: the code determines what you receive at check-in. A reservation booked under BAR sees the front desk agent treat the guest as a standard arrival. A reservation booked under STARS sees the front desk agent see, in the property's PMS, a flag indicating the guest is a preferred-partner booking, which routes the reservation into the upgrade queue and triggers the property's STARS amenity workflow. A reservation booked under VIRT does the same, with a different amenity workflow.

A few specific implications:

When you call the hotel to ask about an upgrade, the answer the front desk gives depends in part on the code on the reservation. A VIRT or STARS or FSPP reservation is more likely to be flagged for upgrade than a BAR or AARP reservation, regardless of how loyal you are to the brand.

When you stack a loyalty status on top of a B2B code — World of Hyatt Globalist plus STARS, for example — both the Globalist benefits and the STARS amenities apply. The codes do not cancel each other out; they layer.

When you book through the hotel's website, you are almost never booking under a B2B code. You are booking under BAR, MEMBR, or AP. The B2B codes require a B2B channel — a Virtuoso advisor, a Four Seasons Preferred Partner advisor, an FHR booking through Amex Travel.

When you book through Hotels.com or Expedia, you are booking under a different category entirely: the OTA rate plan, which the hotel often treats with the lowest priority on the property's upgrade and amenity queues. The discount is sometimes meaningful, but the property-treatment differential almost always is.

The code is on the confirmation email. Look for a three-to-seven-character string near the rate. If it says BAR or MEMBR, you have the room and the rate, and that is all. If it says VIRT or STARS or FSPP, you have the room, the rate, and the layer of value that the booking channel quietly applied without changing the price.

If you do not see a code at all, the reservation may have been booked through a wholesaler or an OTA, in which case you have the room, the rate, and almost certainly the worst treatment at the property.

The codes are not advertised because they are inside-baseball. They are nevertheless determinative. The hotel knows what you booked under. Whether you do, too, is the difference between a transaction and a relationship.
