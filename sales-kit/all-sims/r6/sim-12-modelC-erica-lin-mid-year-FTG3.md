# Sim 12 — Model C ($99/$249/$499) · FTG3 (30-day money-back) · Stage 4 Mid-Year Check-in

**Buyer:** Erica Lin, partner, 17-person Boston M&A boutique. Atlas Office ($499/mo) six months in. 5 of 14 trips through Latitude. EA Diane defaulting to Concur.
**Rep:** Cole (founder).
**Setting:** 30-min Zoom, Tuesday 2:00pm ET, mid-May.

---

## Section 2 — Sales/CS Coach Evaluation

**Outcome:** Save at $249. ARR drops from $5,988 to $2,988 (-50%). LTV trajectory likely improves because the customer is now correctly tiered and the workflow problem is being addressed. Net read: a good save, not a great one — the great one would have included a path back to Office.

**What worked, specifically:**

1. *"I pulled your usage before this call and I'd rather just put the number on the table than dance around it."* Cole led with the disconfirming evidence. In a low-usage check-in, the buyer has already done this math; pretending otherwise costs trust. Operator-correct, confessional register, exactly the brand.

2. *"That's not a Diane problem. That's a workflow problem we didn't solve at onboarding."* Taking the failure. This is the single most important line in the transcript. It reframes the call from "are you going to keep paying" to "what did we miss." Erica's posture visibly shifts after this.

3. The two-of-five-trips question. Cole asked the buyer to underwrite the value herself rather than asserting it. Her own answer — "two were worth it, one was neutral, two weren't" — became the rationale for the downgrade. The buyer convinced the buyer.

4. Naming Basic and recommending against it. This is a costly signal that works precisely because it costs something. A rep optimizing for ARR would not have mentioned $99. By naming it and rejecting it on her behalf, Cole demonstrated that he's optimizing for fit, which is the only durable retention move.

5. The FTG3 handling. Cole did not pretend the 30-day window was still live. He acknowledged it was closed, refused to insult her with a fake refund, and synthesized a smaller, honest version (30-day re-grade on the downgrade itself). This is the right move with a sophisticated buyer six months in. FTG3 was structurally past-tense here; the rep treated it as past-tense.

**What broke:**

1. Cole never tested whether there's a version of Office that works. The downgrade path was presented as inevitable once usage was named. A stronger move would have been: "before we step you down, let me ask — if Diane were routing the New York and Chicago trips to us by default starting next week, does $499 work?" The workflow fix and the tier decision were entangled and Cole resolved them sequentially when he could have tested them together.

2. No referral ask. Erica is a partner at a Boston M&A boutique. She knows fifteen other partners at fifteen other boutiques. The downgrade conversation is actually the highest-trust moment of the relationship — she just watched the founder leave $3K of ARR on the table to do right by her. That's the moment to say "if this lands well for you, the firms I want to talk to are the ones that look like yours." Cole missed it.

3. The 15-minute Diane call should have been booked on the call, not promised. "I'll set up time with Diane" becomes "I'll get to it." Calendar friction will kill the workflow fix that justifies the save.

**Three improvements:**

1. **Test the workflow fix before the tier change.** "Before we talk price, let me ask what changes if Diane defaults to us on multi-leg and international." Resolve the operational question first; let the tier follow.

2. **Book the EA call on the live call.** Open the calendar, pick a slot, send the invite while Erica is still on Zoom. Friction kills follow-through.

3. **Make the referral ask in the save moment.** The downgrade conversation is the trust peak, not the trough. Use it.

**Most important behavioral change for this rep:** Cole defaults to honesty as a closing move. It works. But honesty without the operational follow-through (the EA call, the default routing, the referral) leaves money and momentum on the table. The discipline to install is *finish the operational loop on the call* — don't let "I'll send something over" be the last sentence.

## Section 3 — Business Evaluator

**Unit economics at this conversion.** Erica went from $5,988 ARR to $2,988 ARR. On the surface this is a 50% revenue cut. Underneath, the picture is more complicated. At her actual usage — roughly one real-design trip per month — Atlas Office at $499 was carrying significant negative gross margin contribution from her perception: she was overpaying and she knew it. The probability of silent churn at the 12-month mark was, conservatively, 70%+. Stepping her to $249 with an aligned workflow probably moves 12-month retention probability from ~30% to ~75%. Expected ARR over 24 months under the old tier: $5,988 × 0.30 × 2 = $3,593. Expected ARR under the new tier: $2,988 × 0.75 × 2 = $4,482. The downgrade is a net +$889 over 24 months, before referral value.

**Strategic positioning learnings.** The three-tier ladder did something the two-tier model structurally cannot: it gave the rep a *meaningful middle*. The $499 → $249 step is a 50% price cut that still includes the feature Erica actually values (design on member trips). If Latitude had been on Model A (two tiers: $99/$249), the downgrade from $249 would have been to $99 Basic, which doesn't include design — a feature drop, not just a price drop. Erica would have either stayed at $249 grudgingly or churned to zero. If Latitude had been on Model B (Unlimited, $349 top), the downgrade conversation would have been the same shape but with less price headroom — only a $160 step. The three-tier ladder is structurally the best architecture for mid-year saves at the top tier specifically because it preserves the value-bearing feature across the step.

**However** — and this is the strategic cost — the three-tier ladder is structurally *worst* for the cold-inbound conversation (Sim 3 territory). The middle tier creates analysis paralysis at acquisition. Model C wins on retention flexibility and loses on acquisition simplicity. The interesting strategic question is whether the LTV gain from better retention saves outweighs the CAC penalty from acquisition friction. With Erica's segment (partner-tier professional services), the answer is almost certainly yes. With the founder-segment (Sim 3), it's not obvious.

**Retention probability.** Estimated 75% at 12 months from this conversation, conditional on the EA workflow call happening within seven days and the default-routing rules being installed. If the EA call slips past two weeks, retention probability drops to ~50% — Diane will keep defaulting to Concur, usage won't change, and the next check-in is a cancellation. The save is operationally fragile.

**Referral value.** Unrealized but potentially the highest-value asset on the call. Boston M&A boutique partners are a tight, talkative segment. A satisfied Erica is worth 2-4 warm intros over 18 months. At an estimated 30% close rate and $2,988-$5,988 ARR per close, the referral pipeline from this single save is worth $1,800-$7,200 of incremental ARR. Cole's failure to make the referral ask in the trust peak is the single largest economic miss in the transcript.

**The hidden risk.** Erica's "I'll tell you in October whether Frankfurt was worth it" is the actual renewal date. The downgrade bought six months of runway, not twelve. If the Frankfurt trip doesn't happen, or happens and isn't a clear win, the next check-in is the real cancellation conversation. The save is contingent on a specific future trip going well — which Latitude only partially controls. The CS function needs to flag Frankfurt as a tier-1 trip and over-resource it. If it goes badly, the $2,988 ARR is gone and the referral pipeline goes with it.

## Section 4 — Model + FTG Scorecard

**Did the price land or stick?** The $499 didn't stick — it shouldn't have, given usage. The $249 landed cleanly. Specific friction moment: Erica's "is stepping down the polite way to break up with you?" — that's the real objection, and it surfaced *because* the middle tier exists. In a two-tier model that question never gets asked; the buyer just cancels. Score: **8/10** for the model's ability to surface and resolve the real objection mid-conversation.

**Did the FTG mechanic move the buyer?** FTG3 (30-day money-back) was structurally past-tense — five months past expiration. Cole correctly refused to fake it and synthesized a smaller version (30-day re-grade window on the downgrade). The original FTG3 was inert at this stage; the *adapted* version did meaningful work. Score: **5/10** for FTG3 as designed; **8/10** for the rep's adaptation. FTG3's design assumes the conversation happens inside 30 days. At month six, it has no native role.

**Would the buyer have bought under a different model?** At the *initial* sale six months ago, almost certainly yes under any model. At *this* moment — the mid-year save — Model C is uniquely well-suited. Model A's downgrade ($249→$99) drops the design feature she values; Model B's downgrade ($349→$189) is a smaller absolute step and may not feel like a meaningful save. The $499→$249 step is the largest absolute-dollar save that preserves the feature.

**Net read:** Model C is the *best retention architecture* of the three at the top tier specifically because the middle rung is feature-complete. It makes the downgrade conversation **EASIER**, not harder — the additional decision is offset by the existence of a non-humiliating middle option. The $499 → $249 step feels meaningfully like a save because it's 50% off without losing the headline feature. The risk Model C carries (decision-fatigue, acquisition friction) lives upstream at Stage 1, not here. **Best-fit model for Stage 4 save conversations: Model C. Best-fit FTG for this stage: not FTG3 — FTG1's labor credit or FTG2's service credit would have given the rep live ammunition. FTG3 expires before the save conversation exists.**
