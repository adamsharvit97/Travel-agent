## Section 2 — Sales / Operations Coach Evaluation (~700 words)

**Outcome:** Retention preserved, not strengthened. Member exits the call calmer than he entered, gets to ATL with a 49-minute buffer to a 10am meeting (tight but viable), and receives a single-thread text recap at 3:05am. The "that's a real answer" line is the only positive emotional marker in the entire transcript. Everything else is transactional. Net: this is a B-, not an A. The member will fly, the member will pitch, the member will not churn this week — but the member is now consciously aware that Latitude 43 is fallible at 2am, and that awareness has a half-life.

**What worked, specifically.**

*Line: "I have your file open. Pitch is Tuesday 10am, Buckhead, you'd flagged it as Tier 1 in standing instructions."* — This is the single best move in the call. Inside 15 seconds Joana proves she knows who he is, what the trip is for, and what his priority hierarchy is. This is what $249/mo is supposed to buy and it bought it. Standing-instructions language is on-brand, operator-correct, no banned words.

*Line: "Three live paths."* — Presenting the option set before recommending is correct operator behavior. It gives the member the illusion of control during a moment when he has none, and it lets Joana steer to the right answer ("I'm pushing on the American") without ordering him.

*Line: "I did not use the design escalator on this — same-day reroutes don't trigger a design fee, this is membership-included rebooking. You won't see a Latitude line item."* — Critical. Theo is on Model A where the $250-$5,000 design escalator exists. At 2:47am, anxious, a member could absolutely assume "this rebook is going to cost me a fee on top of the ticket." Joana preempts that worry without being asked. This is the v2.0 design-fee structure intruding on operational decision-making in the right way — Joana has internalized that the escalator does *not* apply to IROP rework and she said so out loud. That is exactly the operator-correct register.

*Line: "Call it four minutes I owe you."* — The single most important moment in the call. Confessional > triumphalist, exactly as the brand guide requires. She didn't deflect to "Delta's system was slow" (true but irrelevant). She named her own four minutes of suboptimal decision-making. This is what bought the retention.

**What broke.**

1. **The four minutes were real.** B6 connection through BOS is a basic same-day reroute check. At 2:47am with a Tier 1 pitch flag on the file, the AA 6:25 should have been identified inside 90 seconds. The honesty was good; the underlying error was avoidable.

2. **Front-of-plane failure flagged but not solved in the moment.** Joana correctly escalated the vendor-depth issue to the founder's desk, but the member is now flying main cabin to a $100M+ pitch meeting. The right move would have been an offer of an in-airport lounge access voucher (Centurion at LGA opens at 5am) as a *gesture during the call*, not just a post-mortem note.

3. **No language around the membership itself.** Joana never said "this is the kind of night that's why you're a member." She let the work speak. In an IROP, the work should speak — but in the *follow-up* (Andre's Wednesday call), the membership ROI conversation must happen explicitly. The risk: Theo metabolizes this incident as "Latitude was okay" rather than "Latitude saved my pitch."

**Three improvements.**

1. **Reroute-decision SLA at 90 seconds.** Joana should have a pre-built decision tree for top-3 same-day East Coast hub reroutes that resolves in under 90 seconds. Build it as a Notion playbook this week.

2. **Lounge / comfort gesture inside the call, not after.** When premium inventory fails, offer a Day Pass or a $50 Cibo Express credit within the call. Cost: $59. Retention value: meaningful.

3. **Membership-anchored sign-off.** Last line of every IROP call should be a single sentence that anchors the work to the $249. Not boastful — operator-correct. "This is what the desk is for." Done.

**The most important behavioral change for this rep:** Joana's instinct to own the four minutes was exactly right and rare. The behavior to harden is *combining* that honesty with a forward-leaning operational gesture in the same breath. Confess and compensate. Right now she confesses and stops. That's 70% of the move.

## Section 3 — Business Evaluator (~700 words)

**Unit economics at this transaction.**

Theo pays $249/mo, $2,988/yr ARR. Marginal cost of this 14-minute IROP at PHT loaded rate (~$11/hr fully burdened) is ~$2.57 of labor, plus Andre's eventual 20-minute Wednesday call at ~$80/hr loaded = ~$26.67. Total incident cost to Latitude 43: ~$29. Member spent $847 on the ticket (pass-through, not Latitude revenue). No design fee was triggered because Model A v2.0 correctly carves IROP rework out of the $250-$5,000 escalator — this is an underappreciated structural choice. Under a model where IROP work *did* trigger a fee, the call would have ended with a price disclosure that would have eroded trust at the exact wrong moment. Model A's escalator design protects the operator from itself in crisis.

Contribution margin on this account at current run-rate: assuming 6 IROPs/year of this severity, ~$174 in incident cost against $2,988 ARR = 94% gross margin pre-allocation of fixed founder/ops cost. The account is profitable even with crisis density.

**Strategic positioning learnings.**

*The Model A $249 price point is delivering value at this crisis moment, but only just.* Theo got: standing instructions recall in 15 seconds, three options presented, ticketing, ground re-sequencing, calendar diplomacy, single-thread recap. That is genuinely worth $249/mo to a Series B founder whose alternative is doing this himself at 2:47am while packing. However — the member did not exit the call thinking "$249 is a steal." He exited thinking "this is what I paid for." That's parity, not delight. Model A pricing creates parity-retention, not advocacy-retention.

*The Model A v2.0 design-fee escalator did not affect this call's decision-making, and that's a feature, not a bug.* Joana correctly internalized that IROP rebooking sits outside the escalator. She said so out loud. The escalator is, in this case, a non-event — which is the right outcome. The risk is the *next* call where the work blurs between "rebooking" and "redesign" (e.g., what if Theo had asked her to also reshuffle Wednesday's dinner and pre-pitch breakfast — does that touch the escalator?). The boundary is fuzzy. A clearer internal definition is needed.

*FTG3 is closed and that changes everything about the retention math.* The 30-day money-back window closed 19 days ago. Theo cannot exit cleanly. That means: (a) if this incident had been mishandled badly, Latitude would still keep the November payment — but would lose the December decision; (b) FTG3's design protects the *first* month and provides zero structural risk reversal at the moment of greatest crisis density (months 2-3, when new members hit their first real IROP). This is a meaningful gap. FTG1's labor credit ($1,000) would have been more responsive here; FTG2's $500 service credit even more so. **FTG3 is the wrong instrument for this stage of the member journey.**

**Retention probability.** 78%. He stays through Q1 because the work was good enough and switching cost is non-trivial. He does not become a referral source until incident #2 is handled flawlessly. If incident #2 is also a B-, churn probability in month 4-5 rises to ~40%.

**Referral value.** Currently zero. Series B founders refer when they brag, and Theo will not brag about a 14-minute IROP where he was told "I owe you four minutes." He'll respect it. He won't repeat it. To unlock referral, Latitude needs one *visibly* heroic IROP — front-of-plane secured, lounge waiting, Blacklane in the right spot — that becomes a story. Tonight was not that story.

**The hidden risk.** Theo now has a calibrated baseline for what Latitude can and cannot do at 2am. He knows the desk is real, he knows it's fallible, he knows the rep will be honest about her own errors. This is *good* for short-term retention and *bad* for ceiling on willingness-to-pay. When Model B Unlimited ($349) is offered to him at renewal, he will benchmark it against tonight's experience and ask: "What does the extra $100/mo actually buy me at 2am?" If the answer isn't a structural upgrade in IROP quality (premium-inventory vendor depth, dedicated overnight ops), he will not trade up. **Tonight's competence ceiling caps tomorrow's price ceiling.** That is the strategic cost of B- crisis handling at the entry tier.

## Section 4 — Model + FTG Scorecard (~300 words)

**Did the price land or stick?** Landed at parity, not delight. The $249 was *earned* by the standing-instructions recall, the option-set framing, and the design-fee-doesn't-apply preemption. **Did the FTG mechanic move the buyer?** No. FTG3 was structurally absent from this transaction — the 30-day window closed 19 days ago and Joana correctly never invoked it. The internal post-mortem flagged this gap explicitly: FTG3 provides zero risk reversal at the moment of highest crisis density (months 2-3). For a member 7 weeks in, FTG3 is a sunk guarantee. Decisive? No. Helpful? No. Ignored? Correctly, yes.

**Would the buyer have bought under a different model?**
- **Model B Unlimited ($349):** Theo would have bought it at sign-up *if* the pitch had emphasized IROP density and overnight ops depth. Post-incident, he will resist the trade-up unless tonight's gaps (front-of-plane, 4-minute hesitation) are visibly closed.
- **Model C three-tier ($499 Office):** Overpriced for what Theo received tonight. He would have churned by month 4 under C.
- **Different FTG:** FTG1's $1,000 labor credit would have given Andre's Wednesday call a tangible gesture to offer ("we're crediting $250 of desk time against your next trip design"). FTG2's $500 service credit, similar. FTG3 provides nothing to offer post-window.

**Net read: which model variant best fit this buyer/stage?** **Model A pricing + FTG1 mechanic.** Keep the $249 entry — it's the right anchor for a Series B founder testing the desk. Replace FTG3 with FTG1 so that month-2 and month-3 crisis events have a structural retention instrument. Model A's design-fee carve-out for IROP is a quiet strength; protect it. The price held, the guarantee was the wrong tool, the operator told the truth — and that last fact is the only reason this account is still alive at 3:01am.
