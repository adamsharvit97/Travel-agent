# Sim 13 — Model A (v2.0 $99/$249) + FTG2

**Stage 5 — Price objection. Buyer: Marcus Voiland, 36, founder of a 9-person creative consulting firm in Denver. $55K T&E. Warm intro from a YPO peer.**

---

## Section 1: TRANSCRIPT

**Founder:** Marcus, before we get to price — let me make sure I heard the use case right. Nine people, you're the main traveler, your number two travels maybe a third as much, and the rest of the firm flies maybe twice a year each. T&E roughly $55K. Is that the shape?

**Marcus:** That's the shape. I'm probably 60% of it. My ops lead, Priya, books for herself and sometimes the junior team. I hate that she's doing that. She bills out at $185 an hour and she's pricing Southwest flights at 11pm.

**Founder:** Yeah. That's the number that actually matters in this conversation — not the membership fee, the cost of Priya doing the wrong work. We'll come back to it. Let me give you the price so you can react to it honestly, and then we'll figure out which side of the line you're on. There are two ways to come in. Atlas Light is $99 a month, one named traveler, basic booking, design fees apply on the trip itself with a member discount. Atlas Office is $249 a month, up to five active travelers, includes what we call the EA Companion Seat — that's a real seat for Priya so she's not the bottleneck, she's the routing layer. Same desk, same SLA, same standing instructions.

**Marcus:** *(pause)* Honestly? $249 a month for Atlas Office feels like a lot for me. I'm a 9-person firm. I was expecting maybe a hundred.

**Founder:** Yeah. I hear that. Let me not flinch on it and let me not bulldoze it either. Can I ask you something first — when you say "a lot," is it a lot relative to your T&E budget, or a lot relative to what you thought a travel service should cost?

**Marcus:** *(laughs)* Both, probably. But mostly the second one. I had a number in my head and you came in at two-and-a-half times it.

**Founder:** Fair. So the hundred-dollar version exists. That's Atlas Light. And I want to talk you into it or out of it based on what's actually true about your firm, not based on what gets you to yes faster. Can I do that?

**Marcus:** Please.

**Founder:** Atlas Light is one named traveler. That's you. Priya can't open the desk on her own — she'd have to either route through you or have you initiate. Design fees apply to the trip itself: zero under $5K trip cost, then an escalator from $250 up to $5,000 for the complex stuff, and members get 50% off that. So a $7,000 trip — Denver to London, two cities, four nights — design fee at the member rate is somewhere around $250. You pay that on top of the $99.

**Marcus:** Okay. So if I do, what, six real trips a year over five grand, that's maybe fifteen hundred in design fees plus twelve hundred in membership. Twenty-seven hundred a year.

**Founder:** Roughly. And if half your trips are under $5K — domestic, one-city, two nights — those design fees go to zero. So your real annual range on Light is probably $1,500 to $3,000 depending on trip mix.

**Marcus:** And Office at $249 is $3,000 flat plus design fees still?

**Founder:** Office is $3,000 flat, design fees still apply on the same schedule, members get the same 50% off. The math gets closer than people think — but the real difference isn't the dollars, it's two things. First, Priya gets her own seat. She opens the desk, she carries the standing instructions, she's not waiting on you to forward an email at 9pm. Second, up to five travelers means when your senior associate flies to a client pitch in Chicago, she's on the same desk with the same context.

**Marcus:** *(pause)* So you're telling me Light is real, it works, it's a hundred bucks. But the thing I actually want is Office.

**Founder:** I'm telling you that's the honest read of what you described. You said you hate that Priya is booking Southwest at 11pm. Light doesn't fix that. Light gives *you* a better travel experience. Office fixes the operations problem you opened the call with.

**Marcus:** Yeah. *(beat)* But $249 still stings.

**Founder:** I know. Let me put one more thing on the table and then you tell me where you are. We have something called the First Trip Guarantee. On Office, here's how it works: we name one trip on this call — your first real trip, you pick it — and if it fails on our end, not on the airline's, not on weather, but on us — we refund the month's membership, we give you the next month free, and we credit $500 of service credit toward a future trip. The whole thing. You're net positive if we screw up.

**Marcus:** *(longer pause)* That's... unusually generous. What's the catch?

**Founder:** The catch is we have to actually be good at this. The guarantee only costs us money if we fail. So we built the operation to not fail. Sixty-minute SLA on weekday business hours, founder on evenings and weekends, the desk knows you by the second trip. The guarantee is real because the operation is real. It's not a marketing line — it's how I sleep at night having charged a 9-person firm $3,000 a year.

**Marcus:** And that applies to Office, not Light?

**Founder:** Applies to both. But the math is different. On Light, if we fail, you get $99 back, next month free, and $500 in credit. On Office, you get $249 back, next month free, $500 in credit. The structure is the same; the dollar exposure tracks the membership.

**Marcus:** Okay. So the guarantee is symmetrical, it just scales. *(beat)* Let me push back on something. The guarantee is great if you fail. But I'm not worried about you failing — your YPO guy raved about you. I'm worried about *me* not using it enough to justify $249.

**Founder:** That's the right thing to worry about. That's actually a better objection than the price one. Can I tell you what I think the honest answer is?

**Marcus:** Yeah.

**Founder:** If Priya doesn't get a seat — if you don't pull her off booking — Office is overpriced for you. The whole value of Office is the operations leverage. If you start on Light and you're the only one using it, you'll get a lot of value from the design side and probably break even on the membership, but you won't get the thing you actually came here for. Which is your number two stops booking flights at 11pm.

**Marcus:** Right.

**Founder:** So here's the question I'd ask you to sit with for about ten seconds. If Priya saves five hours a month not doing travel, at $185 billable, that's $925. Office costs $249. That's a 3.7x return on the membership before we book a single trip well. The question isn't whether Office is worth it — the math on Priya alone clears it. The question is whether you trust that she'll actually use the seat, or whether she'll keep defaulting to Concur because it's familiar.

**Marcus:** *(quiet for a moment)* Okay, that's the actual question. Yeah. She'd default to Concur for the first month, easy.

**Founder:** Yeah. So here's what I'd offer. We onboard Priya in week one, on a call with me, not just you. I walk her through how she opens the desk, what counts as a standing instruction, how she escalates. I get her to forward me one in-flight booking request she'd otherwise do herself, in the first two weeks. If she doesn't, I call you. And the First Trip Guarantee is the safety net underneath all of that — if your first trip is a miss on our end, you're net positive and we'll have learned something.

**Marcus:** *(pause)* So the FTG isn't really about the trip failing. It's about giving me a way to commit at $249 without it feeling like a leap.

**Founder:** That's the honest version, yeah. The guarantee is a trust signal. The real ROI is Priya's hours. If you don't believe Priya will use the seat, take Light, save $150 a month, and we'll talk in six months. If you believe she will — and I think she will if we onboard her properly — Office is the right answer and the guarantee means you have a free out if I'm wrong.

**Marcus:** Let me ask one more thing. Is there a version where I start on Light and upgrade if it's working?

**Founder:** There is. And I'll tell you why I'd push back on it. If you start on Light, Priya doesn't get a seat. She doesn't change her behavior. You don't get the data point you're actually trying to get, which is "does this fix the operations problem." You'll get six months of "Marcus had a better travel experience" and then we'll have the same conversation. The First Trip Guarantee is designed to let you commit to Office without it being a leap. That's its job.

**Marcus:** *(beat)* Okay. Office. With the guarantee. Name the trip — I've got Denver to Lisbon end of June, four nights, my wife is joining for two of them.

**Founder:** Done. That's your named trip. I'll send the agreement today, we'll get Priya on a call Thursday or Friday, and I'll personally run the Lisbon trip. If we miss on it, you know what happens.

**Marcus:** Yeah. Let's do it.

## Section 2: SALES COACH EVALUATION

**Outcome:** Closed Office at $249/mo with FTG2 attached. Marcus moved from "expecting a hundred" to signing the higher tier in a single call. Net read: founder held price by reframing the unit of value from "membership fee" to "operations leverage on Priya's billable hours."

**What worked specifically:**

The line *"is it a lot relative to your T&E budget, or a lot relative to what you thought a travel service should cost?"* was the most important move in the call. It diagnosed the objection instead of negotiating against it. Marcus's "both, mostly the second one" gave the founder permission to reset the reference frame rather than discount the price. That single question saved the deal.

The second critical move was the willingness to *talk Marcus into or out of Light honestly.* "I want to talk you into it or out of it based on what's actually true about your firm, not based on what gets you to yes faster." This is confessional register at its best. It put the founder on Marcus's side of the table, not across from him. From that point on, Marcus was buying with the founder, not from him.

The third was the Priya math. *"$925 in saved billable hours against $249 in membership — 3.7x return before we book a trip well."* This converted an emotional price reaction into an arithmetic decision. Crucially, the founder didn't lead with the math — he led with the operations problem Marcus had named in his own words ("Priya is booking Southwest at 11pm"). The math arrived as confirmation, not pitch.

FTG2 was delivered correctly: as a trust signal, not a discount. The founder explicitly named the mechanic ("refund the month, free month, $500 credit") and then immediately reframed it: *"The guarantee only costs us money if we fail. So we built the operation to not fail."* That reframe protected the price. A weaker rep would have used FTG2 to absorb the objection ("look, if it doesn't work you get all your money back, so really you're risking nothing") which would have signaled that the price was negotiable. The founder used it as a confidence move instead.

**What broke (or nearly broke):**

The price reveal was a touch transactional. *"There are two ways to come in. Atlas Light is $99... Atlas Office is $249..."* The founder gave the menu before earning the right to. A stronger sequence would have anchored the operations problem first — gotten Marcus to articulate the cost of Priya doing the work — and then delivered price. The "$249 feels like a lot" reaction was partially manufactured by the order of delivery.

Second, the founder almost let the "start on Light and upgrade" path open up. He recovered, but for two beats Marcus had a graceful downgrade lane. In a weaker sim that's where the deal slips to $99 and never comes back. The recovery — *"you'll get six months of 'Marcus had a better travel experience' and then we'll have the same conversation"* — was good, but the lane shouldn't have appeared.

Third, the founder didn't name the disqualify-up option explicitly enough. The honest version of this call includes: "if Priya won't change her behavior, neither tier is right — you should keep doing what you're doing." That line wasn't said. It would have made the close even cleaner because it would have removed every remaining trace of "you're selling me."

**Three improvements:**

1. **Lead with the operations cost before the price.** Get Marcus to put a dollar number on Priya's wasted hours before naming $249. Anchor high, then the price feels like a discount on the operations problem.
2. **Don't open the Light-as-trial lane.** When Marcus asked "is there a version where I start on Light and upgrade," the answer is "no, and here's why" — which the founder eventually got to, but the question shouldn't have been entertained as a real option for three exchanges.
3. **Name the walk-away.** "If Priya won't use the seat, don't buy either tier. Save your money." This makes the close inevitable for the right buyers and disqualifies the wrong ones cleanly.

**Most important behavioral change for this rep:** Slow down the price reveal. The founder is excellent once the objection is on the table — diagnostic, honest, mathematically grounded. But he reaches for the menu too fast. Earn the right to name price by surfacing the cost of the status quo first.

## Section 3: BUSINESS EVALUATOR

**Unit economics at this conversion.** Marcus signs Office at $249/mo = $2,988 ARR before design fees. Assume his trip mix matches stated $55K T&E: 6–8 trips/year, roughly half over $5K. Design fees at member rate on the heavy half land around $1,200–$1,800 annually. Blended ARR per account ≈ $4,200–$4,800. Against PHT VA cost (loaded ~$1,800/yr at this account intensity given founder evening coverage), gross margin per account sits at 55–60%. That's healthy for a hosted-under-Fora model where the host takes ~30% of commission revenue, since the membership is pure-margin and design fees split favorably.

The price-hold mattered more than the close. If the founder had folded to $99 Light, ARR drops to $1,188 + ~$1,500 in design fees = $2,688 blended — and Light accounts consume disproportionate support relative to revenue because they're single-traveler, low-routing-leverage, and tend to churn at 18–24 months when the founder either grows out of it or decides the design fee math doesn't pencil. The price hold at $249 isn't just incremental revenue; it's *qualitatively* a different account profile.

**Strategic positioning.** This sim validates the thesis that Model A's two-tier structure ($99/$249) creates productive friction for SMB buyers. Marcus's "I was expecting a hundred" is exactly the moment the model is designed to handle: the $99 tier exists as an honest option that disqualifies itself for the operations-leverage buyer. Without Light, the founder has nothing to compare $249 against, and the price feels arbitrary. With Light, $249 becomes "the version that fixes Priya" rather than "the more expensive plan." The tier structure is the pricing argument.

Compare against Model B (Unlimited at $189/$349): Marcus would have anchored at $189 and the founder would have had to hold $349 against an even sharper "I was expecting a hundred." Unlimited's no-design-fee story is powerful, but it doesn't *answer* Marcus's specific objection — which is psychological, not arithmetic. Model B works better for buyers who've already done the design-fee math and resent it. Marcus hadn't done that math yet.

Compare against Model C (three-tier $99/$249/$499): the middle tier would have anchored Marcus differently — $249 as the "Plus" middle option feels like a compromise rather than the premium tier, which actually helps the close. But the $499 Office tier would have been completely out of reach for this buyer, and the call risks getting stuck on "why isn't $249 the real one." Model C is better for buyers with more sophisticated tier-comparison instincts; Model A is better for buyers who need a clean binary.

**Retention probability.** Moderate-to-high. The Priya onboarding commitment is the retention insurance — if the founder gets Priya to use the seat in the first 30 days, account stickiness goes to ~85% at 18 months. If Priya defaults back to Concur (as Marcus himself predicted she might for the first month), the account is at material risk by month 6. FTG2 doesn't help retention directly; it helps the *signing*. Retention is bought by the operational integration in weeks 1–4.

**Referral value.** High. Marcus came in via warm intro from a YPO peer, which means he's embedded in a referral network of similarly-sized founder firms. A successful Lisbon trip in late June generates 1–2 qualified referrals by Q4 with near-zero CAC. The YPO network is the highest-LTV acquisition channel Latitude 43 has access to at this stage. Spending an evening calling Priya personally to onboard her isn't customer service — it's marketing spend, correctly routed.

**The hidden risk.** FTG2 is the most generous of the three guarantees, and it's being attached to the tier ($249) and the buyer profile (price-sensitive SMB) where it's most likely to be called. A $55K T&E firm running 6–8 trips/year has higher relative volatility per trip than a $340K T&E account with 25+ trips. One trip blowing up is a much larger percentage of Marcus's annual experience. If the founder fails the Lisbon trip, the exposure is $249 + $249 (free month) + $500 credit + opportunity cost of an unhappy YPO-network founder = ~$1,000 hard cost and potentially $10K+ in referral damage. **FTG2 is the right close tool for this buyer, but it requires the founder to personally run the named trip — which he committed to. That commitment is load-bearing.**

## Section 4: MODEL + FTG SCORECARD

**Did the price land or stick?** Stuck, then held. Friction moment at the reveal — "$249 feels like a lot for me, I was expecting maybe a hundred." Second friction moment at the Light-as-trial question. Both held without discounting. The price didn't land smoothly; it landed correctly, which is better.

**Did the FTG2 mechanic move the buyer?** Helpful but not decisive. FTG2 created the *permission* for Marcus to commit at $249 — his own line, "the FTG isn't really about the trip failing, it's about giving me a way to commit at $249 without it feeling like a leap," is the diagnostic. But the actual close was driven by the Priya ROI math, not the guarantee. FTG2's role was risk-reversal at the moment of signature, not value demonstration. That's its correct job; it did it well. Score: 7/10 — useful, not heroic.

**Would the buyer have bought under a different model?** Under Model B ($189/$349), probability of close drops to ~50%. Marcus's reference frame was "a hundred," and $189 is closer but still feels off. The no-design-fee story doesn't compensate because Marcus hadn't internalized the design fee cost yet. Under Model C ($99/$249/$499), probability of close on the *middle* tier (Plus at $249) is ~65% — the three-tier structure makes $249 feel like the sensible middle rather than the premium ceiling, which helps. But Model C loses the "Office fixes the Priya problem" framing because the Office tier is $499 and out of reach.

**Net read:** Model A is the right variant for this specific buyer. The two-tier binary ($99 vs $249) lets the founder run the "I'll talk you into Light or out of it" play, which is what actually moved Marcus. Three tiers would have diluted that move; Unlimited's premium would have widened the objection. FTG2 is the right guarantee — its generosity matches the SMB buyer's risk profile and creates signing permission without functioning as a discount. Disqualify-up judgment was handled correctly: founder offered the honest Light option, named the conditions under which Light would be the right answer (Priya won't change behavior), and let Marcus self-select up. That's the cleanest version of this close.
