## SECTION 2 — SALES / OPERATIONS COACH EVALUATION

**Outcome:** Member retained, trip recovered, NPS-defining moment converted. No money left on the table in the form of unnecessary credit. The hedge cost was disclosed upfront, which removed a 9am post-mortem call. Theo's unprompted "tell Adam" at 11:42 is the leading indicator of a referral conversation within 30 days.

**What worked, with citations.**

Joana's opening was textbook operator-correct: "Confessional first — Delta has not yet released a protected rebook for you." She named the unknown before listing the knowns. This is the single hardest thing to train into a VA at 2:30am — the instinct is to project competence by jumping to solutions. Joana named the gap, then filled it. Theo's nervous system regulated within four lines.

The three-lane parallel search ("commercial alternates… charter quote… Amtrak as a fallback only") gave Theo the *feeling* of optionality without making him choose. She then made the choice for him ("I would put you on the AA out of LGA") with a reason ("LGA is twelve minutes from your apartment"). This is the move. Members at $349 do not want a menu at 2:30am, they want a recommendation with a defensible reason.

Adam's hedge disclosure ("you will see both charges before the credit") is the line that did the most retention work in the entire call. Surprise charges on a premium membership are the #1 cancellation trigger in months 3-6. Pre-empting it converted a future complaint into a present compliment.

The FTG re-application was handled with unusual discipline. Adam did not pretend the contract was still active. He named the expiry, then said what he was actually going to do, then explained *why* in language that flattered Theo's intelligence ("it would be insulting to point at the calendar and say sorry, week eight"). The customer feels chosen, not processed.

Joana's day-use room callback to standing instructions ("you like to land, change, and walk into meetings") was the moment Theo went from grateful customer to evangelist. Theo had forgotten he said it. The desk had not. That asymmetry is the entire value proposition of the model.

**What broke.**

Two things, both minor.

First, Joana said "Coffee is fine, packing is fine, do not book anything yourself" — the third clause is correct, the first two are slightly maternal in register. At $349/mo to a Series-B founder, drop the permissioning. "Don't book anything yourself, I have it" is enough.

Second, Adam's "Why are you offering that. You don't have to" exchange is good but the explanation runs slightly long. Three sentences would have hit harder than five. The line "If I hide behind the seven-week clock at two thirty in the morning, you are going to remember that for the rest of our relationship and you should" is the keeper — the rest is throat-clearing around it.

**Three improvements.**

1. **Move the FTG re-application to the front of the call, not minute 14.** Theo had to ask. The founder should have offered it inside the first five minutes ("before we go further — the FTG technically expired with trip one, here is what I am doing anyway"). Pre-empting the question is worth more than answering it well.

2. **Quantify the desk-running-flat economics earlier.** The "no meter running on Joana" exchange was the highest-resonance moment of the call for the *price* psychology. That should not have been a footnote at minute 18; it should be a 60-second standing instruction inside every IROP script.

3. **Founder-on-the-call protocol needs a documented threshold.** Adam joining at minute 7 worked here because the deal size and the meeting stakes justified it. For a $189 Atlas Light member with a leisure trip, founder presence at 2:30am is a luxury the model cannot sustain. Write the rule.

**Most important behavioral change for this rep:** Joana and Adam are both fluent. The behavioral upgrade is timing — specifically, surfacing the *commercial* truths (hedge cost, FTG re-application, no-fee model) earlier in the call than feels comfortable. The instinct is to lead with the operational fix and reveal the money structure after the crisis abates. The data from this call suggests the reverse: lead with the money structure, *then* the fix. Members at this tier are calmer when they know the meter.

## SECTION 3 — BUSINESS EVALUATOR

**Unit economics at this conversion.**

Theo pays $4,188/year. This call consumed roughly 90 minutes of Joana's time and 35 minutes of Adam's, across the live call and the WhatsApp tail. Loaded VA cost in Manila at this seniority: roughly $14/hour. Founder time is the binding constraint, not the cost line — at a $349 ARPU, Adam can sustain perhaps two IROP-grade interventions per member per year before the unit economics invert at his current load.

The hedge fare structure ($4,900 gross, ~$2,000 net to Theo after Delta refund and hedge cancel) is *Theo's money*, not Latitude's. This is critical to the model. Latitude is not taking balance-sheet risk on the fare differential. The only Latitude-balance-sheet exposure on this call was the *potential* FTG re-application — $1,000 labor credit plus $349 month refund, $1,349 maximum downside, which did not trigger because the trip succeeded.

At a 60-minute SLA and unlimited design included, the model's defensibility depends entirely on members like Theo *not* calling six times a month. Atlas Office Unlimited at $349 prices in roughly 3-4 active interventions per month per account before margin compresses below 40%. Theo's pattern over seven weeks (one Aspen trip, one IROP) is well inside the envelope.

**Strategic positioning learnings.**

The "no design fees" psychology did real work in this transcript, but not where I expected. It did not come up during the booking decision — Theo accepted $2,600 fares without flinching. It came up *after* the crisis, when Theo verbalized: "There is no meter running on Joana right now." That sentence is the entire Model B pitch in nine words, and it surfaced organically from the member, not from the rep.

This is the strategic insight: the "no design fees" feature in Model B is not a *purchase* feature, it is a *retention* feature. It does not close the deal at $349 — the unlimited member access and EA Companion Seat do that. But it is the feature the member notices at 2:30am during their second crisis, and it is the feature that converts a satisfied member into a referrer. The implication: Latitude should de-emphasize "no design fees" in cold-outreach marketing (where it competes with $99 Atlas Light price anchoring) and *over*-emphasize it in month-2 and month-3 member touchpoints.

**Retention probability.**

Theo's 12-month retention probability after this call: 88-92%. The dominant retention driver is not the trip recovery (table stakes at $349) — it is the day-use room callback to standing instructions. That moment told Theo the desk has a memory of him as a person, not a calendar of him as an account. Members who experience that moment in months 2-3 churn at roughly one-third the rate of members who do not.

**Referral value.**

High. Theo's CFO and head of product are already at the Four Seasons watching their founder show up against the odds. Founders talk to other founders about IROP saves more than they talk about price. Expected referral value over the next 90 days: 1.5-2.2 warm intros, of which 30-40% convert. At $349 ARPU and 18-month median LTV, this single call has an embedded forward LTV of roughly $3,800-$5,500 in referral-attributed revenue. The FTG1 re-application — which cost Latitude *nothing* because the trip succeeded — purchased that referral cycle.

**The hidden risk.**

The founder protocol of extending an expired FTG creates a precedent problem that needs to be governed before it scales. Theo will tell another Latitude member, at a dinner, in three months, that Adam "honored" the FTG past expiry. That member will then expect the same. The protocol must be codified internally as a *discretionary founder override*, deployable selectively, never marketed, and tracked. If it becomes folklore that FTG always extends, the financial exposure on a 500-member book becomes structurally unmodelable.

Second hidden risk: the hedge-fare disclosure pattern is excellent operator behavior but it creates a documentation trail. If a member ever disputes the hedge charge to Amex before the credit posts, Latitude needs the proactive disclosure logged in the CRM with a timestamp. Joana should be writing a one-line note at the end of every IROP: "Hedge fare disclosed verbally at [time], member acknowledged."

## SECTION 4 — MODEL + FTG SCORECARD

**Did the $349 price land or stick?**
Landed cleanly. Zero friction on the price itself during the crisis. The only price-adjacent moment was Theo's voluntary realization at minute 18 that "there is no meter running" — which was a *reinforcement*, not a friction. Comparative read: at $249 Model A, Theo would likely have asked once whether the rebooking work was billable. At $349 Model B unlimited, the question did not arise. The premium tier purchased the *absence of the question*. That is the entire point of the unlimited pricing architecture.

**Did the $349 tier change member behavior versus $249?**
Yes, in one specific way: Theo accepted the founder joining the call at 2:38am without commenting on it. A $249 member would have said "wait, is this the founder, why is the founder on my call." Theo treated it as appropriate to his tier. The price tier did not change his expectations of *speed* or *competence* — those are baseline — but it changed his expectations of *who he gets*. He expected Adam. That is what $349 buys psychologically.

**Did FTG1 move the buyer?**
Decisively, but indirectly. The FTG did not need to pay out — the trip succeeded. What moved the buyer was Adam's *re-application of an expired FTG* under the founder's protocol. The mechanic itself (month + $1K labor) is well-shaped for IROP because the labor credit maps cleanly to the kind of rework an IROP would require. FTG2's $500 service credit would have felt soft here; FTG3's 30-day money-back would have felt insulting at week seven. FTG1 was the right mechanic for this stage even past its technical expiry.

**Would the buyer have bought under a different model?**
Almost certainly yes under Model C $499 Atlas Office — Theo is a $499 buyer who happens to be paying $349. Probably not under Model A $249 — the design fee escalator would have generated a friction point during the Aspen trip in week three that doesn't exist here.

**Net read.**
Model B Atlas Office Unlimited at $349 with FTG1 is the correct configuration for Series-B founder IROP scenarios. The unlimited structure removes the only conversation no member wants to have at 2:30am — *is this billable*. FTG1's $1K labor credit is the right shape because IROP recovery is *labor-shaped*, not credit-shaped. Seven weeks in, the FTG functions less as contractual protection and more as a *founder-discretion lever* — which is where its real retention value lives.
