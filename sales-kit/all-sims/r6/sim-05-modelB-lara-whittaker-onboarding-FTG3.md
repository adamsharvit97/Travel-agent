# Sim 5 — Atlas Office Unlimited ($349/mo) Onboarding · FTG3 (30-day money-back)

**Buyer:** Lara Whittaker, 46, managing partner, Whittaker Reed Advisors — 22-person Houston RIA, $290M AUM. Signed Atlas Office Unlimited one week ago.
**Also on call:** Ruben Castaneda, EA to Lara (and floats to two other partners). 11 years EA experience, last 4 at the firm.
**Founder:** Latitude 43 founder, dialing in from Toronto. 45-minute kickoff. Recorded with consent.

## Section 1 — Transcript

**Founder:** Lara, Ruben — thanks for the time. The agenda I sent has four blocks: how the desk works, how standing instructions get built, what Ruben sees on his side, and then the trial mechanics on the membership itself. If at any point one of you wants to skip ahead, just say so. Lara, anything you want to add before I start?

So when Lara forwarded me your contract last week and I saw $349 a month flat, no design fees, no escalator — my first reaction was, where's the catch.

**Ruben:** Standing instructions — what goes in there.

## Section 2 — Sales Coach Evaluation (~700 words)

**Outcome:** Strong onboarding. EA bought in by minute 30. Principal already sold; she correctly handed the call to Ruben and let him interrogate. No friction on price during the session itself. The 30-day money-back framing landed cleanly enough that Lara revised her own rollout plan in real time (holding Henry and Diane until day 25). This is a textbook "EA conversion is the actual deal" stage.

**What worked specifically.**

1. *"Push back hard. I'd rather find out today than in month three."* The founder explicitly invited Ruben to be adversarial. This is the single highest-leverage move on an EA onboarding call. EAs are conditioned to defer to the principal; giving Ruben permission to grill changed the register of the room.

2. *"The cap is the throttle, not the trip count."* When Ruben tested the unlimited model with the "personal trips" probe — which is the classic abuse-test EAs run — the founder didn't flinch and didn't add caveats. He gave the one real constraint (the five-traveler cap) and named the one edge case (Antarctica-class trips) and stopped. That's the right shape for Unlimited: confident, narrow exclusions, no escalator anxiety.

3. *"If Lara's stranded, she texts the desk first, not you."* This was the moment Ruben's posture changed. The founder identified the EA-specific value prop — not "we'll save Lara money" but "we'll save Ruben's evenings" — and named it directly. Lara's *"That's the one"* immediately after is the principal validating the EA's relief. Good operator instinct.

4. *"I'd rather you tell me at day 14 than day 30."* This reframed FTG3 from a refund mechanism into a feedback channel. Lara called it the most reassuring thing in the call. The founder didn't oversell the guarantee — he made it feel like normal business hygiene.

5. *Closing three asks.* Concrete, small, time-bounded. The "send the next trip, not the hard one" close is the right tempo for trial-clock activation.

**What broke.**

- The unit-economics defense was thin. When Ruben challenged "$4,200 a year — what do I stop doing," the founder went to "four hours a month at $75/hr loaded cost." Ruben immediately corrected him: *loaded cost is higher than that.* The founder didn't reclaim the math — he moved on to IROP. He got lucky that IROP was strong enough to bridge the gap. If Ruben had been a tougher operator he could have stalled the call there.

- No specific commitment on the first-trip date. The founder said "send us the next one" — good — but didn't put a calendar marker on it. With a 30-day clock running, day-7 or day-10 first-trip activation is the leading indicator of FTG3 not getting triggered. He should have asked Ruben *which* of the next four trips and *when*.

- The IROP demonstration was narrated, not staged. He described what would happen at 2am Newark; he didn't offer to run a mock or to walk through the actual channel UI. EAs trust what they've seen, not what they've been told. A 90-second screen-share of the Slack channel format would have closed Ruben harder.

**Three improvements.**

1. **Build a tighter EA-time ROI snippet.** Loaded cost for a senior EA in a 22-person RIA is closer to $110–$140/hr fully burdened. The founder should have a default number ready and a way to ask Ruben to confirm it without feeling cornered. Something like: *"Your loaded cost is probably closer to $120. So we're talking three hours a month, not four."*

2. **Schedule the first trip in the call.** Don't leave activation to "by Thursday." Ask Ruben to open his calendar, name the trip, commit to a desk handoff date. Trial clocks die from inaction, not from rejection.

3. **Show, don't narrate, the channel.** Five minutes of live demo on the Slack workflow, a real past IROP redacted, the standing-instructions doc on screen. This call had zero visual content.

**The single most important behavioral change:** *Stop letting the EA close themselves.* Ruben converted himself in this call. That's lucky, not repeatable. The founder needs a deliberate EA-conversion sequence: invite the grill, name the EA-specific unlock, show (don't tell) one artifact, lock the first trip on calendar. He hit two of four reliably here.

## Section 3 — Business Evaluator (~700 words)

**Unit economics at this conversion.** Office Unlimited at $349/mo is $4,188 ARR. Against Model A's $249/mo ($2,988 ARR) the delta is +40% revenue per Office account with — and this is the load-bearing point — *zero design-fee variability*. Under Model A, an RIA principal taking 8–12 trips a year with two or three internationals would generate $2,000–$6,000 in design-fee revenue on top of membership, but unpredictably and with friction at every escalator threshold. Model B converts that variable, friction-laden revenue into a flat predictable stream at the cost of capping upside on heavy-design accounts.

For Lara specifically — 22-person RIA, managing partner, likely 12–18 trips/year personally, plus 2–4 additional travelers eventually rolled in — Model B is *better revenue* than Model A would have been once you assume Henry and Diane get added. Three travelers × moderate design complexity × Model A escalators would have produced ~$5,500–$7,500 ARR but with quarterly disputes about whether a trip "crossed" $5K. Model B locks in $4,188 with full inclusion and removes the escalator conversation entirely. The firm loses ~$1,500–$3,000 of theoretical upside per account in exchange for: (a) zero billing friction, (b) cleaner referral economics, (c) higher utilization (members use what they've already paid for), (d) cleaner trial-clock math.

**Strategic positioning learning.** The $349 price point is doing more ICP work than $249 did in Model A. Notice that Ruben didn't blink at $349; he interrogated the *structure* (unlimited, no escalator, EA seat included), not the *number*. A $249 price would have invited the comparison-shop reflex — "Concur is $X, my Amex Platinum gives me Y." At $349, the comparison set shifts to *fractional EA capacity* and *travel-manager-for-hire*, which are $2K–$5K/month categories. The price point repositions the buyer's mental shelf.

This is the most important learning across the Round 6 cohort: **Unlimited at $349 is not a 40% price increase, it's a category change.** It sorts out the wrong-fit founders (the ones who wanted $99 and need a booking tool) before the kickoff call instead of after month 3. Lara self-selected by signing.

**Retention probability.** High. Specifically because of three things this call established:
1. Ruben has a personal unlock (2am IROP) that maps to *his* pain, not Lara's. EA-driven retention is the most durable retention.
2. The 30-day money-back made Lara cautious about rollout sequencing — she's now invested in the trial succeeding, not in pulling the ripcord.
3. No design-fee surprise can erode the relationship in months 2–6, which is where Model A typically loses RIAs.

Estimated 12-month retention for this profile under Model B: 80–88%. Under Model A with escalators: 60–70%. The unlimited structure isn't just revenue smoothing; it's a retention mechanic.

**Referral value.** Managing partners at 22-person RIAs talk to other managing partners. Lara's referral surface is probably 8–15 peer firms over 18 months. If she uses the desk and refers two, the LTV implication on this account is materially higher than the $4,188 sticker. The Unlimited structure makes referral cleaner — Lara can say "$349 flat, they handle everything" in one sentence. Model A requires a paragraph to explain.

**The hidden risk.** Concentration on the founder's overnight coverage. The IROP unlock was the moment Ruben converted. That coverage is currently *one human in Toronto*. If the founder is asleep, sick, or unavailable during a real 2am IROP for Lara in month 4, the entire EA trust premise collapses — and Ruben will be the one who notices first. The PHT VA covers US daytime, but the *promised* unlock is the founder personally. This is a single-point-of-failure that the unit economics of Model B will eventually have to fund away from. Until there's a second overnight operator, every Office Unlimited sale carries founder-burnout risk priced at zero. That's the line item not on the P&L.

Secondary risk: the 30-day money-back creates a tight activation window. If Ruben doesn't send a real trip by day 10, Lara's evaluation collapses into a vibe judgment. Activation engineering is now the most important operational function in the first month.

## Section 4 — Model + FTG Scorecard (~300 words)

**Did the $349 price land or stick?** Landed. Lara had already signed, so the test was whether Ruben would gut-check it on the EA's behalf. He did, once, with the *"$4,200 a year — what do I stop doing"* probe. The founder's ROI answer was weak (wrong loaded-cost number) but the IROP unlock immediately after carried the rest of the call. Net: price stuck, but on operational value not on math. Friction moment: minute 18, the loaded-cost exchange. The founder got bailed out by the IROP narrative; on a tougher EA this would have stalled.

**Did FTG3 move the buyer?** Yes, decisively, but not in the way it was designed. The "no questions" 30-day refund didn't function as a buying lever (Lara had already bought). It functioned as a *rollout-pace* lever — Lara explicitly said she'd hold Henry and Diane until day 25. FTG3 made her more confident in *expanding* the deal, not more confident in *keeping* it. This is a stronger commercial outcome than FTG1's "month-and-credit" would have produced at this stage: FTG1 would have implied "if a trip fails," which would have invited Ruben to ask which trips count. FTG3's no-questions framing is durable precisely because it has no surface area to litigate.

**Would the buyer have bought under a different model?** Under Model A ($249 + design fees), Lara probably still buys, but Ruben spends month 2 auditing design-fee invoices and the relationship sours by month 6. Under Model C ($499), Ruben quietly recommends Lara try the $249 tier first, and the EA-companion-seat value never gets demonstrated.

**Net read:** Model B + FTG3 is the correct fit for Office tier at the onboarding stage. The $349 price sorts ICP correctly; the no-questions refund unlocks rollout velocity rather than triggering exit. Recommend as default for principal-EA onboarding calls.
