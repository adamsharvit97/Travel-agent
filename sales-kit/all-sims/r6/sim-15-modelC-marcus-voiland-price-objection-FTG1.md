# Sim 15 — Model C (Three-Tier $99/$249/$499) · FTG1 (Month + $1K Labor Credit)

**Stage:** Price objection · **Persona:** Marcus Voiland, 36, founder, 9-person creative consulting firm, Denver, $55K T&E, warm YPO intro.

---

## Section 1: TRANSCRIPT

**Founder (Arvit):** Marcus, before we get to the number — tell me again how the trips actually go right now. You said earlier you book most of it yourself?

**Marcus:** Yeah. Me, mostly. My ops lead Talia picks up the spillover but she hates it. I probably do six trips a year that matter — pitches, two offsites, the family thing in July that I always screw up because I'm doing it Sunday night at 11pm. The rest is shorter stuff, day-trippy, in-and-out.

**Arvit:** And the screw-ups — what do those look like in dollars or in pain?

**Marcus:** Pain mostly. I missed a connection in Newark in March because I'd booked a 45-minute layover like an idiot. Cost me a morning with a client. Probably cost me the second phase of that project, honestly, though I can't prove it. The dollars I waste are smaller — wrong hotel zone, bad fare class because I didn't see the upgrade window. Maybe $4–6K a year in dumb.

**Arvit:** Okay. So here's the structure, and I'll be direct with you because you asked me to be. Three tiers. Basic is $99 a month — that's booking access to the desk, you bring the trip, we execute, design work has a fee escalator on top. Plus is $249 — same desk, but trip design is included on member trips, no escalator. Office is $499 — that's up to five travelers, EA companion seat, the whole apparatus. For a nine-person firm where really it's you traveling, Office is probably overbuilt.

**Marcus:** Okay so $99, $249, or $499. I think I'd want the Plus product but I don't know if I can do $249. Help me think through this.

**Arvit:** Sure. Let me ask the question back at you first. When you say you don't know if you can do $249 — is that a budget ceiling, or is it a "I haven't decided what this is worth yet" thing? Because those are different conversations.

**Marcus:** *(pause)* Honest answer? It's the second one. $249 isn't going to break me. I just don't know if I'm getting $150 more of something than the $99 gets me.

**Arvit:** Fair. So let's actually look at it. Basic at $99 — you call the desk, you say "book me JFK-LAX Tuesday, Marriott downtown, here's my Amex." We execute that cleanly, fast, with your standing instructions on file so you stop telling us your aisle preference every time. What Basic does *not* include is the part where you say "I've got a Tuesday pitch in LA and a Thursday board meeting in Chicago, work it out." That's design. On Basic, design carries a fee — somewhere between $250 and a few thousand depending on complexity. On Plus, design is in.

**Marcus:** How many of my trips are "design" trips versus "just book it" trips?

**Arvit:** You tell me. The Newark layover one — was that a "just book it" or did you actually need someone to think about the connection for you?

**Marcus:** *(laughs)* Yeah. That was a design failure dressed up as a booking. I was treating it like a booking.

**Arvit:** Right. And the July family trip you do at 11pm on Sunday — design or booking?

**Marcus:** Design. Heavy design. Three cities, two kids, my wife has opinions.

**Arvit:** Okay. So if I had to guess from what you've told me — of your six "trips that matter" plus the family one, four or five of them are design trips. On Basic, you'd be looking at design fees stacking on top of the $99. Could easily be another $1,500–$3,000 across the year. So the real comparison isn't $99 vs $249. It's $99-plus-escalator vs $249-flat.

**Marcus:** Hmm. Okay. That reframes it. What's the math actually look like?

**Arvit:** Basic is $1,188 a year plus design fees. If you do four design trips at, call it average $600 each, that's $2,400 in fees. Total: $3,588. Plus is $2,988 a year, design included. So at four design trips a year you're basically flat. Five design trips, Plus is cheaper. Three or fewer, Basic is cheaper.

**Marcus:** And the answer for me is four or five.

**Arvit:** That's what you just told me, yeah.

**Marcus:** *(beat)* Okay but here's the thing. I could just do Basic, and on the trips I don't want to think about, pay the design fee, and on the trips I want to do myself, do them myself. Why is that worse?

**Arvit:** It's not necessarily worse. It's a real option. The question is whether you actually do that, or whether what happens is you skip the design fee mentally — "ah, I'll just book it myself" — and you end up Newark-ing yourself again. The fee escalator on Basic is a friction tax. Some founders use it. Most use it to talk themselves out of using us when they should.

**Marcus:** That's a fair shot at me. I'd probably do exactly that.

**Arvit:** I'm not trying to upsell you. I genuinely think Basic works for some buyers. It works for the person who really does take ten cleanly-defined booking-only trips a year and doesn't need design help. You're not that person based on what you just told me.

**Marcus:** And Office at $499 is —

**Arvit:** Not for you. You've got one real traveler. Office makes sense when you've got three, four, five people whose calendars are getting handled. You'd be paying $250 a month for capacity you don't use. I'd actively talk you out of it.

**Marcus:** Okay. Appreciate that. So really it's a two-option decision dressed up as three.

**Arvit:** For your firm, yes.

**Marcus:** *(pause)* The other thing nagging at me is — what if it doesn't work? Like what if I do the family trip in July and it's worse than what I would have done?

**Arvit:** Yeah, so on Plus and Office we have a First Trip Guarantee. The mechanic is specific. If your first trip fails on our end — and we name the trip on the call, so there's no ambiguity later about which one counts — we refund that month's membership, $249, and we credit $1,000 of labor toward the rework. So if the July trip blows up, you're out nothing for that month and you've got a grand of design labor to fix what we broke.

**Marcus:** Huh. That's pretty specific. I've seen the "30-day money back, no questions" version of this from other vendors. How is yours different?

**Arvit:** Different philosophy. The "no questions" version is — to be honest with you — easier to write and a little less honest. We're telling you exactly what we'll do and putting a dollar number on the rework. The "no questions" version sounds generous but it caps your downside at the membership fee. Ours actually puts labor behind fixing the failure, which is what you'd actually want if it broke. The tradeoff is ours is narrower — it's the first named trip, not a 30-day window of "I changed my mind."

**Marcus:** So yours is "we'll fix it" and theirs is "we'll let you leave."

**Arvit:** That's a clean way to put it. Yeah.

**Marcus:** I think I like yours better, actually. Letting me leave doesn't help me if the family trip is already on fire in July.

**Arvit:** Right. The $1,000 labor credit is the part that matters operationally. A refund is nice. A team rebuilding your week is what you actually need.

**Marcus:** Okay. *(pause)* I'm going to do Plus. $249. The math works, the design inclusion is the thing I actually need, and the guarantee on the family trip — let's name that one — gives me the air cover to actually try this.

**Arvit:** Done. We'll name the July family trip as the FTG trip. I'll send the paperwork this afternoon. One thing before we hang up — Talia, your ops lead. Does she get desk access too, or is this just you?

**Marcus:** Just me for now. Let's see if I actually use it before I spread it.

**Arvit:** Smart. Standing instructions form will land in your inbox tonight. Welcome in.

## Section 2: SALES COACH EVALUATION

**Outcome:** Closed on Plus ($249). Buyer self-selected. FTG1 named on the family July trip. Clean.

**What worked, specifically:**

The strongest move was the early reframe: *"Is that a budget ceiling, or is it a 'I haven't decided what this is worth yet' thing? Because those are different conversations."* That single question collapsed a fake objection into the real one. Marcus admitted it wasn't budget — it was value uncertainty. Once that was on the table, the rest of the call was discovery-led math, not negotiation. This is the move most reps miss in price objections: they treat the dollar number as the obstacle when it's almost always the value frame.

The second-strongest move was disarming Office. *"You'd be paying $250 a month for capacity you don't use. I'd actively talk you out of it."* This is operator-correct behavior — declining revenue to land trust — and it had a measurable effect: Marcus said *"Appreciate that. So really it's a two-option decision dressed up as three."* That sentence is the founder collapsing the ladder for the buyer instead of letting the buyer feel ladder-fatigue. The three-tier structure was a liability until the rep turned it into an asset by killing one tier out loud.

Third: the "fee escalator as friction tax" framing. *"Most use it to talk themselves out of using us when they should."* That's a confessional register, slightly self-deprecating, and it inverted the usual upsell vibe. Marcus called himself out (*"I'd probably do exactly that"*) which is the moment the deal actually closed — eight exchanges before he formally said yes.

FTG1 delivery was strong on the contrast move. The rep didn't just describe the guarantee; he positioned it against FTG3's "no questions" version Marcus had seen elsewhere: *"Ours is 'we'll fix it' and theirs is 'we'll let you leave.'"* That distinction did real work. Marcus explicitly said he preferred the labor credit. For this buyer, specificity beat generosity.

**What broke:**

The math demo was a little long. Four exchanges to get to "$3,588 vs $2,988" — Marcus was already converging by exchange two. A tighter rep would have landed the numbers in one breath and moved on. There's a risk in this kind of buyer of over-explaining once they've already nodded.

The "screw-ups" discovery at the top was good but underused. Marcus mentioned $4–6K in "dumb" dollars per year and *probably losing a client follow-on*. The rep never came back to that. The Plus tier essentially pays for itself out of one prevented Newark — that line should have been spoken aloud, not left as subtext.

The rep also didn't quote Marcus's own words back to him. *"I think I'd want the Plus product but I don't know if I can do $249"* — that admission contains the close. Most reps would have closed off that line within 60 seconds. This rep did it in eight minutes through math. Slower than necessary, though admittedly cleaner.

**Three improvements:**

1. **Land the cost-of-status-quo number explicitly.** When Marcus says $4–6K in dumb dollars, the rep should say: *"So Plus pays for itself out of one prevented Newark. The rest is upside."* That's the line that lets him write the check without doing the math himself.
2. **Shorten the math demo.** One sentence: *"Basic plus your design load runs about $3,600; Plus is $2,988 flat. Plus is cheaper at your usage."* Done.
3. **Acknowledge the buyer's stated preference earlier.** When he says "I'd want Plus" — affirm and explore why, don't immediately go to math. The emotional reason he wants Plus is probably the strongest close.

**Most important behavioral change:** Stop overworking a deal that's already closing. Marcus signaled Plus at minute three. The rep kept selling until minute ten. Disciplined price-holding doesn't require disciplined price-explaining — it requires noticing when the buyer has done the work for you.

## Section 3: BUSINESS EVALUATOR

**Unit economics at this conversion:**

Plus at $249/mo = $2,988 ARR. Assuming Marcus is representative of the SMB-founder cohort Latitude is targeting, gross margin on Plus is structurally tight: design labor is included, and a 4–5 design-trip year consumes roughly 12–20 PHT VA hours plus founder review time. At blended $40/hr loaded cost, variable cost on Marcus is ~$600–$1,000/yr against $2,988 revenue. Contribution margin ~$2,000/yr per Plus member, or roughly 67%. That's a workable SMB SaaS-adjacent margin *if* design hours stay bounded. The risk is Plus members who behave like Office members — heavy design consumption without the price ceiling. Plus needs a soft cap on design hours, or this tier bleeds at the long tail.

Basic at $99 with the escalator has higher gross margin (booking is cheap) but lower LTV — the friction tax Arvit described works against retention. Members who feel nickel-and-dimed leave. Office at $499 has the best unit economics per member but a narrow ICP. Model C's three-tier structure is essentially a self-segmentation funnel: Basic for booking-only buyers, Plus for the modal SMB founder, Office for true multi-traveler firms. That's strategically sound *if* the founder can do what Arvit just did — actively route buyers to the right tier even when it costs revenue.

**Strategic positioning learnings:**

The three-tier ladder is a double-edged tool. In this call it worked because the rep killed one tier (Office) for the buyer, turning a paralysis-inducing choice into a clean A/B. In the hands of a less disciplined rep, three tiers create the classic SaaS pricing trap: buyers anchor on the cheapest, feel guilty about the middle, and dismiss the top. The model needs a sales discipline that says *"Office is not for you"* out loud — otherwise the three-tier structure converts worse than a binary.

Compared to Model B (Unlimited $189/$349), Model C's $249 is a harder sell at face value — but the design-fee escalator on Basic gives the rep a math argument Model B can't make. Arvit's "$3,588 vs $2,988" demo only exists because Basic exists. Model B has no equivalent argument; it has to win on positioning alone. Model C is a math model; Model B is a brand model. For founder-buyers who like to do the arithmetic — and Marcus is one — Model C is the stronger fit.

**Retention probability:**

High, ~80%, conditional on the July family trip executing well. Marcus self-selected, did the math himself, and named the trip. He's bought in cognitively. The risk window is months 4–7 (post-July). If the family trip lands clean, he's a 2–3 year member. If it doesn't and FTG1 fires, retention depends entirely on the rebuild quality — the $1,000 labor credit needs to produce a visibly superior rebuild, not just a refund.

**Referral value:**

Significant. YPO intro means Marcus is already inside a referral graph of similar founders — Denver YPO chapter is dense with SMB founders in the $40–80K T&E range, which is exactly Latitude's ICP. One satisfied Marcus probably yields 2–4 warm intros within 12 months. The math-driven close also makes him a good evangelist: he can repeat the "$3,588 vs $2,988" framing to peers without needing brand language.

**The hidden risk:**

Marcus closed because the rep was good. The model's three-tier structure quietly assumes every closing rep can do what Arvit just did — diagnose ICP fit live, kill the wrong tier, do the design-load math, and hold price. If Latitude scales this offering with reps who don't have founder-level discernment, the three-tier ladder degrades into a discount machine. Buyers will say *"Help me think through this"* and lesser reps will reach for Basic to save the deal. Model C is a high-skill model. It rewards the operator and punishes the order-taker. That's a hiring and training problem, not a pricing problem — but it's the one nobody's named yet.

## Section 4: MODEL + FTG SCORECARD

**Did the price land or stick?**
Landed. $249 closed without discount, escalation, or stall. Friction moment: *"I don't know if I'm getting $150 more of something than the $99 gets me."* Resolved by math, not discount. Second friction moment: *"I could just do Basic and pay design fees a la carte."* Resolved by the "friction tax" framing and Marcus self-indicting. No price concession given.

**Did the FTG mechanic move the buyer?**
Yes, decisively at the close. Marcus explicitly named FTG1's specificity as the reason he preferred it over the FTG3-style "no questions" guarantees he'd seen elsewhere: *"Letting me leave doesn't help me if the family trip is already on fire in July."* FTG1's $1,000 labor credit functioned as operational reassurance, not just financial. Naming the family trip as the FTG trip gave Marcus the air cover to commit. FTG1 was decisive — not just helpful.

**Would the buyer have bought under a different model?**
- Model A ($99/$249, binary): Likely still closes on $249, possibly faster — no Office tier to dismiss, no ladder noise. But the close would have been less defended; Marcus would have less reason to feel he chose well.
- Model B (Unlimited $189/$349): Marcus probably closes at $189 Light Unlimited, not $349. Latitude leaves ~$60/mo on the table. Worse deal economics, easier close.

**Net read:** Model C is the right model for this specific buyer, but only because the rep had the discipline to collapse the ladder in real time. For Marcus — a math-literate founder with a YPO referral and a real design need — Model C extracts ~$700/yr more than Model B while letting the buyer feel he authored the decision. FTG1 paired correctly: a buyer who's worried about a specific named trip values fix-it specificity over generic exit optionality. Best variant for this buyer-stage combo.
