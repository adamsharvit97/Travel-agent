## Section 2 — Sales Coach Evaluation

**Outcome: Closed. Light Unlimited at $189/mo. Marcus committed verbally, asked for paperwork, named a first trip.** Cycle time from objection to commit: roughly 11 minutes of dialogue. Clean close.

**What worked, specifically.**

The single most important move was Devon's first response to the objection: *"Yeah. It is a real number. I'm not going to pretend it isn't."* This is the operator-correct move. Most reps in this situation either (a) immediately defend the price with feature stacking or (b) flinch and start signaling discount availability. Devon did neither. He validated the number was high, then asked a diagnostic question — *"what were you comparing it to in your head?"* — that surfaced the actual reference price ($79 SaaS travel tools) and let him reframe the category.

The category reframe was the second high-leverage move. *"Navan at $50 a seat is a software license… that's not a software cost, that's a staffed-desk cost."* This is the cleanest version of the pitch I've heard Devon run. It does two things at once: it concedes that there are cheaper products (which builds credibility), and it relocates Latitude into a different mental bucket so the price comparison stops being apples-to-apples. The line *"$79 doesn't pay for a person"* is the kind of sentence Marcus will repeat to his wife tonight.

The third strong move was running Marcus's own math against him — but kindly. Devon let Marcus do the $189 × 12 ÷ 18 = $126/trip calculation out loud, didn't fight it, then layered in the design-fee comparison: under Model A he'd be at $2,688 once design fees stacked; under Unlimited he's at $2,268, flat. *Lower total cost, predictable line item.* Founders respond to "predictable" the way RIAs respond to "compliant." It's a values word for the persona.

FTG3 delivery was excellent. The phrase *"the bet we're making is that if we do the work right in the first 30 days, nobody asks for the money back, and if we don't do it right, you shouldn't have to argue to get out"* is the entire FTG3 thesis in one sentence. Devon didn't just deliver the guarantee, he explained why it's structured that way and contrasted it with the trip-level guarantees Marcus had heard from competitors. That comparison — *"the buyer reads 'first trip guarantee' and immediately starts thinking about the edge cases"* — was honest, slightly self-deprecating about a prior version of the product, and disarmed the skepticism reflex.

**What broke.**

Two minor things. First, Devon used the phrase *"value prop"* once, echoing Marcus, but allowed it to sit. The brief discourages corporate-speak. It didn't cost him the deal but it's a tell. Second, when Marcus asked about Office, Devon answered the question cleanly but missed a small upsell opportunity to plant the seed: he could have said *"most of our Light members upgrade to Office in month 4 or 5 when their COO starts traveling — we make that switch painless."* That's not pushy, it's foreshadowing, and it makes the inevitable upgrade conversation easier later.

The bigger missed opportunity was around standing instructions. Marcus mentioned his wife Erin, mentioned the Aspen Tuesday pattern, mentioned hating per-ticket pricing. Devon picked up the Aspen detail at close but didn't bank Erin's name or the per-ticket trauma into the pitch. The buyer is telling you what to weaponize. Use it.

**Three improvements.**

1. **Drop the phrase "value prop."** Use "what we charge for" or "what you're paying for." Operator language, not deck language.
2. **Foreshadow the Office upgrade earlier in the cycle.** Not as a push, as a roadmap. Founders like knowing the second step before they take the first.
3. **Weaponize the buyer's own disclosures more aggressively.** Marcus handed Devon three pieces of ammunition (Erin, Aspen pattern, per-ticket trauma). Devon used one. Use all three.

**The most important behavioral change for Devon: keep doing exactly what he did with FTG3.** Specifically, the move of explaining *why* the guarantee is structured the way it is, not just *what* the guarantee is. That meta-explanation is the unlock. It signals to the buyer that the company has thought hard about the buyer's psychology, which is itself a trust signal. Every rep on the team should be drilled on that pattern: don't just say the guarantee, explain the design choice behind it.

## Section 3 — Business Evaluator

**Unit economics at this conversion.**

Marcus closed at $189/mo, $2,268 ARR. Under the prior Model A he would have closed at $99/mo + ~$1,500 in expected design fees on his 18-trip mix, yielding ~$2,688 of total annual capture but with $1,200 of that in lumpy, transaction-friction-laden revenue. Model B converts the same buyer at 84% of the prior revenue but with 100% of the revenue in predictable recurring form. This is the right trade for a services business with thin operating margins: smoother revenue smooths staffing, and the design fee was always the friction point — both in close rates and in retention. The 16% revenue haircut buys higher conversion, higher retention, and lower customer-service overhead from arguing about design-fee invoices. At any conversion lift above ~19% versus Model A on the same buyer pool, Model B is net accretive. The Round 5 data suggested lift in the 30-40% range on price-sensitive SMB founders like Marcus, so the math is solidly positive.

**Strategic positioning.**

The Unlimited pivot relocates Latitude in the buyer's mind from "travel agency with a subscription wrapper" to "travel office on retainer." That's a meaningful category shift. Travel agencies compete on per-ticket economics and trip quality. Travel offices on retainer compete on relationship depth, response time, and the absence of nickel-and-diming. The $189 price point is high enough to credentialize the service (cheap implies cheap labor) and low enough that an SMB founder can absorb it on a personal AmEx if needed. The Costco comparison Marcus made is actually instructive — he reached for a *recurring premium membership* analogy, not a SaaS analogy. That's the correct mental frame and the pricing is doing its job.

**Retention probability.**

High, with caveats. Marcus exhibits three positive retention signals: (1) warm YPO intro means social switching costs are non-zero, (2) he articulated a specific pain point with per-ticket pricing that Unlimited directly solves, (3) he named a concrete first trip with a repeatable pattern, which means month-two and month-three usage is likely. The caveat is volume. At 15-18 trips/year he's a lower-touch member than the persona Latitude is built around (the founders doing 30-40 trips). Lower-touch members are at risk of the "do I really use this?" mid-year doubt — the Sarah pattern from Stage 4. Latitude should flag Marcus for a proactive month-4 check-in and aggressively encourage him to put his wife's leisure travel through the desk to drive monthly engagement above the business-trip floor. Two-traveler usage on a one-traveler plan creates stickiness asymmetric to the cost.

**Referral value.**

Very high. YPO members refer YPO members; Marcus is the kind of buyer who'll mention Latitude in his forum group within six months if the first trip lands. The 30-day money-back guarantee actually *amplifies* referral value here, because Marcus is more likely to recommend something where he can credibly say "and if you don't like it, you get your money back" — risk reversal is contagious in tight peer networks. Estimated referral lift from a member like Marcus over 24 months: 0.8-1.4 qualified intros, of which 30-50% convert. That's worth $1,800-$4,500 in expected ARR before counting the second-order referrals.

**The hidden risk.**

Model B's no-design-fee promise creates an adverse selection problem on the long tail. The buyers who are most attracted to "all design included" are the buyers who design-fee economics were specifically engineered to filter — i.e., buyers who want complex, custom, design-heavy itineraries and would have generated significant design-fee revenue under Model A. Marcus isn't that buyer (his Aspen Tuesday pattern is repeatable, low-design-cost), so for him Unlimited is net cheap to serve. But the *next* Marcus might be a buyer with three kids, four annual family trips, complex multi-leg itineraries, and an appetite for design hours that would have been $4,000 of margin under Model A and is now bundled into $2,268. Latitude needs a soft-cap mechanism — not a hard fee, but an account-management lever — to identify high-design-load members and either route them to Office or have a candid conversation about scope. Without that, Unlimited's gross margin compresses on the buyers most enthusiastic about the bundle.

## Section 4 — Model + FTG Scorecard

**Did the price land or stick?**
Stuck initially, landed after reframe. Friction moment 1: the Costco-membership analogy ("$189 every couple weeks is a real number") — Marcus had a fixed reference price near $79 and the gap created genuine resistance. Friction moment 2: the $126-per-trip math Marcus did out loud — this is the moment most reps lose the deal because they fight the math. Devon let it stand and added a second math (design-fee comparison) that beat it. Price held without discount. **Score: 8/10.**

**Did FTG3 move the buyer?**
Decisively. The pivot moment in the call is Marcus's *"no questions meaning what — I don't have to justify it?"* That question signals the buyer mentally crossing the line from evaluating to de-risking. FTG3's "you decide if it was worth it" framing landed exactly as designed — Marcus explicitly said *"I like that better"* relative to first-trip guarantees he'd heard from competitors. The no-fault structure removed the last objection (the "what if I want to fire you in two months" concern) without Devon having to discount. **Score: 9/10.** This buyer would not have closed today on FTG1 or FTG2 without further price negotiation.

**Would Marcus have bought under a different model?**
Model A ($99/$249 + design fees): Probably yes on the $99 sticker, but with higher churn risk at month 3-4 when the first design fee invoice hit. Net 24-month value lower despite higher headline conversion.
Model C ($99/$249/$499 three-tier): Yes at $99 Basic, but the design-fee escalator on Basic would have triggered the same per-ticket trauma he explicitly named. Churn risk highest.
Model B was the correct fit: high enough to signal staffed-desk quality, predictable enough to address his explicit pain point, bundled enough that FTG3 could carry the de-risking weight without needing a trip-level fallback.

**Net read:** Model B + FTG3 is the right pairing for price-sensitive SMB founders with moderate trip volume and an explicit allergy to variable cost. The Unlimited bundle neutralizes the headline-price objection by collapsing the all-in number, and FTG3's "no questions" framing closes faster than complex trip-level guarantees because it doesn't invite litigation of edge cases. **Recommended as the default offer for the Marcus persona.**
