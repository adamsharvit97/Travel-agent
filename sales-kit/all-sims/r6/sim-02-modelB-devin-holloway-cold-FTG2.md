## Section 1: TRANSCRIPT

**James:** Devin, thanks for the time. I know cold-to-call in two days is fast — appreciate you taking the slot. I've got you for 25; I'd like to spend most of it understanding how you travel, and reserve the last seven or eight for whether we're a fit. Sound okay?

**Devin:** Me. My EA Marcus is great but he's 9-to-5 Central, and by the time I'd have woken him up I'd already been on hold forty minutes. I just did it.

**James:** Okay. So before I tell you what we do, can I ask — how many trips a year are you personally taking, and roughly how many across the team?

**James:** And of those 22 personal ones — how many require actual design work versus point-and-shoot booking? When I say design, I mean: multi-leg, someone in tow, a dinner that has to land, hotel that has to be the right hotel, not just any Marriott.

**Devin:** Probably half. The straight client visits are simple. But four or five times a year I'm stitching together a board meeting in New York, a client dinner in Brooklyn the same night, and a recruiting breakfast in Manhattan the next morning before flying to a workshop in Chicago. **James:** Right. That's the shape I needed. Let me tell you what Latitude 43 is, and then I want to push back on whether it's actually right for you, because there are versions of your trip mix where it isn't.

**James:** We're a travel office hosted under Fora out of Toronto. Members apply — we don't sell to everyone who emails — and we run what we call "the desk." ## Section 2: SALES COACH EVALUATION

**Outcome:** Soft close at 80% — intake sent, agreement under review, Friday follow-up booked, first trip named. Not a signature, but as strong a cold-call outcome as Stage 1 produces. Conversion probability from this state: 70-75%.

**What worked, specifically:**

The opener — "appreciate you taking the slot... 25 minutes... reserve the last seven or eight for whether we're a fit" — sets a confessional, operator-correct register from word one. James doesn't pitch. He scopes. Devin reciprocates with the Newark bathroom story, which is the moment the call became real. That happened because James asked "was that you on the phone, or was someone helping" — a forensic question, not a sympathetic one. It pulled detail.

The discovery sequence — 22-26 personal trips, half requiring design, four or five complex stitched-together trips — gave James the exact unit-math substrate he needed for the $189 defense. When the price landed and Devin pushed ("almost double"), James didn't retreat. He walked the math: four complex trips × $750-$1,200 itinerary fees = $3,000-$4,800 versus $2,268/year flat. That's the line that earned the call. Cite: *"The unit math works at three complex trips. You said four or five."* That's not selling — that's arithmetic the buyer can verify on his own.

The Marcus handling was textbook. Devin's "are you telling me to fire my EA" was a landmine, and James defused it cleanly: *"Marcus stays on calendar... We become the travel layer he calls when he's out of his depth or off the clock."* Naming the 60% retention stat made it institutional rather than improvised.

**What broke:**

The FTG2 delivery wobbled. James led with the mechanic ("refund the month, free month, $500 credit") and then had to backfill the rationale when Devin called it nervous. The "$500 almost makes me trust you less" line is the most important moment in the call, and James was on his back foot for one exchange. He recovered well — the "the trip itself had value beyond what you paid us" framing is correct — but Devin had to teach him to lead with it. That's a coaching moment, not a fatal one, but it's exactly the kind of friction FTG2 creates that FTG1 (month + $1,000 labor credit) wouldn't.

Second break: James said "no annual commitment, no setup fee" as a throwaway. That's a buying signal accelerator he didn't capitalize on. Should have been: "monthly, cancel anytime, and the FTG protects month one — so the total downside is roughly zero." He left that on the table.

**Three improvements:**

1. **Lead the FTG with the loss, not the mechanic.** "If we blow your first trip, you didn't just lose a month — you lost a real trip with real stakes. So here's how we make that right." Then list the three components. Devin literally told him to do this.
2. **Pre-empt the "overcompensating" read.** Founders pattern-match generous guarantees to desperation. Name it first: "This is going to sound like a lot. Here's why each piece is there."
3. **Close harder on the cancel-anytime structure.** $189 monthly with no annual lock plus FTG2 means month-one downside is functionally zero. Say that out loud.

**Most important behavioral change for this rep:** Stop delivering FTG2 as a list. Deliver it as a sequence of acknowledgments — membership loss, trip loss, trust loss — each with a remedy. The mechanic survives; the framing changes. FTG2 is the most generous offer in the matrix, and James is currently making it sound like a refund policy when it should sound like an operating philosophy.

## Section 3: BUSINESS EVALUATOR

**Unit economics at this conversion:**

Devin at Atlas Light Unlimited is $189/mo = $2,268 ARR. Against a probable 22-26 trips/year, of which 4-5 are complex design trips, the all-in design value the member captures is $3,000-$4,800 of agency-equivalent fees absorbed. Gross-margin implications for Latitude 43: at four complex trips and ~5 hours of desk labor per complex trip plus ~1 hour per simple trip, Devin consumes roughly 40-45 desk hours annually. At a fully-loaded desk cost of ~$45/hr blended (PHT VA + founder time amortized), member-level cost is ~$1,950. Gross contribution: ~$320/year, or 14% gross margin. **This is thin.** Model B's premise is that thin per-member margin is offset by (a) higher LTV from no-escalator simplicity, (b) referral velocity from over-delivered FTG2 events, and (c) the absence of per-trip pricing friction that erodes trust on every complex booking under Model A.

Compared to Model A on the same buyer: Devin would have paid $99/mo = $1,188 ARR + ~$3,000-4,000 in member-rate design escalators (50% off) = $3,000-4,200 effective revenue, against similar labor. **Model A is better revenue at this usage profile.** Model B only wins on this buyer if (i) he refers 0.5+ peers/year because the pricing felt honest, or (ii) he stays 2x longer because there's no per-trip pricing event to renegotiate trust against.

**Strategic positioning:**

The $189 price functions as a qualification filter. Devin's "I was bracing for $99-ish" reaction is the single most important data point in the transcript. Model B explicitly disqualifies the buyer who wants software-priced travel and qualifies the buyer who buys outcomes. Stage 1 cold inbound at $189 will convert lower-frequency on volume but higher-quality on retention. The trade is volume for fit. For a desk that scales linearly with labor and not software, this is the correct trade — *if* sales can hold the price without "all design included" reading as a giveaway.

The "all design included" framing did real work. It transformed the conversation from per-unit pricing ("what does a complex trip cost?") to flat-rate predictability. Founders running 14-person agencies live in subscription mental models. $189 flat sits adjacent to Linear, Notion, Vercel, Ramp — it pattern-matches as a tool subscription, not a service engagement. That's strategically correct positioning.

**Retention probability:** 70-75% at 12 months, given the qualification filter and FTG2's first-trip absorbing-event design. Higher than Model A on this buyer profile.

**Referral value:** This is where Model B + FTG2 either pays for itself or doesn't. Devin's Austin founder network is dense. One referred peer at $2,268 ARR recovers the thin margin entirely. The FTG2 generosity, when it doesn't trigger (i.e., trip succeeds), becomes a story: "they offered me $500 credit on top of a refund if they failed, and they didn't fail." That's referral fuel. **Project 0.4-0.7 referrals per retained Atlas Light Unlimited member.**

**The hidden risk:**

Devin's "almost makes me trust you less" moment exposes FTG2's structural vulnerability: in sophisticated buyer segments, over-engineered guarantees signal weakness, not confidence. With less-experienced sales delivery, FTG2 could *reduce* close rate among the exact founder profile Latitude 43 targets. The mechanic is correct; the delivery risk is real. If 30%+ of buyers read it as nervous and the rep doesn't recover, FTG2 underperforms FTG1's tighter "month + $1,000 labor credit" framing. **Recommend FTG2 pair with sales-floor training before scaling to volume.**

## Section 4: MODEL + FTG SCORECARD

**Did the price land or stick?**
Landed with friction, stuck after defense. Devin's "almost double" reaction was real pushback. The "all design included" framing + four-complex-trips unit math closed the gap inside 90 seconds. **Score: 7/10.** Friction exists; it's resolvable with discovery-first sequencing. Friction would have been higher (8-9/10) without James's pre-pricing discovery on trip mix.

**Did the FTG2 move the buyer?**
Helpful, not decisive — and briefly counterproductive. The "$500 makes me trust you less" line is the strongest single piece of evidence in this sim that FTG2 is over-engineered for sophisticated buyers when delivered as a list. Recovery was successful but rep-dependent. **Score: 6/10.** Move-the-needle potential is there if delivery is upgraded; as delivered, FTG1's cleaner mechanic would have produced equal or better conversion on this specific buyer.

**Would Devin have bought under a different model?**
- **Model A ($99 + escalator + FTG1):** Probably yes, faster close, but per-trip pricing events would erode trust by month 4. Higher near-term conversion, lower 12-month retention.
- **Model C (three-tier $99/$249/$499):** Tier confusion likely; Devin would have anchored on $99 and resented being walked up. Worst fit for cold inbound.

**Net read:** Model B is the right model for Devin — the qualification filter works, the subscription mental model fits, and the flat-rate predictability matches his trip mix. **FTG2, however, is mismatched to this stage.** Cold inbound discovery rewards clean, confident guarantees; FTG2's three-part generosity reads as nervous to sophisticated buyers without rehearsed framing. **For this specific buyer/stage, Model B + FTG1 would have outperformed Model B + FTG2.** FTG2's right home is Stage 4 (mid-year retention save) or Stage 2 (onboarding reassurance), not Stage 1 cold close.

**On the brief's three questions:**
1. *Did $189 create more friction than $99?* Yes, materially — but the friction was productive. It filtered for the right buyer and forced a real conversation about value.
2. *Did "all design included" offset it?* Yes, decisively. It reframed the comparison from $189 vs $99 to $2,268/year vs $4,000-5,000/year all-in.
3. *Did FTG2's generosity feel decisive or like overcompensation?* Overcompensation, as delivered. The buyer named it. Recoverable with framing; structurally risky at scale.
