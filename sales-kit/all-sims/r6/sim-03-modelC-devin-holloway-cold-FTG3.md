## Section 2: SALES COACH EVALUATION

**Outcome:** Soft yes pending overnight reflection. Devin self-selected to Plus ($249) after Marcus actively *deflected* the upsell to Office ($499). Probability of close: 65-70%. Probability of upgrade to Office within 90 days: 40%.

**What worked, specifically:**

The opening move — "give me thirty seconds on what made you reply" — was the highest-leverage line in the transcript. It forced Devin to articulate his own pain ("running travel out of my inbox at 11pm") before Marcus pitched anything, which means every subsequent pricing tier got measured against a pain Devin had just admitted to out loud. This is textbook discovery-before-pitch and it paid off twice — once when Devin volunteered the $80K spend, once when he named Helen unprompted.

The ladder delivery was clean. "I'm going to walk you up the ladder and you tell me where you fall off" reframes a three-tier menu from a confusion problem into a self-qualification game. Devin played along — he literally played the tiers back ("Ninety-nine is booking. Two-forty-nine is booking plus design. Four-ninety-nine is the whole firm.") which is the buyer doing the seller's job. That's a strong signal the structure landed.

The most important moment was Marcus's anti-upsell: "I'd rather have you on Plus for six months and have you ask to upgrade." This is confessional register at its sharpest — it cost Marcus $3,000 of ARR on the call, and it bought him Devin's trust in the same breath. Devin's response — "That's a different sales pitch than I'm used to" — is the sound of a buyer's defenses dropping.

**What broke:**

The FTG3 delivery was *competent but not decisive*. Devin's literal response was "That's... fine." That's not the reaction you want to a marquee risk reversal. The problem is that FTG3 protects the wrong thing for this buyer — Devin doesn't care about $249. He cares about three months of wasted integration time. Marcus partially recovered by reframing the guarantee as "a try-us-once guarantee" and acknowledging the integration risk explicitly, but the guarantee itself did not move the needle. It was a check-the-box moment, not a closing moment.

Marcus also undersold the desk's *people*. Devin asked good questions about pricing and structure but never asked who would actually handle his trips. Marcus should have name-dropped a desk lead or referenced a comparable client ("we have a 12-person agency in Brooklyn on Plus, here's how they use it") to make the service feel real, not abstract.

Third — Marcus let Devin off the call without a calendar hold for tomorrow. "Talk tomorrow" is not a next step. It should have been "I'm holding 2pm tomorrow on my calendar, I'll send the invite now, if you're not ready we cancel it."

**Three improvements:**

1. **Pre-load a concrete trip into the close.** "Pick a trip in the next two weeks" was the right instinct but vague. Better: "Helen's next New York trip — when is it? Give me that one." Specificity makes the pilot real.
2. **Use a peer reference at the price reveal.** A 14-person agency founder wants to know other 14-person agency founders bought this. One sentence: "Two of our members run shops your size — happy to intro after you sign."
3. **Hold the calendar before hanging up.** Always.

**The most important behavioral change:** Marcus needs to stop letting good calls end on the buyer's terms. Anti-upselling earned him trust; he then spent that trust on nothing. Trust is currency — use it to set the next step.

## Section 3: BUSINESS EVALUATOR

**Unit economics at this conversion:** Devin self-selected into Plus at $249/mo = $2,988 ARR. Under Model C, design is included at Plus, which means Latitude carries the variable cost of design labor without an escalator. For Devin's profile — moderate complexity, ~6 member trips/year, mostly domestic with two transatlantic — design labor cost runs an estimated $1,800-$2,400 annually. Gross margin on Devin-as-Plus-member is therefore $588-$1,188, or 20-40%. This is *thin* for a desk business and below the 50%+ target margin that the Office tier is engineered to deliver.

The strategic question Model C surfaces: **is Plus a viable steady state, or is it a feeder tier?** The answer must be feeder. A book of business dominated by Plus members at 20-40% margins cannot fund the founder's overnight coverage or the PHT VA bench. Plus only works if a meaningful fraction (target: 35%+) graduates to Office within 9-12 months.

Marcus's anti-upsell was therefore not just good selling — it was good portfolio construction. He placed Devin in the tier where Devin will actually engage, which is the precondition for the Office upgrade. A Devin who buys Office now and uses 40% of it churns in month 5. A Devin who buys Plus, uses 80% of it, and adds Helen in month 4 is worth 3x lifetime.

**Strategic positioning learnings:** Model C's three-tier ladder solves a problem Models A and B don't — it gives the buyer a *visible cheaper option* they can reject. Devin's willingness to engage with $249 was almost certainly conditioned by the existence of the $99 floor. Anchoring works in both directions; the $99 tier made $249 feel like the considered middle choice rather than the entry price. Under Model A (no Basic tier), Devin would have seen $249 as the floor and likely pushed back harder. Under Model B (Unlimited at $189), he would have anchored down to $189 and the firm would have lost the design-fee escalator entirely.

**Retention probability:** 70% at 6 months, 55% at 12 months. The drop-off risk is the integration-fatigue concern Devin raised explicitly. If Marcus does not personally babysit the first three trips, Devin will revert to the inbox by month 4. The FTG3 guarantee is operationally irrelevant to this risk — Devin is not going to claim a $249 refund; he'll just quietly cancel.

**Referral value:** High. Devin sits in a peer network of Austin design/dev consultancies (likely connected through Capital Factory, ATX founder circles). One referred peer at Plus is worth $3K ARR per intro; the network density suggests 2-3 organic referrals within 18 months if retention holds.

**The hidden risk:** Model C's three-tier ladder requires the founder to coach buyers *away* from over-buying. That is a deeply countercultural sales motion. If the founder is the only person who can execute the anti-upsell, the model does not scale to a second seller. Latitude needs to decide whether Plus is a tier or a coaching artifact. If it's a coaching artifact, hire a second seller and watch this break.

## Section 4: MODEL + FTG SCORECARD

**Did the price land or stick?** Landed cleanly. Devin played back the ladder unprompted, which is the strongest possible signal that a three-tier structure didn't confuse him. The $99 anchor made $249 feel reasonable; the $499 ceiling made $249 feel like a starter, not a stretch. No friction moments on price itself. **Did the three-tier ladder confuse him?** No — and notably so. Devin's playback ("ninety-nine is booking, two-forty-nine is booking plus design, four-ninety-nine is the whole firm") was crisp. The ladder *helped* him self-qualify. Compared to Sim 1 (Model A two-tier) and Sim 2 (Model B unlimited), the three-tier here gave Devin agency, which a founder-buyer values disproportionately.

**Did he self-select correctly?** Yes, with Marcus's active help. Devin's instinct was to grab Office because it "matches reality." Marcus correctly redirected him to Plus on grounds of *usage probability*, not affordability. This is the right answer for retention even though it sacrifices ARR.

**Did the FTG mechanic move the buyer?** Helpful, not decisive. Devin's "that's... fine" reaction confirms that a money-back guarantee on a $249 line item is structurally underweight against the buyer's actual fear (integration loss). FTG3 reads as *less compelling* than offering-specific guarantees (FTG1's labor credit, FTG2's service credit) would have been for this persona, because Devin's risk is operational, not financial. A guarantee tied to the first trip's outcome would have addressed his real worry; a guarantee tied to the membership fee did not.

**Would he have bought under a different model?** Under Model A, probably — but at $249 with friction. Under Model B, possibly — but anchored to $189 with margin loss to Latitude. Model C produced the best portfolio outcome.

**Net read:** Model C + FTG3 is the right *structural* combination for cold inbound SMB founders, but FTG3 is the wrong guarantee for this stage. Pair Model C with FTG1 (trip-specific labor credit) for cold inbound. Reserve FTG3 for stages where the buyer's worry is genuinely about the recurring commitment, not the operational integration — likely Stage 5 (price objection) or Stage 4 (mid-year check-in).
