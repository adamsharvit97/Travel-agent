# Sim 4 — Model A ($99/$249) + FTG2 — Atlas Office Onboarding (Lara Whittaker + EA Ruben)

## Section 1: Transcript (~1,500 words)

**Setting:** Zoom. Tuesday, 10:02am CT. Lara Whittaker (managing partner, Whittaker Carrell, Houston RIA, $290M AUM) is at her desk. Ruben Salazar, the EA who supports her and two other partners, joins from a different floor of the same office. Founder (Toronto) opens.

**Founder:** Lara, Ruben — thanks for the 45. I want to use this time three ways. First, walk both of you through how the desk actually works day-to-day, because Ruben is the one who'll feel it before Lara does. Second, name your first trip so we have something concrete to push through the system. Third, there's one piece of the agreement I want to put back on the table now that you've signed, because it lands different at week one than it did at week zero. Sound okay?

**Lara:** That's fine. I have a hard stop at 10:45.

**Founder:** Noted. Ruben, before I start — you've been supporting Lara and two other partners for eleven years. You already have a system. I'm not here to replace it. The Companion Seat is yours, not a tool we're imposing. If after sixty days you tell Lara it's making your life worse, she should fire us.

**Ruben:** *(laughs, slightly guarded)* Appreciate that. I'll be honest — I read the onboarding doc and my first thought was, okay, another portal I have to learn. I already run Concur, I have a Marriott Bonvoy concierge line, I have the Amex Platinum travel desk on speed dial. What's different.

**Founder:** Fair question and the honest answer is: nothing is different until your first IROP at 11pm on a Sunday. The portals are similar. The difference is that when Lara's Delta connection cancels in Atlanta, you don't get woken up — we do. You wake up Monday, see the rebooking already done, and decide whether to tell her or whether she already knows because we texted her directly. That's the Companion Seat. You stay the principal on her travel. We're the night shift and the escalation layer.

**Ruben:** So I'm still booking the routine stuff.

**Founder:** If you want to. Most EAs in your spot, after about six weeks, hand us the routine too and keep the relationships — the hotel GMs, the car services, the standing instructions. You curate the standing instructions, we execute against them. But that's your call, not ours. Some EAs love offloading the booking grind; some never let go of it. Both work.

**Lara:** Ruben, what do you think.

**Ruben:** *(pause)* I think I want to see it on one trip before I say. The Sunday-night thing is real. I've had two of those in the last six months and both times I was the one rebooking from my kid's soccer game.

**Founder:** That's exactly the trip we want. Which brings me to deliverable two — name a first trip. What's on the calendar.

**Lara:** I have a client offsite in Jackson Hole, June 18 through 21. Me, my CIO, and the client's CFO are flying in from three different cities. I need a house, not a hotel — the client specifically asked for a house with a real kitchen so we can do working dinners. Private chef one of the nights. Ground transport from Jackson Hole airport which is a nightmare. And I want Ruben copied on everything but not running it.

**Founder:** Good. That's a real trip — three travelers converging, a non-standard lodging ask, a service layer on top. Let me tell you what that looks like on the Office plan you signed, because this is where I want to put something back on the table.

You're on Atlas Office at $249 a month. That includes Ruben's Companion Seat, up to five active travelers, all the booking flow. Design fees are separate — that's the trip-design layer, the house sourcing, the chef coordination, the ground logistics. Under $5,000 of design work, there's no fee. Above $5,000 it goes on an escalator — $250 at the low end up to $5,000 for the most complex builds — and as a member you're at fifty percent off that escalator.

**Lara:** Hold on. So the $249 doesn't include the design work on this trip.

**Founder:** It includes it if the design effort comes in under the $5K threshold, which most three-traveler domestic trips do. Jackson Hole with a house and a chef — I'd estimate this one lands around $2,800 to $3,400 of design work. So zero design fee on top of the membership. The escalator only kicks in if you're doing, say, a fourteen-day multi-country build with six travelers and three private aviation legs. Different animal.

**Lara:** Okay. I want to be clear because I don't like surprise invoices. So Jackson Hole — $249 this month, plus the actual cost of the house, the chef, the cars. No design fee.

**Founder:** Correct. And if for any reason the build crosses $5K of design effort, we tell you before we cross it. Not after.

**Lara:** Good.

**Ruben:** Can I see the design estimate before it goes to her? I don't want her getting a number cold.

**Founder:** Yes. That's the Companion Seat working as designed. Every quote routes to you first, you forward or hold.

**Ruben:** Okay. That actually helps.

**Founder:** Now the piece I want to put back on the table. When you signed last week, we mentioned the First Trip Guarantee. I want to operationalize it now that we have a real first trip on the board, because at sign-up it's an abstraction and right now it isn't.

The guarantee on this Jackson Hole trip — if we fail on our end, meaning we miss the brief, we book wrong, the house falls through because we didn't vet it, the chef no-shows because we didn't confirm — three things happen. We refund this month's $249. We give you next month free. And we issue a $500 service credit you can apply to any future trip. That's the guarantee. I want it on record before we start building, not after.

**Lara:** *(beat)* That's a lot of downside coverage for $249.

**Founder:** It's deliberate. The reason it's structured that way is because the first trip is the one where you have no evidence we're real. After Jackson Hole you'll have data. Before Jackson Hole you have a pitch and a contract. The guarantee is the bridge.

**Lara:** What doesn't it cover.

**Founder:** Anything outside our control. Weather cancellations, the airline's IROP, the chef's car breaks down on the way over — those are operating realities, not us failing. What it covers is *us* — our build quality, our vetting, our confirmation discipline. If we hand you a house and the listing photos didn't match reality, that's on us and it triggers the guarantee. If a pipe bursts the morning you arrive, that's the universe and we work the problem with you but the guarantee doesn't fire.

**Ruben:** How do we invoke it. Is there a form.

**Founder:** You email me or text me. I make the call within 24 hours and if it's a coin-flip I default to firing the guarantee, not defending against it. We don't have a claims department. There are thirty-eight members.

**Lara:** That's the part that actually moves me. Not the $500 — the fact that you said you default to firing it.

**Founder:** Good. That was the intent of saying it out loud.

**Lara:** Ruben, anything else you want to ask before we let him build.

**Ruben:** One thing. The Sunday-night scenario. What's the actual number I call.

**Founder:** *(shares screen)* This is the desk line. It's a real human inside 60 minutes, US business hours covered by our PHT VA, evenings and weekends and overnights covered by me directly until we hit scale to hand that off. You'll get the standing-instructions doc by Thursday — that's where you tell us Lara's seat preference, her dietary, her ground-transport defaults, the three hotels she'll always take in New York. We build against that doc, not against memory.

**Ruben:** Okay.

**Lara:** Send me the Jackson Hole intake form today. I'll have it back tomorrow morning.

**Founder:** Done. One last thing — Ruben, I want a fifteen-minute call with just you next week. No Lara, no partners. The Companion Seat works or doesn't work based on how *you* feel about it, and I want to hear it without her in the room.

**Ruben:** Yeah. I'd actually want that.

**Lara:** Good. We're at 10:43. Thank you.

## Section 2: Sales Coach Evaluation (~700 words)

**Outcome:** Clean onboarding. Trip named (Jackson Hole, June 18–21). FTG2 reinforced and accepted on the record. EA Ruben moved from guarded ("another portal") to engaged ("I'd actually want that" re: the 1:1). No price renegotiation. No buyer's remorse surfaced. Founder set up a structural follow-up with the EA, which is the single highest-leverage move on this call.

**What worked, specifically:**

The opening frame — "Ruben is the one who'll feel it before Lara does" — was the most important sentence in the call. It established that the founder understands the actual power dynamic in a partner+EA relationship. Tenured EAs (Ruben at 11 years, shared across three partners) are the ones who kill or save vendor relationships. Lara signs, Ruben decides whether it sticks. The founder talked to him directly, which is rare and which Ruben noticed.

The line "If after sixty days you tell Lara it's making your life worse, she should fire us" did two things. It gave Ruben veto authority publicly. And it framed the founder as someone who would rather lose the account than fight an EA — which is the correct posture, because an EA-hostile rollout fails 100% of the time.

The IROP framing — "you don't get woken up — we do" — was the right hook for Ruben specifically. His earlier comment about rebooking from his kid's soccer game was the tell. The founder caught it and didn't oversell; he just named the night-shift function and let Ruben do the math.

The FTG2 delivery was structurally strong because it was delivered *against a named trip*, not as an abstract reassurance. "I want it on record before we start building, not after" is the operator-correct register the brand demands. And the unscripted moment — "I default to firing the guarantee, not defending against it" — was the line that actually moved Lara, by her own admission. That's the kind of sentence that can't be rehearsed; it has to be true.

The design-fee disclosure was handled cleanly. Lara's "I don't like surprise invoices" was the friction point and the founder didn't dodge it. He estimated the specific trip ($2,800–$3,400 of design effort, sub-$5K, zero design fee), named the threshold, and committed to telling her *before* crossing it. That's the right answer; vague reassurance would have lost her.

**What broke:**

The Model A escalator is fundamentally a confusing structure to deliver verbally and the founder spent more airtime on it than the rest of the call combined deserves. The phrase "$250 at the low end up to $5,000 for the most complex builds — and as a member you're at fifty percent off that escalator" is six numbers in one breath. Lara's "Hold on" was the cost. She got there, but it cost momentum. Under Model B (Unlimited) this conversation doesn't happen at all.

The founder did not anchor the $249 against anything. No comp to Amex Platinum travel desk fees, no comp to what a Houston EA's overtime costs. The price stuck because Lara already signed, not because the founder defended it well in the room.

The founder also under-used Ruben's earlier admission about the Sunday-night rebookings. That was a gift and got one sentence. It deserved a story.

**Three improvements:**

1. **Pre-build the design-fee math on a one-pager.** Don't deliver the escalator verbally. Send a visual before the call showing where typical trips land. Saves three minutes and a "hold on."
2. **Mine the EA's pain.** When Ruben volunteered the soccer-game rebooking story, the founder should have asked one follow-up: "What did that cost you with Lara?" That answer is the wedge for the next twelve months of retention.
3. **Schedule the EA 1:1 in-call, not as a TBD.** The founder offered it but didn't book it. Book it on screen, in the moment.

**Most important behavioral change for this rep:** Stop explaining the design-fee escalator. Estimate the specific trip, name the threshold, commit to the heads-up. The escalator structure is a Model A artifact; talking through it is what creates the friction. Estimate, don't enumerate.

## Section 3: Business Evaluator (~700 words)

**Unit economics at this conversion:** Atlas Office at $249/mo = $2,988 ARR per account on membership alone. Jackson Hole trip carries roughly $2,800–$3,400 of design effort but generates zero design revenue because it falls below the $5K threshold. This is the structural feature of Model A that needs to be understood clearly: on accounts whose trip profile sits predominantly below the $5K design-fee floor, Atlas Office *is* the unit. There is no design-fee upside.

For Lara specifically — a 22-person RIA with three partners sharing one EA, $290M AUM — the likely trip pattern is 8–14 trips per year across the partner group, with maybe 1–2 trips per year crossing the $5K design-effort threshold (international, multi-stop, family travel layered on). On those 1–2 trips, member discount cuts the escalator in half. Expected annual design revenue from this account: $1,500–$4,000 on top of the $2,988 membership. Total expected ARR: $4,500–$7,000.

Against a fully-loaded cost of serving this account — Ruben's onboarding, standing-instructions build, founder's 60-min SLA coverage on evenings, plus PHT VA daytime allocation — the contribution margin on Model A for this buyer is thin in year one and only becomes attractive if (a) Ruben hands over the routine bookings by month 4–6, expanding active-trip volume, or (b) the partner group expands to use of all five Companion Seats allowed under Office.

**Strategic positioning:** Model A's design-fee structure is the right shape for buyers whose trip complexity is genuinely binary — most trips simple, occasional trip complex. For an RIA managing partner with predictable trip patterns (client offsites, conferences, occasional international leisure), this is defensible. But the call surfaced the structural risk: explaining the escalator is a cost. Every minute spent justifying tiered pricing is a minute not spent operationalizing the relationship. Model B (Unlimited) at $349/mo eliminates this conversation entirely and trades $100/mo of certain revenue for zero friction. On an account like Lara's — high-trust, partner-tier, will probably stay 24+ months — that trade is favorable.

**Retention probability:** High. ~75% probability of 12-month retention, ~55% at 24 months. The two retention vectors are (a) EA adoption and (b) trip volume. The founder set up both correctly: Ruben got veto authority and a 1:1, and the Jackson Hole trip is high-stakes enough that good execution will produce a referenceable result. The risk is the four-month dead zone after the first trip — between trip 1 (June) and trip 2 (likely Q4), the account goes quiet, Ruben drifts back to Marriott Bonvoy and Amex Platinum, and the membership becomes a line item nobody defends.

**Referral value:** Very high. RIA managing partner in a tight Houston network (RIA principals refer laterally and to each other's CIOs). One reference call from Lara in month 6 is worth more than the entire H1 marketing spend. The founder should ask for the referral *after Jackson Hole executes*, not before, and should ask Ruben for the EA-network referral separately. EAs talk to other EAs constantly; an enthusiastic Ruben is a 3–5 account pipeline by himself.

**The hidden risk:** Ruben's 11-year tenure across three partners means the two other partners weren't on this call but will form opinions about Latitude based on Ruben's experience and one or two side conversations they catch in the hallway. If trip 1 goes well, the founder has a credible path to converting the other two partners to Atlas Light at $99 each — adding $198/mo of pure-margin ARR with near-zero acquisition cost. If trip 1 wobbles, the other two partners never sign and Ruben quietly de-prioritizes the desk. The leverage point isn't Lara; it's the partner-adjacent expansion through Ruben.

The single most important strategic learning from this sim: Model A's design-fee escalator is the wrong frame at onboarding. The founder should pre-quote the named trip in writing, not enumerate the escalator verbally. Every account onboarded under Model A should arrive at the kickoff with a pre-built design-fee estimate for their named trip in hand. That single change cuts onboarding friction by 30%.
