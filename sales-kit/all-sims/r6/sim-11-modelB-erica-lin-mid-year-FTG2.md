# Sim 11 — Model B (Unlimited $189/$349) + FTG2 — Mid-Year Low-Usage Check-In

**Buyer:** Erica Lin, 44, Partner, Hartline Pierce (17-person M&A boutique, Boston).
**Tenure:** 6 months on Atlas Office Unlimited ($349/mo). 5 of 14 trips routed through Latitude. EA Diane defaults to Concur. Retention risk.
**Founder:** Mark (Latitude 43). Tuesday 2:10pm ET. Zoom. 30 minutes blocked.

---

## SECTION 1 — TRANSCRIPT (~1,500 words)

**Mark:** Erica, thanks for the half hour. I want to be useful, not performative, so I'll skip the warm-up. You're six months in. I pulled the numbers this morning — we've handled five of fourteen trips since November. That's a 36% capture rate on a plan that assumed something closer to 80. Before I say anything else: is that number a surprise to you, or roughly what you expected?

**Erica:** It's not a surprise. I knew Diane was still putting most things into Concur. I didn't know it was that lopsided.

**Mark:** Okay. That's the honest answer I needed. Can I ask the obvious follow-up — is the problem Diane, is it Latitude, or is it the workflow between the two?

**Erica:** Honestly? It's mostly Diane. She's been with me eleven years. Concur is muscle memory. When I'm running between meetings I just forward the calendar invite to her and she books it the way she's always booked it. Your team is good — the Lisbon trip in February was the best travel experience I've had in years — but Diane has to remember to open a different door, and she doesn't.

**Mark:** Okay. Here's where I want to be straight with you, because the math is uncomfortable. You're paying $349 a month on Atlas Office Unlimited. Over six months that's $2,094. You used us for five trips. Per-trip that pencils to about $419, which is more than design fees would have been on the old model. So one read of this conversation is: I should be selling you on staying. The other read — the one that's actually fair — is that I should be telling you that you bought the wrong plan for how your office actually works. I want to do the second one.

**Erica:** I appreciate that. Go on.

**Mark:** The Unlimited tier is built for offices where the EA is calling the desk for everything — five travelers, eight, ten trips a month, design work baked in. You're not that office. You're a partner who travels heavily, but the rest of the firm books their own, and Diane has a system she likes. So the question isn't "how do we get you to use more of the $349." The question is "what's the right shape of this relationship given how you actually operate."

**Erica:** Right. And what's the answer?

**Mark:** Three options. I'll lay them out and then you tell me which one feels closest. Option one — we move you down to Atlas Light Unlimited. $189 a month, single traveler, that's you, design still included, no per-trip fee. You save $160 a month. Diane keeps Concur for the firm; you have a direct line to the desk for your own travel.

**Erica:** That sounds like the right size.

**Mark:** Hold on, let me give you the other two before you commit. Option two — we pause your membership. Full stop. No charge. You come back in three or six months if and when the workflow with Diane changes. Option three — and this is the one I'd argue against, but I want it on the table — we keep you at $349 and I get on a call with Diane personally, walk her through the EA Companion Seat, and you give it another quarter. If capture rate isn't north of 60% by September, we revisit.

**Erica:** Why would you argue against option three?

**Mark:** Because Diane isn't going to change in a quarter. Eleven years of habit doesn't get rewritten by a forty-minute training call from a vendor she didn't pick. I'd be selling you hope. The Light Unlimited tier is honest about what's actually happening — you use us for your travel, Diane handles the firm.

**Erica:** Okay. So before I pick — what about the six months I've already paid? Three hundred and forty-nine times six, most of which I didn't use.

**Mark:** Yeah. This is where I want to talk about the First Trip Guarantee from when you signed. You remember the terms?

**Erica:** Vaguely. Refund-something if the first trip went sideways.

**Mark:** Right. The version you signed under was — if the first trip failed on our end, we refunded the month, gave you the next month free, plus a $500 service credit. Your first trip didn't fail; the Lisbon trip went well. So technically the guarantee doesn't trigger. But the spirit of it was risk reversal — we eat downside when the relationship isn't working. I think the spirit applies here even if the letter doesn't.

**Erica:** Meaning what.

**Mark:** Meaning I'd like to apply the $500 service credit to your account regardless, against future bookings. And I'd like to give you the next month free as you transition down to Light Unlimited. So your June is zero, your July is $189, and there's $500 sitting in the account against the Tokyo trip you mentioned in the intake. That's not a refund of the six months — I'm not going to pretend you didn't get value from the trips we did run — but it's an acknowledgment that the plan wasn't sized right and I should have caught it sooner.

**Erica:** (pause) That's more than I was expecting you to offer.

**Mark:** It's less than you paid in capacity you didn't use. I'm not being generous, I'm being accurate.

**Erica:** Okay. Two questions. First — if I go to Light Unlimited and Diane suddenly figures it out and starts routing the firm's travel to you, am I stuck on a too-small plan?

**Mark:** No. Upgrade back to Office Unlimited any time, no fee, no penalty, prorated on the day you flip. The credit and the free month travel with you regardless of tier.

**Erica:** Second — and this is the awkward one — should I just cancel? Pause it, like you said. If Diane isn't going to change, am I really going to use $189 a month either?

**Mark:** Fair question. Let me put it back to you. How many trips do you personally take in a year — just you, partner travel?

**Erica:** Twelve to fifteen. Pitches, closings, the occasional offsite.

**Mark:** And of those, how many last quarter did Diane book versus you handling yourself or asking us?

**Erica:** Probably eight she booked, two I asked you for directly, one I did myself on a Sunday night because she was off.

**Mark:** So the realistic floor is two to three trips a quarter where you'd call the desk directly. At Light Unlimited that's $63 a trip with design included. The pause option saves you the $189, but it also means the next time you're stuck on a Sunday night you're back on Google Flights at 11pm. The question is what that Sunday-night experience is worth.

**Erica:** Yeah. That's the real number. Okay. Light Unlimited. Move me down.

**Mark:** Done. I'll process the downgrade today. June free. July at $189. $500 credit live on your account by Friday. I'll send the updated terms in writing this afternoon, and I'll loop Diane in on one thing — when you forward her a calendar invite that's a partner trip, she has the option to flick it to us with one click. She doesn't have to change her workflow for the firm; she just has a one-key escape hatch for the trips that are yours. That's the only ask I have of her.

**Erica:** That I can sell her on. The "you're being replaced" framing was never going to work.

**Mark:** Right. Never was the play. One last thing — six months from now I want to do this exact call again. If Light Unlimited is the right size, great. If it's not, we adjust. I'd rather have an honest $189 customer for four years than a confused $349 customer for nine months.

**Erica:** That's the right answer. Send the paperwork.

**Mark:** Will do. Thanks, Erica.

## SECTION 2 — SALES / CS COACH EVALUATION (~700 words)

**Outcome:** Save with downgrade. ARR drops from $4,188 to $2,268 (a 46% haircut), one month forgiven ($189), $500 service credit issued. Net retained ARR: ~$2,079 effective year one post-concession; $2,268 steady-state. Counterfactual is cancellation (zero) or 60-day churn at current plan (~$700 collected before departure plus a likely negative reference). The downgrade is the correct outcome and the rep priced it accurately.

**What worked, with citations.**

The opening data drop is the strongest move on the call. "We've handled five of fourteen trips since November. That's a 36% capture rate." Naming the number first, before Erica has to confess it, inverts the power dynamic — she's not being audited, she's being briefed. Compare to the Model A version of this conversation where the rep can soften the math (design fees keep variable cost low at low usage); on Unlimited there's no hiding the per-trip number and the rep wisely doesn't try. "Per-trip that pencils to about $419, which is more than design fees would have been on the old model" is the most important line on the call. It's the rep eating the unit economics out loud. Buyers reward that with trust, and Erica does — within three turns she's asking for the answer instead of defending her usage.

The three-option fan-out ("downgrade / pause / stay and re-train Diane") is textbook. Three options reads as advice, not a pitch. Critically the rep volunteers that option three is the one he'd argue against — a small piece of negative selling that buys the credibility needed to land the recommendation. Without that line the conversation flattens into a default downgrade pitch.

The FTG2 reframe is the call's most interesting moment. The letter of the guarantee doesn't trigger (first trip succeeded), but the rep applies the $500 credit and the free month under "the spirit of it." This is the right read: FTG2's $500 service credit was always the part of the guarantee that had post-onboarding optionality, because service credit (unlike a month refund) can be applied at any tenure point. The rep treats it as a relationship instrument, not a contractual one. Erica's "more than I was expecting" is the tell that the move landed.

**What broke.**

Two soft misses. First, the rep didn't ask earlier whether Diane has actually been pitched the EA Companion Seat in a real working session. He assumes (probably correctly) that an eleven-year EA won't change, but he assumes it without evidence on the call. A sharper version would have asked: "When was the last time someone from my team sat with Diane for twenty minutes?" If the answer is "never" then the downgrade is premature.

Second — and this is the bigger one — the rep didn't probe whether other partners in the 17-person firm might be candidates for Latitude. He's looking at Erica's seat in isolation. The Office Unlimited plan covers up to five active travelers. If there are two other partners who travel heavily and would actually use the desk, the right answer might be "keep Office Unlimited, but let me onboard partners 2 and 3 to lift the capture rate." That conversation didn't happen. It's left $200/mo of potential revenue on the table and, more importantly, missed the chance to make Latitude a firm tool instead of an Erica tool.

**Three improvements.**

One: before offering the downgrade, ask the "who else travels here" question. Always. The seat count on Office Unlimited is the asset; not exercising it is the leak.

Two: when applying FTG mechanics outside their literal trigger, name the move explicitly. "I'm extending this beyond the contract terms because I think it's the right call" is stronger than letting the buyer notice. Generosity unnamed is generosity wasted.

Three: schedule the six-month follow-up on the call, on the calendar, before hanging up. "I want to do this exact call again in six months" is good. Booking it in the moment is better.

**Most important behavioral change for this rep:** Stop accepting the buyer's frame of the account as a single seat. Unlimited's economics only work at multi-traveler capture; every retention call should start with "who else in the firm is the realistic next user" before it touches price.

## SECTION 3 — BUSINESS EVALUATOR (~700 words)

**Unit economics under Model B at this conversion.**

Erica's six months on Office Unlimited generated $2,094 in subscription revenue against five trips. At a conservative $180 fully-loaded design + ops cost per trip (PHT VA + founder oversight + tooling allocation), variable cost is $900. Contribution margin on the account to date: ~$1,194, or 57%. That's healthier than the same buyer would have looked under Model A, where the design fees on five trips ($0 under $5K, but some escalators likely triggered on the Lisbon and a hypothetical Tokyo trip) would have netted maybe $1,800–$1,900 in total revenue against the same $900 variable cost — contribution closer to $900–$1,000.

So the counterintuitive read: Model B is *better* for Latitude when the customer underuses. Higher fixed subscription, no design fee leakage, predictable margin even at 36% capture. The Unlimited model effectively transfers usage risk from Latitude to the customer — which is exactly when you'd expect the retention risk to spike, because the customer feels the underutilization more acutely than under a usage-based model.

**Post-downgrade economics.** Light Unlimited at $189/mo with a realistic 2–3 trips per quarter (~0.8/mo) implies variable cost of ~$144/mo against $189 revenue. Contribution drops to ~$45/mo, or 24%. That's thin. The downgrade saves the relationship but converts a high-margin underuser into a low-margin right-sized user. Across the portfolio, if 30% of Office Unlimited accounts downgrade to Light Unlimited, blended margin compresses meaningfully. The pricing team should model the cohort behavior, not just the per-account.

**Strategic positioning learnings.**

The Unlimited model surfaces low-usage problems faster and more painfully than the variable-fee model. Under Model A, a 36% capture rate is uncomfortable but the customer's monthly bill is lower because they didn't trigger design fees — the pain is muted. Under Model B, the customer sees $349 every month regardless and the gap between value and price becomes the central narrative of the relationship. This means: (a) mid-year check-ins on Model B must be earlier and more proactive — month four, not month six — because the customer is already doing the math by month three; (b) the sales motion at signup must include a usage forecast the customer commits to out loud, so the mid-year conversation has a referent. "You told me in October you'd run 10-12 trips a quarter; we're at five in six months — what changed?" is a different conversation than "you're underusing."

**Retention probability.** Pre-call: ~25%. The customer was actively comparing the $349 line item to ROI and the trajectory pointed at cancellation by month nine. Post-call with downgrade and FTG2 service credit applied: ~75% at 12 months, ~55% at 24 months. The free month and credit are not the deciding factor — the honest framing is. Erica will tell two or three M&A peers about this call.

**Referral value.** This is the hidden upside of the downgrade. M&A partners talk to other M&A partners constantly. The story Erica now tells at the next industry dinner is not "I cancelled a travel service" — it's "the founder called me and downgraded me himself, gave me a credit, told me I'd bought the wrong plan." That's a strictly better referral story than "the Lisbon trip was great." Expected referrals from a downgraded-and-saved account at this profile: 1.5–2.5 inbound leads over 12 months. At a 25% close rate at $189–$349, that's $850–$2,100 in attributable ARR. The downgrade pays for itself inside a year.

**The hidden risk.**

Diane. Not Erica. The structural risk in this account isn't the partner's retention — it's that as long as the EA remains the choke point and the firm's other partners default to Concur, Latitude will never reach the multi-seat economics that justify Office Unlimited. The downgrade is a tactical save but a strategic retreat: it concedes the firm to Concur and accepts being the partner's personal tool. To reverse that, Latitude needs an EA-channel motion — a way to land in firms through the EA rather than around her. Until that exists, every M&A boutique account on Office Unlimited is at risk of the same compression. The Erica call is a leading indicator for a category problem.

## SECTION 4 — MODEL + FTG SCORECARD (~300 words)

**Did the price land or stick?** Price did not stick at $349. The Unlimited model amplified the low-usage pain rather than absorbed it — exactly the opposite of how it's positioned at signup. Friction concentrated at the $419-per-trip moment when the rep stated the unit math out loud. The $189 downgrade, by contrast, landed cleanly because it's a real change in product shape (single-traveler tier vs. multi-traveler), not a discount. **Score: 5/10 at original price; 9/10 at downgrade tier.**

**Did the FTG2 mechanic move the buyer?** Yes, but laterally rather than at the decision point. The $500 service credit was decisive in converting Erica's "should I just cancel" question into "okay, Light Unlimited." Without the credit and the free month, the pause option likely wins. FTG2 is the only one of the three guarantees with post-onboarding optionality — the service credit can be redeployed mid-life because it's tied to future bookings, not the first trip. FTG1 (labor credit) would have been useless here. FTG3 (30-day refund) expired month two. **FTG2 was the right guarantee for this stage. Score: 9/10.**

**Would the buyer have bought under a different model?** Yes — Model C's Atlas Light Plus at $249 might have been the correct original sale, since Erica's actual usage pattern is partner-only with design support. Model A at $249 with design fees would have looked cheaper monthly but generated the same retention conversation at month six, just with less margin for Latitude. Model B was wrong at signup, right at retention (the downgrade tier is the actual fit).

**Net read.** For the *mid-year save*, Model B + FTG2 is a strong combination because the downgrade ladder ($349 → $189) is a real product move, not a discount, and FTG2's service credit converts cleanly to a save instrument. For the *original sale six months ago*, this account should never have been on Office Unlimited. **Stage-fit: B is a recovery model, not an acquisition model, for this persona.**

**Key answer to the prompt's specific question:** The Unlimited model makes low-usage problems *visibly worse* for the customer (full $349 hits every month regardless of trips) but *economically better* for Latitude (predictable margin, no design-fee leakage). The $349→$189 downgrade conversation is materially different from Model A's $249→$99 — it's a real product change (multi-traveler to single-traveler) rather than a near-cancellation, which gives the rep a face-saving ladder Model A doesn't have. That ladder is the single best argument for Model B at this stage.
