# Sim 10 — Mid-Year Low-Usage Check-In · Model A · FTG1 (Recovery Variant)

**Persona:** Erica Lin, 44, partner at Hartwell Briggs, a 17-person Boston M&A boutique. Six months on Atlas Office at $249/mo. Used Latitude for 5 of 14 trips. EA Diane still defaults to Concur. Two design-fee escalators on file ($425 and $780). Total paid to Latitude six months in: $1,494 sub + $1,205 design = **$2,699**. Founder (Andrew, Latitude 43) on the call.

---

## Section 1 — Transcript

**Andrew:** Erica, thanks for keeping the slot. I want to be honest about what this call is. You've been with us six months, and when I pulled your file last night I had a hard time selling myself on why you'd renew. So I'd rather we talk about that head-on than pretend this is a normal check-in.

**Erica:** Okay. That's a different opening than I was expecting. Go ahead.

**Andrew:** Five trips out of fourteen ran through the desk. Diane has booked the other nine in Concur. You've paid $1,494 in membership and another $1,205 in design fees on the two complex trips — Lisbon in March and the Aspen offsite. Roughly $2,700 to us, and by my read maybe $500 to $700 of that felt like value you couldn't have gotten from your corporate tool. So the math is bad, and you already know it's bad, which is why this meeting exists.

**Erica:** *(pause)* I appreciate you saying it that way. I was going to be more diplomatic about it. But yes. Diane and I had a version of this conversation two weeks ago. She thinks I'm paying for a status symbol.

**Andrew:** Diane is mostly right. Can I ask what's actually happening when a trip comes up? Walk me through the last one Diane put in Concur instead of sending to us.

**Erica:** Sure. New York, two nights, client dinner, Carlyle. Diane had it booked in eleven minutes in Concur. By the time she would have written the brief for your desk and waited for options, she'd have been an hour in. She's measured on throughput. So she defaults.

**Andrew:** That tracks. Two nights at the Carlyle is not a trip we add value to. If Diane sent that to us we'd send back essentially what she already had, maybe shave $40 on the rate, and it'd take longer. Concur is the right tool for that trip. I want to be clear about that — I'm not here to argue you should send us everything.

**Erica:** Okay, that's also not what I expected. Most vendors would tell me I'm using it wrong.

**Andrew:** You're using it wrong by our pricing model, not by common sense. Which is the actual problem. Let me show you what I mean. The two trips you sent us — Lisbon and Aspen — those were the right trips. Lisbon had three connecting travelers, a missed leg out of Newark we rebooked overnight, and a villa instead of a hotel block. Aspen was the partner offsite, twelve people, ground transport from Denver, dietary stuff, the whole thing. Those are the trips Concur falls over on. And those are the trips where you paid us a design fee on top of the sub.

**Erica:** Right. The Aspen invoice surprised me, honestly. I knew there was an escalator. I didn't expect $780.

**Andrew:** That's fair, and that's part of why I wanted to have this call before renewal and not after. The $249 sub plus design-fee model works when you're sending us six, eight, ten complex trips a year. You're sending us two. So you're paying the subscription as if you're a heavy user and the design fees as if you're a light one. Worst of both.

**Erica:** That is exactly how it feels. Yes.

**Andrew:** So I want to put three things on the table and let you tell me which one is least wrong.

**Erica:** Go.

**Andrew:** One. You stay on Atlas Office at $249, and I commit personally — me, not Diane — to a monthly fifteen-minute call where we look at your calendar three weeks out and I tell you which trips to send us and which to leave in Concur. We stop pretending Diane is going to triage. I do it.

**Erica:** Hm.

**Andrew:** Two. You move down to Atlas Light at $99. You keep access, design fees still apply on complex trips at the member rate, but you're not paying $249 for capacity you're not using. The math gets honest. You'd have paid us about $594 in sub plus the same $1,205 in design — $1,800 instead of $2,700 — for the same outcome.

**Erica:** And the third?

**Andrew:** Three. You cancel. I refund you one month — the $249 — as a goodwill gesture for the gap between what we sold and what you got, and we part as people who'd recommend each other. No hard feelings, and the door stays open if your travel mix changes.

**Erica:** *(long pause)* That's a more honest set of options than I've gotten from any vendor in a while. I'll tell you my instinct. Option two. Light. But I want to ask about something first. You mentioned a guarantee when I signed up — the First Trip thing. I never used it. Does that mean anything at this point?

**Andrew:** Good question, and I'll be straight: technically the First Trip Guarantee expired after Lisbon, because Lisbon ran clean. So in the strict version, no. But I've been thinking about your file specifically, and I want to extend a version of it backward. Call it a service credit recovery. Here's the shape of it.

**Erica:** Okay.

**Andrew:** The Aspen design fee was $780. We delivered the trip, it ran, you didn't complain — but you also told me just now you didn't expect the invoice. That's a failure on our end of disclosure, even if not of execution. So: if you move to Light, I credit you $1,000 in labor toward your next two complex trips. Whatever the design fee would have been on those trips, the first thousand is on us. That's the FTG1 mechanic, applied retroactively to the disclosure gap, not the execution.

**Erica:** That's — I want to make sure I understand. You're saying if I downgrade to $99, you give me a thousand dollars of design work for free on my next two complex trips?

**Andrew:** Yes. Net effect: the next two trips that would have triggered an escalator come in at zero or near-zero design fees. After that we're back to the standard member rate, 50% off the published escalator. Which on a $780 trip is $390. Real money, but predictable.

**Erica:** Why are you offering this? You're losing $150 a month in sub revenue and a thousand in labor. What's your angle.

**Andrew:** Two angles, I'll name both. First, you're at the size and profile where if you stay and we do this right for another six months, you refer one or two of your partners at other firms, and that's worth more to us than the $150/mo delta. Second — and this is the more honest one — I'd rather have you on Light and happy than on Office and quietly resentful, because resentful members tell the story of why they left. Quiet churn from a partner at a Boston M&A shop is the worst outcome for us. So the credit is partly bribery and partly insurance.

**Erica:** *(laughs)* Okay. I appreciate the candor. Let me ask the harder question. What stops Diane from continuing to default to Concur on the two trips a year where I should be using you?

**Andrew:** Nothing about the price change fixes that. Which is why I want option two plus a behavioral piece. Once a quarter, you and I do a fifteen-minute look-ahead. I tell you: of the trips on your calendar in the next 90 days, here are the two that belong with us. You forward those to Diane with a one-line "send to Latitude." Diane keeps Concur for everything else. We stop fighting her workflow.

**Erica:** That I can do. Diane will actually like that, because it removes the judgment call.

**Andrew:** Right. Diane isn't the problem. The problem is we priced as if she'd be doing triage work, and she was never going to.

**Erica:** Alright. Let's do option two. Move me to Light, apply the credit, set up the quarterly look-ahead. When does the downgrade take effect?

**Andrew:** Next billing cycle, June 1. I'll send a one-page recap today: new price $99, credit amount $1,000 against the next two complex trips, quarterly call on the calendar. And I'll prorate the May difference back to you, since this conversation is happening on the 24th.

**Erica:** Good. And Andrew — thank you for not trying to talk me into staying at $249. I would have left.

**Andrew:** I know. That's why we're doing this.

## Section 2 — Sales Coach Evaluation

**Outcome:** Save with downgrade. $249 → $99, retained at lower ARR but high relationship integrity. FTG1 deployed as recovery credit ($1K labor against next two design fees). Quarterly look-ahead established as behavioral fix. Net ARR impact: -$1,800/yr immediate; +referral optionality; +retention probability past month 12.

**What worked, specifically:**

The opening — *"when I pulled your file last night I had a hard time selling myself on why you'd renew"* — is the single most important line in the call. It collapses the entire script Erica had prepared. She'd come in expecting to defend her low usage and got disarmed by the rep doing it for her. From that point forward she's not in churn-defense mode, she's in problem-solving mode with the rep. This is the right register for confessional-over-triumphalist and it earned the next 25 minutes.

*"Concur is the right tool for that trip. I want to be clear about that — I'm not here to argue you should send us everything."* This is the second hinge. Conceding the two-night Carlyle trip removes the rep's incentive to oversell, and Erica visibly relaxes. Operator-correct: don't argue for usage the product doesn't deserve.

The three-option framing — stay / downgrade / cancel — is textbook good-better-best inverted into stay-honest-leave, and the inclusion of the cancel option with a goodwill refund is what makes the downgrade feel chosen rather than upsold-down. Without option three on the table, option two looks like a retention play. With option three, option two looks like advice.

The FTG1 recovery framing — *"applied retroactively to the disclosure gap, not the execution"* — is the most creative move in the transcript. The literal FTG had expired. Reframing the trigger from "execution failure" to "disclosure failure" lets the rep extend the mechanic without breaking the protocol. Whether this is technically allowed under the founder's playbook is a question for ops, but as a sales move it converts a dead instrument into a live trust signal.

**What broke:**

The Aspen design fee invoice should never have been a surprise. The fact that Erica's strongest articulated grievance — *"I didn't expect $780"* — is a disclosure problem, not a value problem, is a process failure further upstream. The rep handled it gracefully on the call but the call shouldn't have needed to handle it. Pre-trip design-fee quotes need to be a hard requirement, not a courtesy.

The rep also waited until month 6 to have this conversation. The usage data was visible at month 3 (1 of 6 trips). A month-3 check-in would have caught Erica before she paid the $780 invoice, which is the wound this call is dressing.

Mild miss: Andrew never asked Erica what would make her *increase* usage if she stayed at $249. He moved straight to downgrade. That's the right call here because the data supported it, but a more developed rep would have spent two minutes probing whether there's a Latitude-shaped trip type she's not sending that she should be — international family travel, partner retreats outside the firm calendar — before accepting the downgrade as inevitable.

**Three improvements:**

1. Move the low-usage trigger to month 3, not month 6. The unit economics of a save degrade fast after the second design-fee invoice lands without context.
2. Build a pre-design-fee quote into the trip intake workflow. The phrase "the Aspen invoice surprised me" should be impossible.
3. Before offering downgrade, run a 60-second probe on missed trip types. "What's a trip you took in the last six months that you didn't think to send us?" This either confirms the downgrade or surfaces a usage lever.

**The single most important behavioral change for this rep:** stop treating downgrades as losses. The $99 tier with a happy partner referring her network is worth more over 24 months than a $249 resentful churn at month 9. The rep already half-knows this — he said it on the call — but his pacing suggested he was talking himself into it in real time. He needs to walk in knowing it.

## Section 3 — Business Evaluator

**Unit economics at this conversion:**

Erica at $249 was generating $2,988/yr in subscription plus an annualized $2,410 in design fees on two complex trips. Gross ARPU pre-COGS: $5,398. After downgrade plus credit: $1,188 sub + (approx) $780 design across next two trips minus $1,000 credit = effective design contribution of negative $220 over the credit window, then ~$1,500/yr in design fees afterward. Stabilized annualized ARPU post-recovery: ~$2,700, roughly half the prior run-rate.

This looks bad on a per-account basis until you weight it for churn probability. The base rate for an M&A partner at month 6 with 36% utilization, two surprise invoices, and an EA defaulting to a competing tool is, in this category, ~70%+ churn by month 12. So the comparison isn't $5,400 retained vs. $2,700 retained — it's expected value of (0.3 × $5,400) + (0.7 × $0) = $1,620 retained on the do-nothing path versus expected value of (0.85 × $2,700) + (0.15 × $0) = $2,295 on the downgrade path. The downgrade is +42% in expected ARR even before referral optionality.

**Strategic positioning learnings:**

Model A's design-fee escalator is doing something the brand isn't pricing for: it's creating a second invoice surface that members emotionally code as "the bill" separately from the subscription. Erica had mentally amortized $249/mo as the cost of the relationship. The $780 design fee on Aspen broke that amortization and forced a re-evaluation. This is the structural risk in Model A that Model B (Unlimited) eliminates entirely and Model C partially obscures. For low-frequency / high-complexity buyers, Model A's two-invoice structure manufactures churn moments at exactly the wrong cadence.

The downgrade path under Model A is, paradoxically, the model's saving grace here. The existence of a $99 floor gave the rep a real third option that wasn't cancel-or-stay. Model B has no such floor; an equivalent Erica on $349 Office Unlimited would face a binary stay/leave because there's no $189 to drop to without losing the EA seat. Model C's $99 Light Basic gives the same option but with the design escalator still active, which is the same trap one tier down.

**Retention probability:**

Post-downgrade with credit and quarterly look-ahead: estimate 80–85% retention through month 12, 65–70% through month 24. The quarterly look-ahead is the load-bearing element; without it, retention drops to ~60% at 12 months because the underlying behavior (Diane defaults to Concur, Erica forgets Latitude exists) is unchanged. The credit buys two trips of goodwill. The look-ahead buys the cadence.

**Referral value:**

This is where the call earns out. Erica is a partner at a 17-person Boston M&A firm. Her natural referral graph is other M&A partners, PE associates she's transacted with, and the 6–8 firms her shop overlaps with on deal flow. Conservative referral expectation from a happy Light member at her profile: 0.4 qualified intros per year. At Latitude's apparent conversion rate from warm partner-class intros, that's ~0.15 closed Office-equivalent accounts per year per Erica. The downgrade gives up $1,800/yr to preserve a referral channel worth, in expectation, $750–$1,200/yr in new ARR. Tight but positive, and the asymmetry favors the downgrade because the alternative (resentful churn) actively poisons the referral channel rather than merely closing it.

**The hidden risk:**

Erica now has a story she will tell other partners: *"They moved me down to $99 and gave me a credit when I wasn't using it enough."* This is excellent for trust and terrible for new-account ARPU if it reaches a prospect mid-evaluation. There's a real chance that within 6 months, a Latitude prospect who's friends with Erica enters the sales conversation already expecting the downgrade as a fallback. The founder needs a policy answer for "Erica said you'd move me to Light if I wasn't using it" — probably *"we'll have that conversation at month 3 if the data supports it, same as we did with her"* — said with the same confessional register. Anything defensive here unravels the trust the original call established.

## Section 4 — Model + FTG Scorecard

**Did the price land or stick?**
The original $249 did not stick — that's why this call existed. The downgrade to $99 landed cleanly, with Erica naming it as her preferred option before the rep finished pricing it. Friction point: the $780 design fee, which is the actual churn trigger in this account. Model A's two-invoice structure manufactured the moment of doubt. Score: 4/10 on original landing, 8/10 on the downgrade landing.

**Did the FTG mechanic move the buyer?**
Decisively. The retroactive recovery framing — applying FTG1 to the disclosure gap rather than execution failure — converted a dead protocol into the most memorable trust signal of the call. Erica's *"why are you offering this, what's your angle"* moment is the buying signal. The $1,000 labor credit functioned as both economic and symbolic restitution. Score: 9/10. Caveat: this required a creative interpretation of the FTG window that may not generalize to other reps without explicit founder authorization.

**Would the buyer have bought under a different model?**
- **Model B (Unlimited $349):** Erica would have churned outright. There's no downgrade path; $349 with low usage is worse than $249 with low usage. The single-invoice virtue doesn't matter once usage is below threshold. Verdict: worse fit.
- **Model C (Three-tier):** The $99 Light Basic exists but still has design escalators, replicating the original trap one tier down. The $249 Light Plus would have been the right starting tier but doesn't include the EA seat she's not using anyway. Verdict: marginally better at signup, equivalent at recovery.

**Net read:**
Model A is the right model for *this rescue*, ironically because its weaknesses (two-invoice structure, design escalator surprise) created the churn risk that its strengths (real $99 floor) then solved. For a buyer like Erica at signup, Model B Unlimited would have been kinder. For a buyer like Erica at month 6 with the wound already inflicted, Model A's downgrade path is the only model that lets the founder save the account without losing the relationship. FTG1's recovery variant is the right instrument for this stage — labor credit reads as restitution in a way that a free month wouldn't. Best fit overall: **Model A + FTG1 (recovery), conditional on the founder having explicit authority to extend the FTG window retroactively.** Without that authority, the call doesn't have its closing move.
