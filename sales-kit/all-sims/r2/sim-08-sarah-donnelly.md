# Sim 09 — Sarah Donnelly, COO, Series B Startup

## 1. Transcript

**Founder (F):** Sarah, thanks for the thirty minutes. Before I run my pitch — what made you take the call?

**Sarah Donnelly (SD):** Honestly? I'm tired. Our CEO decided 2026 is the year he meets every customer in person. We're a 65-person Series B, I run ops, and somehow I'm now booking his Tuesday flight to Austin and his Thursday connection to Denver while I'm trying to close a Series C deck. We use Brex for cards and Google Flights for the booking itself. It works, technically. It also means every trip lives in my head.

**F:** That's not a tool problem. That's an org-design problem you've patched with your calendar. How many people are traveling regularly?

**SD:** CEO, me, our CRO, our Head of Customer, and our VP Eng when he visits the Vancouver office. Five core. Maybe three more occasional. Two offices — Boston HQ and Vancouver.

**F:** Okay. Then I'm going to be direct: you're not the buyer for our $99 personal tier. You'd hate it. You'd be forwarding emails between five people and us, which is the job you're trying to stop doing. What you want is Atlas Office — $249 a month, covers up to five named travelers, one channel, one human on our side who knows all five profiles. Your CEO texts us. Your CRO texts us. You stop being CC'd.

**SD:** Okay. Hold on. Why are we not just buying Navan? They're built for this. They have a card, an app, a dashboard. My CFO will ask me in the first five minutes.

**F:** Fair question, and the honest answer is: if your CEO wants a self-serve OBT and your finance team wants a policy engine with auto-approvals, buy Navan. We don't compete with that. Navan is software. We're a desk. The thing Navan can't do is have a human at 11pm on a Sunday who already knows your CEO won't fly United through Newark and that your CRO needs an aisle on anything over two hours. We hold those preferences. We make the call. Navan makes you do it through a UI, faster than Google Flights, but still you doing it.

**SD:** So you're saying we might want both?

**F:** Some of our members run that way. Navan or Ramp for the card-and-policy layer, us for the actual booking and the 2am problems. We're not trying to be your expense system. We're trying to be the reason you stop being the travel coordinator.

**SD:** Mm. Okay. Walk me through what Atlas Office actually covers for five people.

**F:** One named principal on our side — call her your office's travel lead. She holds all five profiles: seat preferences, hotel programs, dietary, passport expirations, TSA numbers. Any of the five text or email a single address. First response inside 60 minutes, day, night, weekend, holiday. Design fees waived on every trip your members take. 24/7 coverage via our Toronto desk plus the Fora overnight pool, who have read-access to the profiles. Material decisions wait for your principal; the overnight pool acknowledges and holds inventory.

**SD:** What's a design fee and why does it matter that it's waived?

**F:** Complex trips — multi-city, group of four going to an offsite, anything over $5K in trip cost — non-members pay a planning fee, $250 and up. For your members, zero. Given your CEO's 2026 plan, you're going to hit that bracket often.

**SD:** Can you handle eight people across two offices? Be specific. I don't want a yes that turns into a no in March.

**F:** Atlas Office is built for five named travelers. For eight, two options. One: pick your five most-frequent and the other three book as guests under your account at non-member rates — we still do it, design fees apply on complex trips. Two: we quote you a custom seat for eight at $349 a month. I'd recommend starting with the five-seat plan and watching the actual trip volume from the other three for ninety days before paying for capacity you might not use. Two offices is irrelevant to us. We don't care where the traveler sleeps.

**SD:** Okay. Finance question. I have to model this. $249 a month, $2,988 a year, plus what?

**F:** Plus nothing on the member side for standard trips. Hotels pay us commission, which is disclosed on every booking — line item on the post-trip receipt. Air ticketing, when we issue it, has a $50 ticketing fee per ticket, also disclosed. No markup on rates. The receipt you get after every trip shows what was booked, what was captured as commission, and what goes on the expense report. Your finance team will like that more than what Google Flights gives them today, which is nothing.

**SD:** And contractually? If your principal quits or you flame out in month four, what's my recourse?

**F:** Monthly billing, no annual lock. Cancel any month with thirty days' notice and you stop paying. If the named principal leaves us, you get a replacement inside ten business days and the month is credited. We operate under Fora Travel — IATA-accredited host, not a two-person shop in a WeWork. That's the insurance under the insurance.

**SD:** Okay. I want to take this to my CEO. What I'd want is a way to try this without committing the company for a year.

**F:** I'll offer you a 90-day pilot. Same $249 a month, same five seats, full service. At day 75 we sit down for thirty minutes and look at what got booked, what got fixed, what the five members actually used. If you're not in, you walk at day 90 and we part well. If you're in, you roll month-to-month and we revisit the seat count.

**SD:** Can you do the pilot at $149?

**F:** No. The price holds. The concession is the pilot itself — we don't normally do a structured 75-day review, that's labor on our side. What I won't do is discount the headline, because then every member who paid full freight has a reason to email me. Same price, real exit ramp, real review.

**SD:** *(pause)* Fine. That's a fair answer. I'd rather you say no to discounting than wobble.

**F:** One last thing. Your CEO is going to ask you what this gets him that Google Flights doesn't. The honest answer is: at 11pm on a Sunday in Singapore when his connection is cancelled, he texts one number and a human he's met answers inside 60 minutes. He doesn't call you. That's the product.

**SD:** *(small laugh)* That's the product I'm buying. Send the pilot terms. I'll have an answer inside a week.

**F:** I'll have them in your inbox by tonight.

## 2. Outcome

**Conditional yes — closed with concession (90-day pilot, full price).** Sarah is taking it to her CEO with intent to sign. ~85% close probability inside 7 days.

---

## 3. What worked

- **"You're not the buyer for our $99 personal tier."** Killing the Light pitch in the founder's own mouth, before Sarah had to reject it, neutralized her walk-away trigger and bought trust for the rest of the call.
- **Navan rebuttal without trashing Navan.** "Navan is software. We're a desk." Concrete, non-defensive, acknowledged the legit use case for both. Sarah is operational; she respects a vendor who knows their lane.
- **Holding price, conceding structure.** "The price holds. The concession is the pilot itself." Sarah explicitly rewarded this — *"I'd rather you say no to discounting than wobble."* Round 1 lesson 9 landing exactly as designed.
- **The CEO-at-11pm-in-Singapore close.** Reframed the purchase from "another vendor" to "the reason I stop being on-call." That's the COO's actual pain.

## 4. What broke

- **The 8-person question got a slightly mushy answer.** "Two options... one... two..." was honest but felt like the founder hadn't thought it through. Sarah caught it ("be specific, I don't want a yes that turns into a no"). Should have led with the recommendation — *start at five, watch the other three, upgrade in 90 days* — not offered both and let her sort it.
- **Design fees explanation was reactive.** Sarah had to ask what a design fee was. For an Office buyer, fees should be surfaced up front as "and here's the thing that goes away for your members," not surfaced when she asks.
- **No reference offered.** Sarah is taking this to a CEO. The Reference Bench (Round 1 lesson 13) should have been deployed unprompted: "Before you take it to him, I'll connect you with the COO at [named Series B] who runs the same setup." Missed.
- **Didn't name the principal.** "Call her your office's travel lead" is abstract. For a vendor-relationship buyer, naming the actual human ("Your principal would be Maya — I'll introduce you Thursday") would have closed harder.

## 5. One improvement

**Build an "Office buyer" diagnostic branch.** The current playbook has a persona-band fork for frugal founders, PE MDs, and CFOs (Round 1 lesson 1). It needs a fourth: the **operational buyer who is buying for an exec team, not herself.** Her diagnostic questions are different — *who travels, how often, across how many entities, what's the current tool stack, who owns the vendor relationship internally* — and her close is different too: she needs ammunition for her CEO and her CFO, not for herself. The script should default to **naming the principal, surfacing the post-trip receipt early, and offering a reference call before she asks for one.** That converts "conditional yes" to "closed in the room."
