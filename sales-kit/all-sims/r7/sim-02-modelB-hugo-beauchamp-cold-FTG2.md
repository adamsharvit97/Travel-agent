## Section 2 — Sales Coach Evaluation

**Outcome:** Verbal close on Atlas Office Unlimited at $349/month, month-to-month, with a Friday onboarding call locked. No price haggling. The buyer asked for the agreement to be sent to his EA before the call ended. By any reasonable definition this is a clean cold-to-close inside ten minutes, which almost never happens and which I want to dissect carefully because the rep may be drawing the wrong lesson from it.

**What worked, specifically:**

The opening 30 seconds were operator-correct in exactly the way this persona requires. "I run a small travel office out of Toronto" is confessional, not triumphalist. It calibrates downward, which a VC partner instinctively trusts more than upward calibration. Hugo's first real reaction — "*$349. That's it.*" — is the moment the model B pricing did its work. The flatness of the number, combined with the rep's flatness of delivery ("That's it."), created a price-anchor moment where Hugo had to actively look for the catch. That is the correct dynamic for an Unlimited model.

The line "We're priced for members who do six to fifteen trips a year. If you do two trips a year we're expensive" is the single most important sales move in the entire call. It does three things at once: it qualifies the buyer (forcing him to surface his real trip volume), it pre-empts the buyer's-remorse risk by being honest about who shouldn't buy, and it implicitly raises the value anchor — Hugo immediately calculated himself into the bullseye. A weaker rep would have said "you're going to love this" and lost the moment.

The handling of FTG2 was unusually well-pitched. The rep refused to sell on the $500. "*It's a backstop, not a feature*" is the right register for this buyer. A VC partner has been pitched too many gimmicks; the rep correctly read that turning FTG2 into a marketing point would have cheapened the conversation. By framing the guarantee as *the rep's* asymmetric risk rather than the buyer's reward, he flipped the polarity of trust. Hugo's "*That's…generous. To the point of suspicious*" was a real objection, and "*we're a small desk, the only way I get a portfolio of partners like you is by absorbing the asymmetric risk on trip one*" was the correct disarm.

The IC comparison ("*if what you want is one person who knows your wife's name…an IC is the right shape*") was a textbook competitive concession. Conceding the place where you're weaker buys you credibility on the place where you're stronger.

**What broke:**

Two small things. First, the rep let Hugo set the SLA narrative ("*What happens at 3am when Delta cancels my flight*") rather than offering it proactively. In a 10-minute cold call with a partner-tier buyer, the founder-on-overnights story is one of the strongest differentiators against an IC and should have surfaced inside the first two minutes, not minute six. Second, the rep accepted "Pilar will find time" on the standing instructions call without naming three specific Friday slots in the call itself. He recovered by promising it in the follow-up — but locking calendar time inside the call is always better than promising to send slots.

**Three improvements:**

1. **Lead with the overnight coverage, not the price.** For a VC partner whose travel volume is bursty and time-zone-fractured, the operator-on-evenings line is the differentiator. The price is the proof, not the offer.
2. **Don't volunteer "month-to-month" until the buyer asks.** Hugo asked. Good. But if he hadn't asked, leading with "no annual lock-in" can read as defensive. Save it as a closer, which is what happened here by accident.
3. **Name the trip on the call.** "*Let's get Sun Valley on the desk*" should have been said by the rep, not the buyer. The rep waited for Hugo to surface Sun Valley as the first trip. Surfacing it himself ("*tell me about Sun Valley*" at minute three) would have moved the close 90 seconds earlier and put the founder in the driver's seat.

**The most important behavioral change:** The rep's instinct to under-claim is correct and rare. Keep doing it. But under-claiming requires that he proactively surface the differentiators the buyer hasn't asked about yet — coverage, overnight, the EA companion seat. He has the right register; he needs to use it on offense, not only on defense.

## Section 3 — Business Evaluator

**Unit economics at this conversion:**

Atlas Office Unlimited at $349/month produces $4,188 of ARR per member with zero design-fee upside. At 8-10 real trips per year for this persona, plus personal travel for spouse and occasional partner travel, expected fulfillment cost lands around $1,400-$1,900/year in VA hours (assuming a fully-loaded PHT VA cost of ~$11/hr and 130-170 fulfillment hours per member-year for a member in this bullseye). Gross contribution per member: roughly $2,300-$2,800/year, or ~55-67% gross margin. This is materially worse per-member economics than Model A would have produced on this exact buyer, where the design escalator on Tokyo-style trips and one-off Sun Valley would likely have added $1,500-$3,500 of incremental design revenue.

But — and this is the entire strategic question — *that revenue almost certainly does not exist under Model A for this buyer*. Hugo explicitly said his previous IC's $1,800 Tokyo design fee was the moment he started looking for alternatives. Under Model A, the same Tokyo trip generates a $1,200-$2,500 design fee at this trip complexity, which trips the same wound. The rational buyer behavior is then to either route complex trips outside Latitude or invoke the FTG. Either path destroys the relationship. Model B's lower per-trip margin is the price of *getting the trips in the first place* and of *retaining the member past trip three*.

**Strategic positioning:**

Model B is the right model for the VC/PE partner segment specifically because the buyer's price-sensitivity is inverted: they are insensitive to the *level* of the fee and acutely sensitive to the *predictability* of the fee. Hugo's "*I'm not actually price-sensitive on $4K*" combined with "*the last person who told me a flat number was lying*" is the segment in one breath. Model A optimizes for revenue per trip; Model B optimizes for trips-on-the-desk. For a $300K T&E buyer with 20-25 flights a year, trips-on-the-desk is the only metric that compounds.

The $349 number itself does important work that $249 cannot do for this buyer. At $249, Hugo's instinct would have been "this is cheap, what am I missing." At $349, the price reads as serious-but-not-luxury — a category he trusts. The $100 step from Office to Office Unlimited is also a useful anchor: it telegraphs that the design-included version is the *premium* product, which a partner-tier buyer self-selects into without resistance.

**Retention probability:**

High, conditional on trip one. The structural retention risk in Model B is the inverse of Model A's: under Model A, members churn when the design fee bills exceed expectations. Under Model B, members churn when their usage drops below the break-even of $4,188/year. For Hugo at 8-10 trips, churn risk in year one is low (estimated 12-15%). Churn risk in year two depends entirely on whether the spouse-travel and partner-travel volume materializes as promised in this call. If Pilar successfully adds Italy and one partner adds a trip per quarter, lifetime expected value lands at 4-6 years, $17-25K LTV.

**Referral value:**

Above average. Hugo mentioned Marc by name; Marc referred him. The VC/GP network has unusually high referral density and unusually high referral conversion (a warm GP referral closes at roughly 40-50% versus cold inbound at 8-12%). One Hugo properly served is worth, conservatively, 1.5-2.5 net new members in the following 18 months. The strategic asset is not Hugo's ARR — it's Hugo's willingness to mention Latitude in the GP group chat unprompted, which only happens if trip one is operator-correct.

**The hidden risk:**

Hugo's last line — "*don't surprise me*" — is the hidden risk made explicit. Partner-tier buyers will tolerate a service failure if they're informed in real time. They will not tolerate a service failure they learn about from their EA or, worse, from the airport. FTG2's structure (refund + free month + $500) is irrelevant to this risk. The actual risk-mitigation is the standing instructions architecture and the proactive-notification discipline of the desk. If Latitude has a process failure where the *desk knows* something is wrong but doesn't tell the member fast enough, FTG2 does not buy the relationship back. This is a process risk, not a pricing risk, and the founder needs to ensure the desk's escalation protocol is operationally robust before Sun Valley, not after.

## Section 4 — Model + FTG Scorecard

**Did the price land?** Yes, cleanly. The $349/month for Atlas Office Unlimited registered as serious-but-not-luxury, which is the precise register a $300K-T&E partner trusts. Hugo's "*$349. That's it.*" was a price-anchor moment, not a price-objection moment. No friction on the absolute number; the only friction was on the *credibility* of flat pricing, which is a believability problem the rep handled with the "two-trips-a-year is expensive, twelve-trips-a-year is a steal" honesty. The $349 number is right-sized for this buyer in a way $249 (Model A Office) would not be — at $249 Hugo's reflex would have been suspicion of under-pricing.

**Did FTG2 move the buyer?** Helpful, not decisive. Hugo's own words: "*That's the line that's going to make me say yes, by the way. Not the $500.*" The line he meant was *month-to-month, no annual lock-in*. FTG2's generosity registered as suspicious before it registered as reassuring; the rep had to actively *disarm* the guarantee by framing it as his own asymmetric risk. For this buyer, FTG2 is over-engineered. The $500 service credit was noise. The free-month component did some work. The refund was assumed. A simpler guarantee (FTG1 or FTG3) would have landed identically.

**Would the buyer have bought under a different model?**
- Model A ($99/$249, design escalator): Would have closed cold, churned on trip two when the Tokyo-style design fee hit. Net negative.
- Model C ($99/$249/$499): Would have closed at $499 Office, but the three-tier ladder would have cost 60 seconds of explanation in a 10-minute call. Probably also a yes, but with more friction.
- Model B is the correct fit. Confidence: high.

**Net read:** Model B + FTG1 (or even FTG3) would have been the optimal combination. Model B + FTG2 is over-insured for the VC partner segment. Save FTG2's generosity for buyers who are actually price-sensitive — the SMB founders in Stage 5, not the partners in Stage 1.
