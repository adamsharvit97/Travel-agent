## Section 2 — SALES COACH EVALUATION (~700 words)

**Outcome:** Close on Atlas Office at $499/month, annual, with FTG2 attached to a named first trip (Marcus, Frankfurt, June 8). Six-thousand-dollar ARR, ~5% of stated T&E budget. Doug — the actual economic buyer — closed himself in the final exchange.

**What worked, specifically.**

The opener that named the wrong tier first ("I'm going to tell you which one I think is wrong for you") was the most consequential move in the call. It accomplished three things at once: it inoculated against the cheap-tier reflex, it positioned Arvit as someone diagnosing rather than selling, and it gave Brett a frame for what he was about to hear Doug say. By the time Doug asked "why not the two-forty-nine," Brett had already heard the rebuttal once. The seller pre-loaded the buyer's deputy.

The Marcus/Houston pivot — "The Houston miss wasn't a Doug problem. It was a Marcus problem" — was the line that did the actual selling. It reframed Plus tier from "cheaper option" to "wrong instrument." Once Brett heard "Plus is a single-traveler product. You don't have one outlier. You have a firm," the Plus tier was dead. He stopped defending it. The seller then repeated this exact frame verbatim when Doug joined, which is the right move — Doug wasn't there for round one, and Arvit didn't paraphrase, he replayed.

The FTG2 delivery was unusually disciplined. Arvit didn't sell the guarantee, he sequenced it after Doug's "what if you blow it" question. That ordering matters. A guarantee offered before the buyer has asked about risk reads as a sales tool. A guarantee offered in response to a risk question reads as a contract clause. The line "the structure of the guarantee says we'd rather lose money than have you leave quietly" was the strongest single sentence in the call — it converted FTG2's mechanics into a statement about the seller's intent.

Naming the trip ("Frankfurt June 8") was the closing move. The buyer turned the guarantee from an abstract policy into a specific bet on a specific Tuesday. That's the moment the deal closed; the paperwork is admin.

**What broke.**

Two things. First, the "five-ninety-nine, sorry, four-ninety-nine" slip from Brett was a tell that the price was anchoring uncomfortably even after the Houston cost was acknowledged. Arvit didn't address it — he moved on. A better move would have been to pause and let Brett name the discomfort out loud, then resolve it.

Second, the comparison to thirty-day money-back guarantees was reactive — Brett raised it, Arvit responded well, but the seller should have pre-empted. Brett's frame ("other shops do thirty-day money-back") suggests he had shopped competitors between calls one and two. Arvit didn't ask who else he was looking at. That's a missed discovery item that should have surfaced in the first three minutes.

**Three improvements.**

1. When the buyer slips on a price ("five-ninety-nine, sorry, four-ninety-nine"), pause and name it. "You said five-ninety-nine. What's the version of this where four-ninety-nine still feels like five?" Surfaces the real objection.
2. Open call two with a competitive sweep: "Between Tuesday and today, did you talk to anyone else?" Two minutes. Changes the rest of the call.
3. The three-tier walk-through was clean but mechanical. Next time, ask the buyer to name which tier they think fits before presenting the recommendation. Forces them to commit to a mental model you can then correct, rather than presenting one for them to react against.

**Most important behavioral change for this rep:** Slow down on price reactions. Arvit talks past discomfort because he's confident in the product. The Brett slip was a one-second window into the buyer's real anxiety, and Arvit closed it instead of opening it. The deal closed anyway — but in a tougher room, that missed beat is the difference between a close and a "let me think about it." Treat every price slip, sigh, or repeated number as a discovery question in disguise.

## Section 3 — BUSINESS EVALUATOR (~700 words)

**Unit economics at this conversion.** Holloran closes at $499/month, $5,988 ARR. Against Latitude 43's cost structure — Toronto-based EA pod plus founder coverage — a single Atlas Office account at this profile is gross-margin-positive from month one, assuming an average trip-design load of 12-18 itineraries per month across active travelers. Marcus's international load is the variable that matters: a Frankfurt trip with multi-leg routing, two hotels, restaurant holds, and ground coverage absorbs roughly 2-3 hours of desk time. Across an estimated 60-80 trips per year for this firm, total desk hours land between 90 and 130 hours annually, or roughly $30-40/hour of revenue per service hour at fully loaded cost. That's healthy but not extraordinary — the unit economics improve materially only if Holloran sits at the lower end of trip volume or if Sophie's EA seat absorbs the routine bookings rather than escalating them.

**Strategic positioning learnings.** Three.

First, Model C's three-tier structure functioned as intended *in this specific room*. The $99 tier did the job of legitimizing the price ladder without being a serious option, the $249 tier was the foil that made $499 feel chosen rather than imposed, and the $499 tier carried the actual value proposition. This only worked because the seller killed the $249 option explicitly. If Arvit had let Plus tier hang as a live option, Doug would have anchored there. The three-tier ladder is a powerful tool but it requires the seller to be the one who removes options, not the buyer.

Second, $499 is the right ceiling for an 8-person M&A boutique with travel-heavy partners. The 5%-of-T&E ratio is psychologically defensible and the per-traveler math (~$62/seat/month equivalent at full activation) reads as reasonable relative to what a single Concur miss costs in partner time. Below $499, Latitude 43 leaves margin on the table for this segment. Above $499, the conversation shifts to RFP and procurement, which is a different sale entirely.

Third, FTG2 is structurally well-matched to the M&A buyer specifically. M&A partners think in deal-failure terms — what happens when this goes wrong, who pays, how do we cure. FTG2's three-part remedy (refund + free month + service credit) reads to that buyer as a properly drafted indemnity clause rather than a marketing promise. A money-back guarantee (FTG3) would have read as consumer-grade and undermined the operator-correct register. FTG1's labor credit would have read as adequate but transactional. FTG2 was the right instrument for this room.

**Retention probability.** Moderate-to-high, contingent on Frankfurt. The named-trip mechanism is doing double duty here: it's both a risk reversal *and* a focusing mechanism that tells the buyer exactly what to evaluate at month two. If Marcus's June 8 trip runs clean, retention probability through month twelve is around 75%. If Frankfurt has any wobble — even a wobble the guarantee covers — retention drops to roughly 45%, because Doug's sixty-day check-in becomes a referendum rather than a review. The single biggest retention lever is not the desk's quality average; it's whether Sophie adopts the EA seat actively in the first thirty days. Sophie's reflex to default back to Concur is the predictable failure mode and it should be the desk's explicit onboarding focus.

**Referral value.** High, and underused in this sale. Brett is a Charlotte-based M&A partner with peers at Robinson Bradshaw, McGuireWoods's M&A bench, and the Carolina PE LP community. One closed Holloran referral is worth, conservatively, two warm Charlotte intros. Arvit did not ask about referral mechanics or community fit in this call. That's an asset on the table.

**The hidden risk.** Doug. Doug closed himself in the room but he closed under partial information — he didn't actually walk through what happens if he, personally, has a low-travel month. The $499 tier is justified by *firm-level* utilization, not Doug's individual usage. In month four, when Doug has flown twice and seen one $499 invoice land, he will ask Brett whether they're getting value. That conversation happens without Arvit in the room. The mitigant is a structured month-three usage report showing firm-wide trip volume, time saved, and exceptions caught — not Doug's personal trip count. If Arvit doesn't build that report into the onboarding plan, Doug becomes the churn risk in month five.

## Section 4 — MODEL + FTG SCORECARD (~320 words)

**Did the price land or stick?** It landed, but not without friction. Brett's "five-ninety-nine, sorry, four-ninety-nine" slip is the diagnostic moment — the number was sticky on first hearing. The price was rescued by two specific mechanisms: the 5%-of-T&E reframe ("six grand on a one-twenty budget") and the explicit naming of Plus tier as the *wrong* answer. Without the explicit kill of the $249 option, Doug would have anchored there on arrival. Friction score: real but managed. **7/10.**

**Did the FTG mechanic move the buyer?** Decisively. Doug's "that's generous" was the FTG2 inflection point — it shifted the conversation from price-defense to trust-construction. The three-part remedy (refund + free month + $500 credit) read as properly indemnified to an M&A buyer who thinks in cure-period terms. The "we'd rather lose money than have you leave quietly" line was the single most persuasive sentence in the call. **9/10.**

**Would the buyer have bought under a different model?** Probably yes under Model B Unlimited ($349) — but at lower confidence and with a longer close cycle. Doug would have pushed harder against the price because the gap between Light ($189) and Office ($349) is smaller and the foil is less effective. Under Model A v2.0 ($249 ceiling), Holloran would have closed faster but Latitude 43 would have left $250/month of margin on the table for a firm that can clearly absorb $499. Model C did the right thing for this room: it sorted the firm into its correct tier.

**Net read.** Model C + FTG2 was the correct combination for this buyer. The three-tier ladder created *empowerment, not paralysis*, because the seller did the work of killing two of the three options before the buyer had to. FTG2 outperformed FTG1 and FTG3 in this room because M&A buyers read multi-part remedies as serious and single-clause guarantees as light. **Combined fit: 9/10.**
