# R7 Sim 12 — Model C (Three-tier $99/$249/$499) + FTG3 (30-day money-back)

**Stage:** Negotiation
**Buyer:** Tomasz Werner, 35, PE associate at $2.4B fund, $80K T&E (continuing arc from R7 Sims 10 + 11)
**Founder:** Latitude 43 founder (Toronto, hosted under Fora)
**Setup:** Tomasz has been through discovery (Sim 10) and a follow-up working session (Sim 11). He has seen the three-tier card. He is now actively negotiating. He wants Plus at Basic pricing, or Plus at $199 locked for 12 months.

## Section 1: TRANSCRIPT (~1,500 words)

**Tomasz:** Okay, so I sat with the deck overnight. Here's where I am. I see Basic at $99, Plus at $249, Office at $499. I want Plus. I'm not a 5-traveler team, I'm one guy who travels twelve, fifteen times a year. So Office is wrong. But Basic doesn't include trip design, which is the whole reason I'm calling you. So I'm in Plus. The number is $249. I'll be honest — $249 a month feels like a stretch for what is, on paper, one person's travel. Can I get the Plus product on Basic pricing? Or — and this is more reasonable, I think — $199 a month for Plus, locked for 12 months. That's $2,388. That's a serious commitment from me. What can you do.

**Founder:** Appreciate you putting it that directly. Let me say what I can and can't do, and then let me push back on one thing. Plus at $99 — I can't. That's just not a real number. The $99 tier exists for a specific buyer, and it isn't you. The $199 for 12 months — I want to walk through that with you, because I think it's the wrong shape of deal even though I understand why you're proposing it. Can we do that?

**Tomasz:** Go ahead.

**Founder:** So first, what Basic actually is. Basic at $99 is a booking desk. You email, we book. We hold your loyalty numbers, your seat preferences, your standing instructions. If something breaks at 2 a.m., we're on it inside 60 minutes. That's $99. What Basic is *not* — it doesn't include trip design. So when you say "I want to go to Lisbon and Porto in October, here's what I care about, build me the trip" — that work, on Basic, gets quoted under our design fee escalator. $250 to $5,000 depending on complexity. Members get 50% off, but it's still a line item every time.

**Tomasz:** Right. That's the part I don't want.

**Founder:** Exactly. So Plus at $249 isn't Basic plus a logo. Plus is: trip design is *included* on member trips. Every trip. No escalator, no surprise invoice. That's the whole product difference. The reason the gap between Basic and Plus is $150 is because that $150 is paying for design work that, if billed à la carte, would be $250 to $5,000 a *trip*. You travel twelve to fifteen times a year. If even three of those are real design trips — Lisbon-Porto, a ski week, a board offsite — you've already burned through more in Basic-plus-escalator than you'd pay in Plus.

**Tomasz:** I hear that. But you're assuming I do three designed trips. Two of my fifteen trips a year are designed. The rest are New York, Boston, Chicago — book a flight, book a hotel, done.

**Founder:** Fair. So let's run that math honestly. Thirteen booking-only trips a year — Basic handles those at $99. Two designed trips a year — under Basic, that's likely $1,500 to $4,000 in design fees, member-discounted to $750 to $2,000. Add that to $99 x 12 = $1,188. Your all-in on Basic is roughly $1,900 to $3,200. Plus is $2,988. So you're right — the gap is real, it's $200 to $1,000 a year depending on trip complexity. That's the actual decision.

**Tomasz:** So why would I do Plus.

**Founder:** Two reasons. One is friction. On Basic, every designed trip starts with a quote conversation — "this one looks like $1,800, do you want to proceed." You have to decide twice: do I want the trip, do I want to pay for the design. On Plus, you just say "build it." That's a behavioral difference, not a financial one, and for some people it's worth $40 a month and for some it isn't. Two is predictability. Plus is one number on your card. Basic plus escalator is a variable expense your finance team has to chase. You're at a PE fund, your T&E is $80K, you're not budget-constrained — but you do have to code expenses and you do have an auditor.

**Tomasz:** Mm. The coding thing is real. Last year I had eleven different vendor lines for travel. Okay. So I hear you on Plus. I still want to negotiate the price. $199 for 12 months. I'm signing a year. That's worth something to you.

**Founder:** It is worth something. Let me tell you what it's worth and what it isn't. A 12-month commitment from you is worth — call it three months of reduced churn risk on my side. That's real, I don't dismiss it. What it *isn't* worth is $50 a month off the published price. Because if I do $199 for you, I do $199 for the next PE associate who asks, and the next, and within a quarter Plus is a $199 product. The list price isn't a starting bid. It's the price.

**Tomasz:** That's the line every salesperson says.

**Founder:** It is. And in my case it's also true, because I'm small enough that you can see my book. I have — I'll just tell you — I have under 30 Plus members right now. If three of them are at $199 and twenty-seven are at $249, that's a problem the first time two of them compare notes at a dinner. So I can't.

**Tomasz:** Okay. What *can* you do.

**Founder:** Three things. First, the 30-day money-back guarantee on the membership. You pay $249 for month one. If at day 29 you look at it and say "this isn't what I thought," you email me, I refund the $249. No questions, no exit interview, no clawback on anything I built for you in that month. That's not a marketing line, that's how it works. So the real risk of saying yes today is zero dollars and one month of your attention.

**Tomasz:** 30 days isn't long enough to know.

**Founder:** It's long enough to do one real trip. Which is the test. If the first trip lands the way I'm telling you it'll land, you'll know. If it doesn't, you're out. The 30 days is calibrated to the trip cycle, not to the calendar.

**Tomasz:** Okay. What's the second thing.

**Founder:** Second — I'll waive the first month's design fee escalator if you start on Basic. So if you want to try Basic at $99 first, do one designed trip in the first 30 days with no design fee, and then if Plus makes sense you upgrade. You'd be paying $99 instead of $249 for month one, and the design trip is free as a try-it. That's a real concession — it's worth $750 to $2,000 in fees waived.

**Tomasz:** That's interesting. That's actually interesting.

**Founder:** Third — if you sign Plus today at $249 and stay 12 months, on month 13 I'll either hold your price flat or, if we've raised the list, you stay at $249. So you get price protection forward, not backward. That's the version of a "12-month deal" I can do without breaking the book.

**Tomasz:** So to be clear. You won't do $199. You won't do Plus at Basic price. You will do — Basic at $99 with one design trip free in month one, or Plus at $249 with a 30-day money-back and a price-hold on renewal.

**Founder:** That's the menu.

**Tomasz:** And if I do Basic and the design trip is great, what stops me from just staying on Basic and paying the escalator for the two designed trips a year. Why would I upgrade.

**Founder:** Honestly? Maybe nothing. If two designed trips a year is your real number and you don't mind the per-trip quote conversation, Basic might just be your tier. I'm not going to tell you Plus is right for everyone. The reason I'm pointing you at Plus isn't margin — it's that in discovery you said you wanted to stop thinking about travel. Basic still has thinking in it. Plus doesn't. That's the call.

**Tomasz:** *(pause)* Yeah. That's the call. Okay. Plus. $249. 30-day money-back, price hold on renewal. Let's do it.

**Founder:** Done. I'll send the agreement in an hour. One ask — name the first trip on the call today, so we start.

**Tomasz:** Lisbon and Porto. Second week of October. Wife and I. Six nights.

**Founder:** Open the desk. Talk Friday.

## Section 2: SALES COACH EVALUATION (~700 words)

**Outcome:** Closed Plus at list ($249), 30-day money-back honored, price-hold on month 13. No discount given. First trip named on the call. Strong close.

**What worked specifically.**

The founder did the single most important thing a negotiation rep can do: he refused to negotiate price and instead renegotiated *what was being compared*. Tomasz opened with "Plus at Basic pricing or $199 for 12 months." The founder didn't counter with $229 or $219. He pulled the conversation back to *what Basic actually is versus what Plus actually is*. Cite the line: "Plus at $249 isn't Basic plus a logo. Plus is: trip design is included on member trips. Every trip." That sentence is the whole sale. It reframes the $150 gap from "more money for the same thing" to "this $150 buys you a different product."

Second, the math moment. When Tomasz pushed back ("two of my fifteen trips are designed, not three"), the founder did not retreat. He *ran Tomasz's actual numbers out loud*: thirteen booking trips, two designed, $1,900 to $3,200 on Basic-plus-escalator versus $2,988 on Plus. That gap of $200 to $1,000 is not a slam dunk for Plus on pure dollars — and the founder *named that*. He conceded the dollar comparison was close. Then he won on a non-financial axis: friction and predictability. "On Plus, you just say 'build it.'" Conceding the weaker argument to win the stronger one is advanced behavior. Most reps would have fudged the math.

Third, the FTG3 deployment. "The real risk of saying yes today is zero dollars and one month of your attention." This is FTG3 doing exactly the job it's designed to do — collapse the decision from "should I commit" to "should I try." When Tomasz pushed back that 30 days isn't long enough, the founder had the right rebuttal ready: "It's long enough to do one real trip. Which is the test." He tied the guarantee to the *product cycle*, not the calendar. That's the right framing.

Fourth — and this is the line I'd highlight for any sales hire — "The list price isn't a starting bid. It's the price." Delivered without apology, followed immediately by transparency about *why*: "if I do $199 for you, I do $199 for the next PE associate who asks." Showing the buyer your book is a power move when your book is small. Tomasz is sophisticated enough to respect the math on the founder's side.

**What broke.**

The founder gave a concession menu that included Basic-with-free-design-trip. That's a fine fallback but it created a real risk: Tomasz almost stayed on Basic. The closing exchange ("what stops me from just staying on Basic") was the riskiest moment in the call, and the founder won it by going to a *non-financial* argument ("you said you wanted to stop thinking about travel"). But he could have lost it. The Basic option, once offered, was hard to take back.

Also, the price-hold on month 13 was a soft concession. It costs the founder nothing today but it does signal that list price is not entirely fixed forward. A more disciplined version would have either offered it unconditionally to all members (making it a feature, not a concession) or not offered it at all.

**Three improvements.**

1. **Don't offer Basic-with-free-design as a fallback.** It's a downsell disguised as a concession. If Plus is the right product, hold there. The 30-day money-back already covers the "try it" need.
2. **Make the price-hold a feature, not a deal point.** Put it on the website. Then it's not a concession — it's just how the business works.
3. **Name the trip earlier.** The founder asked at the very end. Asking at the moment Tomasz said "okay Plus" is fine, but anchoring on the actual Lisbon-Porto trip earlier in the call would have made the dollar comparison more vivid ("for this Lisbon trip specifically, design on Basic is $1,200, on Plus it's included").

**Most important behavioral change for this rep:** Stop ending negotiations with a "menu." Menus invite shopping. End with a single recommendation framed as the answer to the buyer's real problem. The founder almost did this — he just gave Tomasz one too many doors.

## Section 3: BUSINESS EVALUATOR (~700 words)

**Unit economics implications at this conversion.**

Tomasz closed at $249/mo Plus. ARR contribution: $2,988. Assume gross margin on Plus is 55-60% after VA labor, founder time on design, and Fora split — call it $1,700/yr contribution margin per Plus member. The price-hold concession on month 13 caps future ARPU expansion from this account at zero for one year, which is a real but bounded cost (estimated $300-600 in foregone price lift assuming a 10-20% list increase in 2027).

The critical unit-economic observation: **the three-tier ladder works in this negotiation precisely because the middle tier had defensible feature differentiation**. The $150 gap between Basic and Plus isn't arbitrary — it's the design-fee inclusion, which has real cost basis (founder + Fora design time per trip). When the founder explained that gap, he wasn't doing marketing; he was doing accounting. That's defensible against a buyer who knows how to ask.

Compare to Model A (two-tier $99/$249): in Model A, Tomasz has nowhere to anchor downward except $99. The Basic-Plus comparison is starker — $99 is clearly a booking desk, $249 is clearly a service. In Model C, the $499 Office tier *helps* the founder by making $249 look like the reasonable middle, but it also *gives Tomasz ammunition* — "I should be in the middle, not the top." The founder handled this well because Tomasz wasn't actually arguing for Office discount; he was arguing for Plus discount. Model C's risk is a different buyer who tries to negotiate Office down to Plus pricing — that fight would be harder, because the Office tier's incremental value (5 travelers, EA Companion Seat) is more abstract.

**Strategic positioning learnings.**

1. **Three-tier ladders give the founder a "reasonable middle" anchor but cost him the ability to use $99 as a real entry point.** Tomasz used $99 as a negotiation reference ("Plus at Basic pricing") even though $99 is structurally a different product. In Model B (Unlimited $189/$349), this comparison can't happen — there is no $99 to point at.
2. **The middle tier must be feature-defensible, not feature-implied.** "Plus includes design" is defensible. "Plus has better support" would not be. The founder won because the feature gap is concrete.
3. **FTG3's simplicity is a negotiation asset, not a liability.** Tomasz tested it once ("30 days isn't long enough") and the founder had a clean answer ("it's calibrated to the trip cycle"). FTG1 (month + labor credit) would have triggered a longer back-and-forth on what counts as "labor"; FTG2 (refund + free month + credit) would have invited "can I have the free month upfront." FTG3's "no questions asked" cuts through complex negotiation talk because there's nothing to negotiate.

**Retention probability.**

High. Tomasz negotiated hard, was told no twice, and closed at list. Buyers who close at list after a real negotiation are *more* retained, not less, because they've internalized the value rationale. The Lisbon-Porto trip becomes the proof point. If that trip lands, Tomasz is a 24-month member. Probability of cancellation in month 1 under FTG3: low (15-20%) — he's invested in the negotiation outcome.

**Referral value.**

High and specific. Tomasz is a PE associate at a $2.4B fund. His peer set is 8-15 other associates and 3-5 partners at the fund, plus a broader network of 30-50 PE/VC peers. He is exactly the buyer whose referral converts a category. The referral risk is the inverse of the win: if Tomasz tells a peer he got Plus at $249 with a 30-day refund, that peer arrives expecting the same deal — which is fine, because that *is* the deal. No "friends and family" discount means the referral conversation is clean.

**Hidden risk.**

The Basic-with-free-design fallback. Tomasz didn't take it, but the founder *offered* it, which means it's now in the founder's repertoire. The next negotiation, he'll offer it again. Within five negotiations, some buyer will take it, and that buyer is now in the wrong tier with a free design trip already burned. That's a margin leak that doesn't show up for two quarters. The fix is to remove that concession from the playbook entirely — Plus or no deal, with FTG3 as the safety net. Don't downsell to Basic at the negotiation table.

Second hidden risk: the price-hold on renewal. If granted as a one-off, it's a CRM data-hygiene problem. If granted to everyone, it's a strategic ceiling on 2027 pricing power.

## Section 4: MODEL + FTG SCORECARD (~300 words)

**Did the price land or stick?** Stuck. $249 closed at list. Two friction moments: (1) opening ask for Plus-at-Basic-price, dispatched by the founder reframing the feature gap as a different product, not a discount; (2) mid-call ask for $199-locked-12-months, dispatched by transparency on book size ("I have under 30 Plus members"). Real risk moment: the Basic-with-free-design fallback nearly redirected the deal downward. Founder recovered by anchoring on Tomasz's discovery quote ("you said you wanted to stop thinking about travel"). 8/10 on price discipline; -2 for offering the downsell at all.

**Did the FTG mechanic move the buyer?** Helpful, not decisive. FTG3's "30-day money-back, no questions" did exactly what it's designed for: collapsed the commitment from a 12-month bet to a one-month test. Tomasz pushed back once ("30 days isn't long enough") and the founder tied it to trip cycle, not calendar — clean save. FTG3's simplicity held up under negotiation pressure better than FTG1 or FTG2 would have, because there was nothing to negotiate inside the guarantee itself. 7/10.

**Would the buyer have bought under a different model?**
- **Model A ($99/$249):** Yes, probably faster — no $499 tier to argue around, cleaner two-option choice. But also less margin headroom; Tomasz might have anchored harder on $99.
- **Model B ($189/$349 Unlimited):** Maybe. $349 would have been a harder sell to Tomasz (one-traveler PE associate), but the "no escalator ever" simplicity matches his "stop thinking about travel" need. Coin flip.
- **Model C (this sim):** Worked, but the three-tier ladder gave Tomasz negotiation ammunition that a two-tier model wouldn't have. The founder needed to be sharper than under Model A.

**Net read:** Model C is *defensible* in negotiation but *harder* in negotiation. For a sophisticated buyer who will compare tiers, Model B is the cleaner offer. For Tomasz specifically — sophisticated, price-sensitive, asks the right questions — Model A would have been the easier close at equivalent revenue. Model C wins the long game (Office tier creates a 5-traveler expansion path Tomasz may grow into) but pays for it in negotiation friction today. FTG3 is the right pairing — its simplicity is the antidote to the model's complexity. **Recommendation: Model B + FTG3 remains the cleanest pairing for this buyer profile; Model C + FTG3 is viable but demands a more disciplined rep.**
