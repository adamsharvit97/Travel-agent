**Founder:** Vanessa, thanks for blocking 45 minutes. I'd rather use the time than rush. Before I show you anything, can I ask you to walk me through how travel actually moves through your office right now? Not the policy version — the real version.

**Vanessa:** Sure. So I have an EA, Marguerite, eight years. She books most of mine through Concur and a Delta corporate code. **Founder:** And the $150K T&E — how's that split, roughly?

**Vanessa:** Maybe sixty percent is mine. The conferences eat another fifteen. The juniors are the rest. It's grown about twenty-two percent a year since '23.

**Founder:** Okay. And what triggered you taking this call? You replied to Tom's intro a week ago — what was the actual moment?

**Vanessa:** Honest answer? Marguerite was out for her daughter's wedding. I had to rebook a Charleston-to-Aspen leg myself at ten at night and I sat there for forty minutes trying to figure out if I should pay the change fee or eat the segment. I'm a fiduciary. I do this for a living for other people's money. And I could not make a clean decision about my own travel at ten p.m. That bothered me.

**Founder:** That's the right reason to be looking. Let me reflect something back and you tell me if it's wrong. You have three different travel patterns in one firm. You — high-frequency, high-stakes, prospect-facing. Senior advisors — variable, two different competence levels. Juniors — low-frequency, mostly process. One EA covering all of it. That fair?

**Vanessa:** That's exactly it. And Marguerite is fantastic at me. She is okay at the seniors. The juniors basically self-serve.

**Founder:** Good. That actually matters for what I'm going to show you, because I don't want to sell you one product when you have three problems. Let me walk you through how we're structured and then we'll figure out which piece fits where. Three tiers. I'll go slow.

**Vanessa:** Please.

**Founder:** Tier one — Atlas Light Basic. Ninety-nine dollars a month. One named traveler. You get access to the desk for booking — flights, hotels, ground. Design work — meaning we plan a trip, build the itinerary, handle the logistics for something more complex than a point-to-point — that's billed separately on an escalator. Five hundred for a simple multi-city, up to five thousand for something with multiple parties and ground coordination. Members pay fifty percent of that. No First Trip Guarantee at this tier — it's a booking utility, not a relationship.

**Vanessa:** That sounds like what a junior advisor would use.

**Founder:** That's exactly who it's for. Tier two — Atlas Light Plus. Two forty-nine a month. Still one named traveler. But design work on member trips is included. No escalator on anything you'd reasonably do in a year. First Trip Guarantee applies. That's the tier for someone like you personally if you only wanted to put yourself on the desk and leave the rest of the firm where it is.

**Vanessa:** And the third one?

**Founder:** Atlas Office. Four ninety-nine a month. Up to five active travelers under one membership. Includes an EA Companion Seat — meaning Marguerite gets her own login, her own standing instructions on file, she can open the desk on behalf of any of the five. All design included. First Trip Guarantee on every named traveler's first trip.

**Vanessa:** Pause. Four ninety-nine for five travelers is — that's a hundred a head if I fill it. That's less per traveler than the Basic tier.

**Founder:** Correct. It's structured that way on purpose. The Office tier is where we make money on density and standing instructions. Once Marguerite has all five profiles loaded — your aisle preference, Brian's kosher meal, the COO's Hyatt status — the marginal cost to us of the sixth booking in a month is much lower than the first. So we share that.

**Vanessa:** Okay. So if I'm doing the math out loud — Office at four ninety-nine, that's six grand a year. My T&E is one fifty. That's four percent of the line. The juniors alone probably burn three hours of Marguerite's time per trip in rebooks and confirmations. She's at — call it ninety an hour fully loaded. Twenty trips between them, that's fifty-four hundred a year just in her time on the juniors. The math isn't the problem.

**Founder:** What is the problem?

**Vanessa:** The problem is I don't know if I'm Plus or Office. If I'm honest, my first instinct is Plus for me personally, leave everyone else where they are, see how it goes for ninety days. But you put the EA Companion Seat in Office, and Marguerite is the actual leverage point. If I go Plus, she's still doing the work the old way.

**Founder:** That's the cleanest articulation of the choice anyone's given me on this call format. Let me push back on the Plus-for-ninety-days idea, gently. Plus for you alone solves the ten-p.m.-Aspen problem. It does not solve the Marguerite-out-for-the-wedding problem. Because Plus is one traveler, named — it's you. When she's out, the seniors are still self-booking, the juniors are still self-booking, and you're still the redundancy.

**Vanessa:** That's fair. So the actual question is whether I trust you enough to put Marguerite on the platform on day one.

**Founder:** Yes. And I want to address that head-on, because it's the right question to ask and I'd be suspicious of anyone who didn't ask it. Can I tell you what we do if we fail your first trip?

**Vanessa:** Please.

**Founder:** First Trip Guarantee. One trip, named on the onboarding call — so you and I literally pick which trip is the test. If that trip fails on our end — we miss a confirmation, a desk error costs you a segment, anything where the failure traces to us and not to weather or the airline — we refund the month's membership, and we credit one thousand dollars of desk labor toward fixing or rebuilding the trip. Not a future trip. That one. We eat the rework.

**Vanessa:** A thousand dollars of labor toward the rework. So you're not just giving me my money back and walking away.

**Founder:** Right. That's the part I want you to feel. A month refund on its own is a hundred-dollar gesture on a four-ninety-nine product. It says sorry. The thousand in labor says we're going to fix what we broke before you have to call us a second time. That's the offer.

**Vanessa:** I like that better than what I was expecting. The honest answer is I was expecting "money back, no questions asked," which always reads to me like the company hasn't thought about what failure actually costs the client. Money back doesn't get me to Aspen.

**Founder:** That's the distinction we're trying to draw.

**Vanessa:** Okay. Question. The named trip — am I picking the easiest trip or the hardest one?

**Founder:** Pick the one you'd be most upset to have go wrong. The guarantee is structured so we want you to point us at the trip that matters. If we can hold that one, the rest is process.

**Vanessa:** Schwab IMPACT in November. Vegas, three nights, two dinners I'm hosting, one breakfast meeting offsite. If you can run that and Marguerite doesn't have to think about it, you have me on Office for two years.

**Founder:** That's the named trip. Written down. November IMPACT. Now — back to the tier question. I'm not going to push you to Office if Plus is the right starting point. **Vanessa:** Yeah. When you put it like that, Plus is the wrong answer. The whole point of bringing in an outside desk is to fix the system, not just my personal pain.

**Founder:** I want to be clear I'm not closing you right now. I want you to sit with it overnight. But if Office is the direction, here's what I'd want to do. **Vanessa:** Send me the agreement and a one-pager I can show my COO. He'll want to see the math on the Office tier versus what we spend on Marguerite's overtime now. And I want the FTG language in writing, not just on the call.

## SECTION 2: SALES COACH EVALUATION (~700 words)

**Outcome:** Verbal commitment to Atlas Office at $499/mo, contingent on COO review of the math and written FTG language. November IMPACT named as the FTG trip on the call. Onboarding scheduled (conditional) with EA Marguerite required in the room. This is a 90%+ close — the remaining risk is COO sticker-shock at $6K annual, which the rep has already pre-empted by framing it as 4% of T&E against Marguerite's overtime line.

**What worked, specifically:**

The opening move — *"walk me through how travel actually moves through your office right now? Not the policy version — the real version"* — is the single most important line in the transcript. It gave Vanessa permission to be granular and it gave the rep the three-pattern diagnosis (Vanessa / seniors / juniors) that every later move depended on. Without that diagnosis, the three-tier ladder becomes a menu. With it, the ladder becomes a fitting exercise.

The reflection at minute six — *"You have three different travel patterns in one firm... I don't want to sell you one product when you have three problems"* — is the move that earned the right to walk the full ladder. Vanessa is a fiduciary; she responds to people who understand the structure of a problem before pricing it. The rep treated the ladder as diagnostic, not as a price-anchoring trick. That's the right register for this buyer.

The pushback on Plus-for-90-days was clean and necessary. Vanessa self-selected Plus initially — the lower-risk, lower-commitment option — and the rep had to make her see the *systems* cost of a phased rollout without bullying her into Office. The line *"There's a cost to a phased rollout that's not on the price sheet"* is the inflection. It reframes the decision from a personal-comfort one (Plus for me) to a firm-leverage one (Office for the system). Vanessa flipped herself; the rep didn't flip her.

FTG1 delivery was the strongest moment of the call. The rep didn't lead with the refund — they led with the labor credit. *"A month refund on its own is a hundred-dollar gesture on a four-ninety-nine product... The thousand in labor says we're going to fix what we broke before you have to call us a second time."* For a deliberate, fiduciary-minded buyer, the specificity of the labor credit reads as operationally honest. Vanessa explicitly contrasted FTG1 favorably against the no-questions FTG3 mental model: *"Money back doesn't get me to Aspen."* That single sentence is the validation that FTG1 was the right mechanic for this persona.

The naming of the FTG trip in-session — November IMPACT, Vegas — converted an abstract guarantee into a contractual artifact. Once she named the trip, walking away got psychologically harder.

**What broke:**

The rep did not surface the EA Companion Seat early enough. It came in as a Tier-3 feature description rather than as a centerpiece. For a buyer whose entire constraint is "Marguerite is the leverage point," the EA seat should have been the headline of the Office tier, not a sub-bullet. Lucky save — Vanessa connected it herself — but in a less-fluent buyer, the rep would have left the value on the table.

Pricing math was done by the buyer, not the rep. Vanessa ran the four-percent-of-T&E calc herself. That worked here because she's a finance person, but in another call the rep would have needed to do that math out loud. Build the unit-economics talk-track.

The rep never named the competitor (Concur). Concur came up unprompted from Vanessa and was never re-engaged. Missed opportunity to contrast the desk model against the OBT model directly.

**Three improvements:**

1. **Lead Office tier with the EA seat, not the price.** Open Tier 3 with *"this is the tier where your EA gets her own login,"* then price.
2. **Build a 60-second unit-economics talk-track** the rep can deliver: T&E percent, EA overtime line, error cost per trip. Don't outsource the math to the buyer.
3. **Always ask about personal travel before the buyer does.** Vanessa surfaced the husband at the very end. That should have come up at minute 20.

**Most important behavioral change for this rep:** Stop being the menu. Be the fitter. The ladder is a diagnostic instrument; don't let the buyer experience it as three prices in a row.

## SECTION 3: BUSINESS EVALUATOR (~700 words)

**Unit economics at this conversion:**

ARR landed: $5,988 ($499 × 12). At Vanessa's projected utilization — 5 seats, ~40-50 trips/year across the firm, design work on most principal trips — Latitude's cost-to-serve is concentrated in the first 60 days (profile loading, standing-instruction buildup, Marguerite training) and the named-FTG trip in November. After IMPACT clears successfully, marginal cost per trip drops materially because Marguerite is now a co-operator on the platform, not a friction point.

Gross margin implication: If IMPACT fails and FTG1 triggers, the cost to Latitude is the month refund ($499) plus the $1,000 labor credit plus the actual labor to rework the trip — call it $1,500-$2,000 of desk time. Total downside on a failed first trip: ~$3,500. Against $6K ARR, that's a 58% margin hit in year one — but only if the trip fails. Probability-weighted, if Latitude's actual first-trip failure rate is at or below 5%, FTG1's expected cost is ~$175/year — under 3% of ARR. That's a cheap risk-reversal instrument for the trust it buys.

Critically, FTG1 is *structurally cheaper* than FTG2 (refund + free month + $500 service credit) at the same trust signal, because the labor credit is paid in labor — partially fixed cost — not in cash. FTG2 would have cost ~$1,100 on a failed trip in pure cash. FTG1 trades cash for labor that's already on the org chart. For an SMB-scale desk, that's the right financial structure.

**Strategic positioning learnings:**

Model C — the three-tier ladder — produced the right outcome here, but for a specific reason: the deep-discovery format gave the rep time to *make the ladder diagnostic*. In a cold inbound or a price-objection sim, three tiers is too many — the buyer anchors to the cheapest and the rep has to work back up. In a 45-minute discovery, three tiers is exactly the right number because it maps onto the buyer's actual organizational structure (principal / seniors / juniors).

The Plus tier ($249) is the most strategically dangerous tier in Model C. It is the "safe middle" that a deliberate buyer like Vanessa will self-select if the rep doesn't actively reframe. Plus is a worse deal for Latitude than either Basic or Office: it commits desk capacity to one named traveler with all design included, at a price point that doesn't reflect the cost of that inclusion. Plus exists primarily as a *psychological floor* for Office — it makes $499 feel like a reasonable jump, not a leap from $99. The rep's job is to use Plus as a reference point and close on Office.

The $499 Office tier is where the model works financially. At full utilization (5 seats), it's effectively $99/seat — below the Basic price point — which is the right density signal. At low utilization (2-3 seats), Latitude is overpaid relative to design-hours delivered. Vanessa is the ideal Office customer because her seat utilization will be 5/5 within 60 days.

**Retention probability:**

High — 80%+ at 12 months, conditional on IMPACT going well. Vanessa's switching cost compounds: every standing instruction loaded into Marguerite's flow is a tendril that makes leaving more expensive. The named FTG trip is a retention instrument disguised as a risk-reversal instrument — once Latitude holds IMPACT, Vanessa's mental model shifts from *"trying this out"* to *"we use Latitude."* Language change is the retention signal.

**Referral value:**

Very high. Vanessa is a Charleston RIA principal — she's two degrees of separation from 40-60 similar firms via FPA, Schwab IMPACT, and the regional RIA network. The COO sign-off requirement is actually a feature: it pre-builds an internal advocate. If IMPACT clears, expect 2-3 warm intros within six months of the renewal date.

**The hidden risk:**

Marguerite. She is the actual user, not Vanessa. The rep handled this by requiring her in the onboarding room, but the relationship risk is real: if Marguerite resents the platform, defaults back to Concur, or feels her job is being commoditized, Vanessa's confidence collapses. The FTG covers operational failure. It does not cover *EA passive resistance*, which is the more probable failure mode for this account. Mitigation: the rep must run the onboarding call as if Marguerite is the buyer, not Vanessa.
