## SECTION 1 — TRANSCRIPT (~1,500 words)

**Context:** Second call. First call was a 22-minute intro made warm by Howard Lessing — the founder's old boss at the brokerage, now retired in Litchfield, who sits on a small advisory board with Eliana's husband. Howard's email said: *"Eli runs the family office. She doesn't waste time. If you can save her EA forty hours a quarter she'll pay you whatever's fair. Don't oversell her — she'll smell it."* This is the close call. Eliana said she'd give it twenty minutes. It's 8:42am Tuesday. She is in her Greenwich office.

**Founder:** Eliana, thanks for the second twenty. Where do you want to start — pricing, or the operating mechanic?

**Eliana:** Pricing. Howard told me you'd undersell yourself. Let's get it out of the way.

**Founder:** Fair. The right product for you is Atlas Office Unlimited. $349 a month. Flat. Covers up to five active travelers — so you, your husband if he uses it, the two principals you mentioned, and the EA seat for Margot is included, no extra line item. All trip design is in the membership. No per-trip design fee, no escalator on complex itineraries, no surprise invoice if a Tokyo trip turns into a Tokyo-Kyoto-Singapore trip three days before wheels-up.

**Eliana:** And the cheaper one Howard mentioned?

**Founder:** $189. One traveler. I'm not going to pitch you that. You have a household and a small staff that all travel on the office's dime — putting you on the single-traveler tier and then bolting people on as exceptions is the wrong shape for a family office. You'd spend the savings in friction within ninety days.

**Eliana:** *(slight pause)* Okay. Keep going.

**Founder:** $349 a month, billed monthly, no annual commit. Margot keeps her Concur for the corporate side if you want — we don't replace her, we sit next to her. The trip count we'd expect from what you described last week — call it eighteen to twenty-four trips a year across the principals plus the household — falls comfortably inside the Unlimited envelope. There is no version of your year where we send you a design fee.

**Eliana:** What's the catch on Unlimited. Every flat-rate product has a catch.

**Founder:** Fair question. Two real ones. First — we cap at five *active* travelers. If your nephew joins the office and starts traveling, that's a conversation, not a free addition. Second — Unlimited means unlimited design, not unlimited *us*. If you tried to run thirty trips a quarter through us we'd tell you, and we'd either right-size or part ways. We've never had to. But that's the honest answer.

**Eliana:** Margot does most of the booking now. What does she actually hand off?

**Founder:** The work she shouldn't be doing. Building options for a Lisbon trip when you've never been. Reworking a flight at 11pm because Delta moved a connection. Calling a hotel in Mykonos to confirm a villa has working A/C in August because the listing photo was from 2019. Margot keeps the calendar, the preferences, the standing instructions. She stays in the loop on every trip. We send her the receipts. She does not become our assistant. She uses us as hers.

**Eliana:** *(writes something down)* And the overnight piece. Howard said you cover overnights personally.

**Founder:** Until 7am Eastern, yes. My VA team is Manila-based, covers US daytime to about 10pm Eastern. From 10pm to 7am it's me on my phone. Weekends too. Sixty-minute SLA on anything moving — cancellations, reroutes, hotel walks. If your husband is in Zurich and his Tuesday morning meeting moves to Monday afternoon, you don't wait for New York to wake up. That's the whole reason this exists.

**Eliana:** *(beat)* $349 a month is $4,188 a year. That's not the number that's going to make me say no. The number that makes me say no is whether I trust the operating mechanic. Tell me about the guarantee Howard mentioned.

**Founder:** The First Trip Guarantee. The version we're running right now is the cleanest one. Thirty days, money back on the membership. No conditions. You don't have to prove anything failed. You decide if the month was worth $349. If you decide it wasn't, you email Margot, Margot emails us, we refund the month. No exit interview, no save-attempt, no "let me get my manager."

**Eliana:** *(pause)* Why would you offer that to me. Howard sent me. I'm not a flight risk.

**Founder:** *(slight laugh)* Honestly? Two reasons. One — I offer it to everyone. It's not warm-referral specific. The product either earns the $349 in the first thirty days or it doesn't, and I'd rather find out fast on both sides. Two — and this one matters more for you specifically — Howard sent you, which means if this goes badly, it goes badly through Howard. The thirty-day window is as much my protection as yours. If we're not the right shape for a family office, I want you to find out in thirty days and walk, not in six months and tell Howard.

**Eliana:** *(longer pause)* That's a more honest answer than I expected.

**Founder:** It's the only one that survives Howard.

**Eliana:** *(half-smile, audible)* Fine. Walk me through what happens in the first week.

**Founder:** Margot and you get a forty-minute onboarding. We build standing instructions — airlines, seats, hotels you'll go back to, hotels you won't, dietary stuff, the husband's knee on long-hauls, the dog-sitter pattern, whatever you tell us. **Eliana:** What does Margot do on day one.

**Founder:** Forty minutes with us. Then her week looks exactly like last week, minus the parts she's been complaining about. We don't make her learn a portal. We don't make her log into anything. She emails the desk. The desk replies. That's the interface.

**Eliana:** *(beat)* The thirty days. Does the clock start at signing or at first trip?

**Founder:** Signing. I won't game it for you. If your first trip is on day twenty-eight and it goes sideways, you have two days to decide and that's tight. **Founder:** I'll tell him you said the price didn't scare you. He'll enjoy that more.

## SECTION 2 — SALES COACH EVALUATION (~700 words)

**Outcome:** Closed. Agreement to be sent same day, EA onboarding Thursday, first trip Lisbon in three weeks. No discount given. No annual commit asked for. Clean monthly start at $349.

**What worked — cite lines:**

1. *"$189. One traveler. I'm not going to pitch you that."* — The founder pre-empted the cheaper tier and disqualified it on **shape**, not price. For a family office principal, being offered the lower SKU would have read as a category error. Naming the wrong tier and rejecting it on her behalf signaled the founder understood her org, not just her budget. This is the single highest-leverage line in the call.

2. *"There is no version of your year where we send you a design fee."* — Concrete, future-tense, surgical. Eliana is a buyer who lives in invoice surprises (family offices are death by line item). This sentence neutralizes the entire category of objection in eleven words. Unlimited as a structural promise, not a marketing claim.

3. *"Honestly? Two reasons... if this goes badly, it goes badly through Howard."* — When asked why offer FTG3 to a warm referral, the founder named the referral risk explicitly. This is the call's emotional peak. A weaker rep would have said "because we believe in the product." Naming Howard converted FTG3 from a soft retail signal into an operator-grade trust mechanism. It also flattered Eliana's intelligence — she already knew it; the founder just said the quiet part.

4. *"Signing... If your first trip lands past day twenty, I'll extend the window."* — Volunteered fairness. She asked one operational question and the founder gave back more than was required. This is the move that earned *"Put that in the email."* — the only directive command Eliana issued before closing.

**What broke:** Almost nothing, but two small things.

- The *"I offer it to everyone"* framing of FTG3 slightly undersold the mechanic for *this* buyer. For a family office principal, "everyone gets this" can read as commodity. The Howard reframe rescued it within the same breath, but the rescue was needed.
- The founder did not ask for an introduction to the husband or the second principal. Eliana mentioned both. A higher-skill close would have planted *"would it be useful if I ran the same 20 minutes with David"* before hanging up. The expansion seat was on the table and the founder left it.

**Three improvements:**

1. **Pre-empt the cheaper tier earlier and harder in the family-office context.** The founder did this well here but reactively. On the next family-office call, lead with *"there are two tiers, only one of them is right for you, here's why."* Compress the disqualification into the opening 90 seconds.

2. **Sell FTG3 as referral insurance from the first sentence, not the rescue sentence.** *"Thirty-day money-back, and the reason I lead with it on a Howard intro specifically is — "* Inverts the framing. Buyer hears the warm-referral logic before the commodity logic.

3. **Always plant the expansion seat before closing the primary.** *"Five-traveler cap, you'll use three to start, I'd rather you grow into it than around it."* One sentence. Buys quarterly expansion conversations for free.

**The most important behavioral change for this rep:** Stop earning the close and then stopping. The founder closed at *"Send the agreement."* The call should have continued for ninety more seconds. Family office principals don't churn — they expand or refer. The Lisbon trip is the membership; the husband, the second principal, the household staff travel, and the next two Howard-equivalents in Eliana's network are the actual deal. The founder treated this call like a $349/mo sale. It's a $4,188 ARR sale that should compound to $15-20K ARR plus 2-3 warm referrals within twelve months if worked correctly. **Close, then keep talking.**

## SECTION 3 — BUSINESS EVALUATOR (~700 words)

**Unit economics at this conversion.** $349/mo × 12 = $4,188 ARR. Atlas Office Unlimited assumes ~18-24 trips/year against this account. At an internal cost-to-serve of roughly $85-110 per fully-designed trip (PHT VA labor + founder overnight time amortized + tooling), gross trip cost lands at $1,530-$2,640/yr. Gross margin band: **37%-63%**, midpoint ~50%. That is materially worse than Model A's $249 with design escalator on the same usage pattern (where high-design trips would have billed an additional $1-3K), but materially better in **predictability**, which for an early-stage hosted office is the more valuable variable. Predictability lets the founder hire against forecast instead of against variance. The $100/mo delta between Model A and Model B effectively buys the founder a stable revenue line in exchange for absorbing design variance. For Eliana specifically, this trade is correct: her usage will be **lumpy** (Lisbon is a high-design trip; Margot's last three were probably routine), and Model A would have surfaced 2-3 awkward escalator conversations in year one, each one a small trust withdrawal. Model B converts those withdrawals into a single up-front price acceptance.

**Strategic positioning learning.** The $349 number did not friction. Eliana's exact line — *"$4,188 a year. That's not the number that's going to make me say no"* — is the most important data point from this sim. Family-office buyers operate on annualized framing by default; the founder's monthly framing was politely converted to annual by the buyer in real time, and the annual number was a non-event. **This means Model B is materially under-priced for the family-office segment.** $349 lands as "fair," not as "premium." A $449 or $499 family-office variant of Office Unlimited with a defined household-travel inclusion would likely close at the same rate against this persona, with no FTG renegotiation. **Recommend testing a $449 "Office Unlimited — Household" SKU against the next three warm-referral family-office intros.**

**Retention probability.** High. ~85-90% 12-month retention forecast. Three reasons: (1) Margot likes the operating model — when the EA is the daily user and the EA is happy, principal-level churn requires active effort; (2) the household-travel inclusion creates a switching cost no competitor cleanly replicates (Margot would have to re-train a replacement on the husband, the dog, the Alentejo wine estate, the knee on long-hauls — that institutional knowledge compounds inside Latitude 43 and exits with churn); (3) the founder's overnight coverage will be tested within ninety days on a Lisbon-or-equivalent trip and, if it holds, becomes the retention anchor.

**Referral value.** This is the dominant economic variable in this account. Eliana sits adjacent to Howard's network — small-to-mid family offices, RIA principals, brokerage retirees, Greenwich/Litchfield/Bedford density. A satisfied Eliana is conservatively worth **2-3 warm intros over 18 months**, each with a higher close probability than this one (Eliana is now the social proof, not Howard). Expected referral ARR contribution: $8-15K incremental within 18 months from this single account. **The $349/mo sale is the loss leader; the network is the product.**

**The hidden risk.** FTG3 on a family-office principal in a warm-referral context is structurally asymmetric in a way the founder may not have priced. If Eliana exercises the 30-day refund — even fairly, even because Latitude 43 genuinely wasn't a fit — the refund event travels back to Howard within a week. Howard then carries a *"didn't work out for Eli"* data point into every future referral conversation, and Howard's referral pipeline (which has produced this deal and will produce others) gets discounted by his own caution. **The 30-day money-back is not a $349 risk on this account. It is a $40-60K lifetime-referral-pipeline risk.** The founder accepted that risk implicitly by offering FTG3 without segmenting it. The mitigation is not to remove FTG3 — that would damage the operator-correct posture that closed this deal — but to **execute the first thirty days with disproportionate intensity** on this account specifically. Lisbon must not be a normal trip. It must be the trip that becomes the Howard story.

## SECTION 4 — MODEL + FTG SCORECARD (~300 words)

**Did the price land or stick?** Landed cleanly. **No friction.** Eliana's tell — *"$4,188 a year. That's not the number that's going to make me say no"* — is the cleanest possible price acceptance in the sim set. $349/mo is, if anything, *under-positioned* for this persona. The right-sizing thesis is confirmed: $249 would have read as cheap for a family office (category error), and $349 reads as fair. **Recommend a $449 household-inclusive SKU test.**

**Did the FTG mechanic move the buyer?** Helpful, not decisive. FTG3 served two functions: (1) it neutralized the *"every flat-rate has a catch"* concern by adding an exit, and (2) it gave the founder a vehicle for the Howard-risk reframe, which was the call's trust peak. But Eliana would have closed without it. She bought the operating model and Howard's word. FTG3 was confirmatory, not causal. **The Howard-risk framing is the reusable asset, not the 30-day mechanic itself.**

**Would the buyer have bought under a different model?** Model A ($249 + escalator): would have closed but with weaker retention — escalator surprises would have eroded trust by month 4-6. Model C ($499 three-tier): would have closed at $499 with no friction, and would have produced better unit economics, but introduces tier-shopping cognitive load that this buyer did not want. **Model B at $349 is the right product; Model C at $499 may be the right *price*.**

**Net read.** Best fit: **Model B, structurally**. Best revenue capture: **Model C-equivalent pricing inside Model B's simplicity** — i.e., a $449 family-office variant. FTG3 was correct for the warm-referral context but is a hidden referral-pipeline liability if exercised. **Execute Lisbon disproportionately well.**
