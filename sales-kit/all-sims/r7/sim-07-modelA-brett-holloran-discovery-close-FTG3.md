## SECTION 2 — SALES COACH EVALUATION (~700 words)

**Outcome:** Closed. Atlas Office at $249/mo, FTG3 in writing, intake scheduled, first trip already named (Dallas/Houston June 9). Time-to-close from second call open: 27 minutes. Signed-back same day at 4:11pm.

**What worked, specifically.**

The opening move was the highest-leverage thing the rep did all call. *"Are we here to figure out if this works, or are we here to figure out how it starts?"* That line forced Brett to declare his temperature out loud, and he did — "70/30 toward yes." Once a buyer says a number out loud, the rep's job changes from selling to clearing obstacles. The whole rest of the call was operating on Brett's stated 30%, not the rep's imagined 50%.

Second, the rep let Brett run *his* agenda first. "Run yours. Faster." That's a confidence move that pays twice — Brett feels respected, and the rep now knows the exact two objections instead of guessing. M&A founders are not patient with sequenced discovery on a second call. They have a list.

Third, the answer to the design-fee objection was the best work in the transcript. The rep did three things in 90 seconds: (1) collapsed the abstract escalator into concrete dollar amounts on Brett's actual trip types — $9K → $225, $15K → $375, $18K → $600. (2) Reframed the fee as governance, not extraction — *"it tells your EA that asking us to plan a four-city sprint is a different ask than booking a Delta one-way."* That reframe is what turned the variable from a pricing problem into a discipline feature. (3) Pre-empted the upsell by *naming the $349 Unlimited plan and explaining why he wasn't selling it.* That move — *"the other version is to flat-rate it at $349, here's why I didn't put you there"* — is rare and extremely high-trust. Brett called it out explicitly at the end of the call.

Fourth, FTG3 was delivered with discipline. One sentence: *"Thirty-day money-back on the membership. No conditions."* The rep did not over-decorate. When Brett tested the boundary on design fees, the rep held the line cleanly without flinching — *"the design fee is for work we already did."* That refusal to over-promise made the guarantee more credible than a bigger guarantee would have.

**What broke.**

Not much, but the rep let one thing dangle. Brett mentioned the EA-service burn from two years ago — six-month minimum, couldn't get out. The rep responded with FTG3, which addresses *exit*, but didn't address *the trauma* — didn't say "that sucks, that's why we did month-to-month." A 10-second acknowledgment of the prior bad experience would've deepened the bond and accelerated the close by two minutes. Brett would've signed faster if the rep had named it.

Also: the EA Companion Seat distinction got handled correctly but late. Brett raised it at minute 22. It should've been surfaced earlier as a Light-vs-Office contrast because it's the cleanest justification for the $150/mo step-up. The rep got lucky that Brett pre-decided.

**Three improvements.**

1. **Acknowledge prior bad experiences explicitly before pivoting to the guarantee.** "That's exactly the pattern we built against — here's how we're different structurally." Five seconds. Big deposit.
2. **Quantify the EA seat earlier in the design-fee conversation.** The escalator concern is partly an "is this worth it" concern. Reminding Brett that Megan gets a named seat on the desk reframes the $249 as a *team* line item, not a *Brett* line item.
3. **Ask for the trip name *before* asking for the signature.** Naming Dallas/Houston June 9 as "the first trip" *before* sending the agreement creates a forward commitment that makes the agreement a formality. The rep did this in the right order but barely — could be tighter.

**The single most important behavioral change for this rep.**

**Lead with the upsell-refusal earlier.** The "I'm not selling you the $349 because you'd be subsidizing solo travelers" was the moment Brett's posture changed. Bring that move forward by ten minutes. On future calls, pre-empt the Unlimited question in the first five minutes of the close call — it's the cheapest, most powerful trust deposit available. Right now the rep treats it as a closer. It should be an opener.

## SECTION 3 — BUSINESS EVALUATOR (~700 words)

**Unit economics at this conversion.**

Brett signs Atlas Office at $249/mo = $2,988 ARR on membership. Eight sprint-trip design fees at average $375 to member (50% off $750 list) = $3,000 in design-fee revenue annually. Total Brett-level revenue: ~$5,988/yr. Gross of cost-to-serve (PHT VA hours + founder evenings + tooling allocation), Latitude 43's contribution margin on an Atlas Office member at Brett's usage profile lands around 55-62% — call it ~$3,500 contribution per year if he stays a full year.

Critical observation: the design-fee revenue is *almost equal to* the membership revenue at Brett's usage. That makes the escalator a *load-bearing* part of the model, not a footnote. Strip the escalator out and revert to flat $349 Unlimited (Model B), and Brett's ARR is $4,188 — a real $1,800 worse for L43 on this specific buyer, before factoring that Unlimited would compress design-team utilization without commensurate margin. The rep's instinct to keep Brett on Model A was correct unit-economically *and* tonally.

**Strategic positioning learnings.**

Three.

First: **the design-fee escalator works as a self-selection mechanism for high-design-load buyers.** Brett is exactly the customer the escalator is built for — multi-city, multi-person, high-complexity. He grumbled, but he accepted. A buyer who *can't* accept the escalator is a buyer who would have churned at month four under Unlimited because they'd discover the trips weren't complex enough to justify $349. The escalator does pricing discovery for you.

Second: **FTG3 (30-day money-back) is the only guarantee that survives the "I've been burned" objection without inviting more objections.** FTG1 (month + $1K labor credit) would have triggered Brett to ask what "labor credit" means and how it's calculated. FTG2 (refund + free month + service credit) would have triggered exactly the "are you guys desperate?" reaction the rep referenced. FTG3 is *boring enough to be believed.* For a 41-year-old M&A founder who reads contracts for a living, boring is a feature.

Third: **the refused upsell is now Latitude 43's most replicable closing move.** It's reproducible across every founder-stage buyer, costs nothing, and converts at a measurably higher rate than the standard upsell-attempt-and-fold pattern. This should be documented as the L43 "ladder-down close."

**Retention probability.**

High. Estimated 18-month retention probability for Brett: 72-78%. Reasons: he named the first trip on the call (commitment device); his ARR is dominated by the *design fees on the trips he most needs help with*, meaning the value moments are concentrated in the highest-cognition trips; Megan adopting the workflow is the variable, not Brett's enthusiasm. Hidden lever: if Megan defaults back to Concur for the simple Delta-one-way trips and only uses L43 for sprints, retention drops to 55% by month 9 because Brett re-evaluates the membership against design-only consumption (which feels like an a-la-carte service, not a membership).

**Referral value.**

Very high. Brett is a Charlotte M&A founder. His network is 20-50 other founder-operators in the $5-50M EV M&A boutique segment, plus PE associates and family-office principals he sources deals from. One Brett referral at this profile converts at ~40% because of source credibility. If retention holds 12 months, expected referrals: 1.5-2.5. Each at ~$6K ARR. Brett's LTV-with-referral is likely $25-40K, not $6K.

**The hidden risk.**

**Design-fee surprise on trip three or four.** Brett accepted the escalator logic *abstractly* on the close call. The risk is the first $600 design fee invoice that lands on his desk in month two when he hasn't fully internalized that he agreed to it. He will remember "the $249" much more vividly than "the variable." Mitigation: the intake call next week should walk through the escalator in *exact dollars* on his *next three actual trips* — not as a sales move, as an operational expectation-setting move. If the first design-fee invoice doesn't surprise him, he stays 18 months. If it does surprise him, he calls within 60 days and tests the guarantee. Founder needs to brief Megan on this directly during intake.

**One sentence:** This is a textbook-fit buyer for Model A + FTG3, closed correctly, with one operational landmine on month-two billing the team must defuse during intake.
