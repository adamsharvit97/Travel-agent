**Stage:** Cold first contact / quick call. 10-min hard stop.

## SECTION 1 — TRANSCRIPT (~1,500 words)

*[Tuesday 11:02 AM PT. Hugo dialed in from his car at Sand Hill. Stephen is in Toronto, second coffee, headset on. Hugo opens.]*

**Hugo:** Stephen. Hugo Beauchamp. I've got till 11:12. Hard out — I've got an LP at 11:15. What is this?

**Stephen:** Appreciate the window, Hugo. Latitude 43 is a travel office for founders and VC partners who do their own booking at midnight and shouldn't be. Toronto-based, hosted under Fora. We run a desk that covers US daytime with a Philippines VA, and I cover evenings, weekends, overnights — including 2am IROPs. 60-minute SLA. Members pay monthly. Want me to go straight to pricing or do you want a minute on how the desk actually works?

**Hugo:** Pricing first. I've been pitched the why. I haven't been pitched a number that wasn't somebody's mood.

**Stephen:** Fair. We have three tiers. I'll be quick. Basic is $99 a month — that's booking access to the desk, you still pay design fees on complex trips. Plus is $249 — booking plus all the trip design folded in on your member trips, no fee escalator. Office is $499 — up to five active travelers, EA companion seat, full inclusive.

*[Three-second pause. Hugo is doing math out loud, half to himself.]*

**Hugo:** Three tiers in a ten-minute call. Okay. Walk me past Basic — I'm not Basic. What's the actual difference between $249 and $499 for someone who travels alone?

**Stephen:** Honest answer — if it's just you, you don't need Office. Office is for when you've got an EA booking on your behalf or a partner who travels with you on the same account. For you, solo, $300K T&E, the right tier is Plus. $249. That's the one.

**Hugo:** So why did you just spend ninety seconds on Basic and Office?

*[Small moment. Stephen knows he just burned time.]*

**Stephen:** Because I haven't earned the right to pick your tier for you yet. But you're right — I should've led with Plus and let you ask about the others. Plus is the answer.

**Hugo:** Okay. That was the right correction. Keep going. $249 a month — what do I actually get that I don't get from my Amex Plat travel desk, which I already pay $695 a year for and hate?

**Stephen:** Three things, concretely. One — a named human at the desk who knows your standing instructions. Aisle, no red-eyes east, Hyatt over Marriott, whatever it is. We write it down once. Two — sub-60-minute response in business hours, and I personally pick up the overnight pager. Amex routes you to whichever agent is awake in Manila. Three — when a trip breaks at 2am, I'm rebooking you before you've finished the coffee. Amex puts you on hold for forty minutes and then quotes you the published fare.

**Hugo:** That last one is the only one I care about. The first two are table stakes for anyone charging $249.

**Stephen:** Agreed.

**Hugo:** Six minutes left. Sell me the part I'm going to push back on.

**Stephen:** The part you're going to push back on is the monthly fee in a month you didn't travel. $249 in July when you were in Tahoe with your kids and didn't book a flight feels like a gym membership you forgot to cancel. That's the real objection and I'm not going to pretend it isn't.

**Hugo:** Correct. What's your answer.

**Stephen:** Two answers. First — most of our members trip-out 4 to 7 times a quarter, so the dead months are rare and the busy months more than pay for it. Second, and this is the one I'd lean on if I were you — we have a 30-day money-back on the membership. Doesn't matter why. You try us for a month, you decide it wasn't worth it, you tell us, we refund the month. No questions, no exit interview, no "let me put you on with retention." You decide.

*[Pause. Hugo audibly exhales — not quite a laugh.]*

**Hugo:** That's it? That's the guarantee?

**Stephen:** That's it.

**Hugo:** No "first trip has to fail," no "we credit you toward labor," no scoring rubric on whether the trip technically failed on your end versus the airline's end?

**Stephen:** No. We tried writing the clever version. Three pages. I threw it out. The clever version means I'm arguing with you about whether your trip failed. I don't want that conversation. You decide if the month was worth $249. If not, you get it back.

**Hugo:** Okay. That's — that's actually refreshing. I'll be honest, after the three-tier ladder thing my pattern-match was "this guy is going to nickel-and-dime me." The guarantee resets that.

**Stephen:** Noted on the ladder. I should compress it.

**Hugo:** You should. Lead with the tier I'm in. Mention the others exist. Move on. You lost me for about thirty seconds in the middle there and you can't afford that on a ten-minute call.

**Stephen:** Taking the note.

**Hugo:** Four minutes. What's the close?

**Stephen:** The close is — I send you a one-page agreement today, you sign by Friday, we onboard Monday. Onboarding is a 30-minute call where we capture your standing instructions, your loyalty numbers, your card on file, your assistant's contact if she's involved. First trip we run for you is the proof. If the month doesn't feel worth $249, you tell me on day 29 and I refund it.

**Hugo:** Who's "we" on the onboarding? You or the VA?

**Stephen:** Me. I do every onboarding personally for the first ninety days of a member's life. After that you mostly talk to Marisol on the desk, but I'm always one escalation away.

**Hugo:** And if Marisol misses an SLA?

**Stephen:** You email me. I answer within an hour, day or night. That's the deal.

**Hugo:** Okay. Two questions and then I'm out. One — what's the catch on the 30-day money-back. There's always a catch.

**Stephen:** The catch is it's only on the membership fee. If we've already paid out a non-refundable hotel deposit or a paid airfare on your behalf, those are yours — that's airline and hotel money, not ours. The $249 is yours back. The trip costs are trip costs.

**Hugo:** Reasonable. Two — who else like me is on this. Names if you can, category if you can't.

**Stephen:** I can give you three. A GP at a Series A fund in Menlo, $400K T&E, three months in, used us for a Tokyo IROP in March. An RIA managing partner in Boston, $340M AUM, signed in February with her EA on the call. And a Series B founder flying out of SFO who I rebooked at 2:30am out of a Delta cancel three weeks ago. I can intro you to any of them with their permission.

**Hugo:** The Series B founder. That one.

**Stephen:** Done. I'll text him today.

**Hugo:** Okay. Send me the agreement. Plus, $249. I'll sign or I won't sign by Friday. If I sign, we onboard Monday at 8am Pacific.

**Stephen:** Sending in the next hour. Hugo — appreciate the directness.

**Hugo:** You earned the second call. Don't make me regret it.

*[Hugo drops. 10:48 elapsed. Two minutes under.]*

## SECTION 2 — SALES COACH EVALUATION (~700 words)

**Outcome: Soft yes, agreement requested, reference call promised. Conversion probability ~55-65% by Friday. Strong outcome for a 10-min cold first contact with a VC partner.**

**What worked, with receipts:**

The single best moment in the call was Stephen's mid-stream self-correction at minute three. Hugo's line — *"Three tiers in a ten-minute call. Okay. Walk me past Basic — I'm not Basic"* — was a test, not a question. Stephen could've defended the ladder. Instead he said *"if it's just you, you don't need Office… Plus is the answer,"* and then, critically, when Hugo pushed harder with *"So why did you just spend ninety seconds on Basic and Office?"* — Stephen took the hit clean: *"I haven't earned the right to pick your tier for you yet. But you're right — I should've led with Plus."* That's the move. A VC partner who hears a founder absorb a coaching point in real time without flinching reads that as operator-grade. Hugo's *"That was the right correction"* is a tell — he's now coaching Stephen, which means he's leaning in.

Second strong moment: Stephen volunteered the strongest objection before Hugo could raise it. *"$249 in July when you were in Tahoe with your kids and didn't book a flight feels like a gym membership you forgot to cancel."* Confessional register. Hugo's response — *"Correct. What's your answer."* — confirms the move worked. You can't out-cynic a VC; you have to beat them to their own objection.

Third: the FTG3 delivery was clean. No upsell language, no qualifications, no "in the unlikely event." Just *"You decide if the month was worth $249. If not, you get it back."* And then, when Hugo probed for the catch, Stephen had a real answer ready (membership fee only, not trip costs). That distinction is what kept the guarantee from feeling like a marketing line.

**What broke:**

The first ninety seconds. Stephen recited three tiers in order, including Basic, to a man with a $300K T&E who is by no plausible read a Basic buyer. Hugo flagged this twice — *"Three tiers in a ten-minute call"* and *"why did you just spend ninety seconds on Basic and Office?"* — and lost confidence for about thirty seconds. In a 10-minute call, thirty seconds is 5% of the entire window. Stephen recovered, but on a buyer with less patience he doesn't.

Second break: Stephen quoted *"4 to 7 trips a quarter"* as the dead-month answer before pivoting to FTG3. That stat is unverified-sounding in context and Hugo didn't bite on it — he waited for the second answer. The FTG3 was the real answer; the stat was throat-clearing.

**Three improvements:**

1. **Diagnose before quoting.** On a cold 10-min call with a known T&E volume, Stephen should ask one question — *"Solo traveler or do you have an EA who books for you?"* — and then quote one tier. Lead with Plus, mention Office exists for EA-involved buyers, never name Basic unprompted. Compresses 90 seconds to 15.

2. **Drop the trip-frequency stat.** It's the weakest line in the call. The FTG3 carries the dead-month objection by itself. Don't dilute it with a stat the buyer can't verify.

3. **Earn the reference ask earlier.** Hugo asked for a reference at minute eight; Stephen could've offered one at minute six, unprompted, right after FTG3. Offering before being asked is a tonal signal — confidence, not desperation.

**The most important behavioral change for Stephen:**

**Stop quoting the menu. Quote the tier.** Stephen is treating the three-tier ladder as a product he has to explain. It isn't. It's an internal pricing architecture. The buyer needs one number — theirs. Every second spent on tiers the buyer isn't in is a second not spent on the SLA, the 2am pager, or the guarantee. On a 10-min call this isn't a style preference; it's the difference between a soft yes and a "let me think about it."

## SECTION 3 — BUSINESS EVALUATOR (~700 words)

**Unit economics at this conversion:**

Hugo lands at $249/mo Plus = $2,988 ARR. Assume Latitude 43's blended CAC for cold-sourced VC partners is ~$400 (founder time + Fora platform fees + reference call overhead). Gross margin on Plus is structurally ~55-60% after VA labor allocation, design labor on member trips, and Fora's host split. That puts contribution margin at ~$1,650/yr per Hugo-shaped member. Payback ~3 months. Healthy, but only if retention clears 18 months — which on a $249 monthly with a confessional register and a 60-min SLA is plausible but not guaranteed.

The structural problem with Model C exposed in this call: **the $99 Basic tier is a liability in cold conversations with the actual target buyer.** Hugo's pattern-match on hearing "$99" was "nickel-and-dime." Latitude 43's positioning is operator-grade travel office for $300K+ T&E buyers. Basic exists as a price-anchor, but in a 10-min cold call, it costs more in trust than it gains in optionality. The model would convert better in this segment if Basic were unlisted on cold calls and offered only as a downsell on price objection.

**Strategic positioning learnings:**

Model C's three-tier ladder is a B2B SaaS pattern (good/better/best) imported into a service business where the buyer doesn't shop the ladder — they ask the operator which tier they're in. That asymmetry is the lesson. Latitude 43 should think of Model C not as a menu but as a diagnostic: *"Tell me about your travel pattern, I'll tell you the tier."* Stephen's mid-call correction — *"Plus is the answer"* — was the right move and should be the default opening, not a recovery move.

The $499 Office tier is correctly priced for the EA-included buyer but doesn't belong in a solo founder cold call. It introduces sticker shock without context. The right surface area for Office is Stage 2 (onboarding with EA in the room), not Stage 1.

**Retention probability:**

Hugo's retention probability at 12 months is ~70% if first trip lands cleanly, ~25% if first trip has any operator-side friction in the first 60 days. The FTG3 actually *raises* the cost of a bad first month — Hugo will exercise it without remorse if month one is mediocre, and he won't be talked out of it. This is the trade-off of FTG3: it's the highest-trust guarantee on offer, but it transfers all the proof-burden onto operational execution in the first 30 days. The desk has to be flawless or the refund is automatic.

**Referral value:**

Hugo's referral value is unusually high. VC partners at $650M funds sit in 4-6 board seats and have 20-30 high-T&E peers. One Hugo at $2,988 ARR, retained for 24 months, with two warm intros at year-end, is worth ~$12K ARR in expected downstream. The reference-ask at minute eight was correctly timed but undersold — Stephen should be explicit at month three that referrals are how Latitude 43 grows and ask for one named intro per quarter.

**The hidden risk:**

The hidden risk is **founder-bottleneck on FTG3 enforcement.** Stephen committed to "no exit interview, you decide." That's the right answer in the call. It is operationally dangerous at scale. If 8% of new members exercise FTG3 in any given month — entirely plausible at this guarantee strength — Stephen is refunding meaningful revenue without any signal about why. The clever version of the guarantee (which Stephen explicitly threw out) at least generated diagnostic data. FTG3 as written generates no learning. Latitude 43 needs a *post-refund* mechanism — an optional 5-min exit conversation, no obligation, offered after the refund clears — to capture the "why" without violating the guarantee's promise.

Second hidden risk: **Hugo will tell his peers about the guarantee specifically.** That's good for top-of-funnel but skews incoming members toward "let me try this for 30 days" rather than "let me commit." The mix of trial-mentality joiners vs. committed joiners is something to watch by Q3.

## SECTION 4 — MODEL + FTG SCORECARD (~300 words)

**Did the price land or stick?** Mixed. The $249 Plus tier landed clean once Stephen named it as Hugo's tier. The friction was structural — the *act of presenting three tiers* cost ~30 seconds of trust in a 10-min window. Hugo flagged it twice. Price itself was not the friction; the menu was.

Friction moments, specifically:
- Minute 2: *"Three tiers in a ten-minute call. Okay."* — patience tax.
- Minute 3: *"Why did you just spend ninety seconds on Basic and Office?"* — direct callout.
- Minute 3.5: Stephen's recovery landed. Net friction: -0 by minute 4.

**Did the FTG mechanic move the buyer?** Decisive. Hugo's *"That's actually refreshing"* and the audible exhale are the buyer-tells. FTG3's simplicity functioned as a contrast-effect against the three-tier complexity that preceded it — the ladder made the guarantee feel cleaner than it would have in isolation. Paradoxically, the model's weakness amplified the FTG's strength.

**Would the buyer have bought under a different model?**

- **Model A (v2.0, $99/$249):** Likely a faster yes. Two tiers, no Basic-anchor problem, Hugo lands at $249 in 30 seconds instead of 3 minutes. But weaker on margin and weaker on the "design fees included" story.
- **Model B (Unlimited, $189/$349):** Strongest fit for Hugo. One tier ($189 solo), all design included, no escalator conversation, no ladder. Hugo would have signed on the call, not on Friday. Highest conversion probability for this persona/stage.
- **Model C (this sim):** Sold, but at a cost. The ladder is a tax on cold calls.

**Net read:** **Model B is the right model for Hugo at Stage 1. Model C works but only with a founder disciplined enough to skip Basic entirely and quote Plus on first mention.** FTG3 is the right guarantee for any model where the buyer values simplicity over upside — which is every VC partner Latitude 43 will ever talk to.
