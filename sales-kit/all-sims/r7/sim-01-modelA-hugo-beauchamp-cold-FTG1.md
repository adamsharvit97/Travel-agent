## Section 2 — Sales Coach Evaluation (~700 words)

**Outcome:** Soft yes to a second meeting with the gatekeeper (Marisol) in the room. No close, no card. Realistic and correct for a 10-minute cold first contact at this persona's level. The rep correctly did not push for signature.

**What worked, specifically:**

1. *"I'll skip the windup."* — opening line acknowledged Hugo's time without performing deference. This is the right register for a VC partner; obsequiousness would have killed it inside ninety seconds.
2. *"We don't do policy. We do you."* — clean differentiator against FROSCH/Ovation without trashing them. Hugo named three competitors unprompted, which means he's pre-qualified the category. The rep's job was to position, not educate, and he did.
3. *"Marisol doesn't have to brief us each time."* — naming the EA by name on a cold first call, after Hugo said her name once, is the kind of operator move that signals attention. Small but load-bearing.
4. *"It's a small business pretending to be a small business."* — disarmed the "can you afford to care about me" objection by reframing scale as a feature, not a bug. This is the single best line in the transcript.
5. *"The most honest read I've gotten of that mechanic. I'll take it."* — accepting that the FTG dollar amount didn't move Hugo, rather than re-selling it, was the right call. Trying to defend the $1K credit at a $300K T&E level would have eroded credibility.

**What broke:**

1. The $249 reveal landed *too low* — Hugo audibly recalibrated downward ("Squarespace subscription"). The rep recovered, but the price point did real damage to perceived seriousness before the recovery. At this persona level, leading with price was a mistake; the rep should have established the *operating model* (cap, VA load, founder-on-overnights) *before* the number, so the number reads as the punchline rather than the headline.
2. The FTG was introduced reactively at minute 8, not as a planned beat. It worked because Hugo is generous, but the rep didn't frame it — he just dropped it. A better framing would have anchored the FTG to a specific failure mode Hugo already articulated (the 2am Delta cancellation he mentioned implicitly via the podcast reference).
3. The rep never asked Hugo about his actual travel pattern — destinations, cadence, who else travels on his ticket (fund partners? portfolio CEOs he books for?). This is a discovery miss. By minute 10 the rep should have known whether Hugo books for one or for a fund of four partners, because that changes Atlas Office's value calculation enormously.

**Three improvements:**

1. **Re-sequence the price reveal.** Establish the cap, the VA load, the founder-on-overnights coverage *first*. Hold price until Hugo asks twice, or until minute 7. The number is too low to lead with at this persona; it needs scaffolding.
2. **Discover before you pitch the FTG.** One question — "How many of the partners at Meridian travel on the fund's dime?" — reframes Atlas Office from "$249 for me" to "$249 for the partnership, which is $50/seat." That math is the antidote to the under-positioning problem.
3. **Pre-empt the under-positioning.** A line like "The price will sound low. That's deliberate, and I'll explain why" — said *before* the number — converts the friction from a credibility wound into a setup for the explanation. The rep had the explanation; he just deployed it after the wound.

**The most important behavioral change:** This rep is good at the *substance* of the offer and weak at the *sequencing*. He plays his cards in the order he learned them, not in the order the buyer needs to receive them. The single highest-leverage change is to treat the first 10 minutes as a deliberate sequence — discovery → operating model → coverage → price → FTG — and to refuse to skip ahead even when the buyer pulls him forward. Hugo asked "what's it cost" at minute 4. The right answer is "I'll get there in two minutes — first I need to know one thing about how you travel."

## Section 3 — Business Evaluator (~700 words)

**Unit economics at this conversion:** Assume Hugo signs Atlas Office at $249/mo = $2,988 ARR on the membership line. Design fees on a $300K T&E spend at his profile — international, multi-city, often last-minute — probably generate 8–14 escalator events per year. At member 50% rate, assume blended $400/event × 11 events = $4,400. Total direct revenue: ~$7,400/year. Supplier-side Fora commissions on $300K of travel — call it 4–7% blended on the bookable portion (hotels and premium air mostly; coach domestic is thinner) — adds an estimated $8,000–$14,000/year in commission flow, of which the desk sees a meaningful split. **Total economic value of a Hugo: $15K–$22K/year, of which only $3K shows up on the membership line.**

This is the central business insight from the sim: **Model A's $249 sticker badly under-represents the actual economic value of a high-T&E member.** The desk is monetizing the supplier side and pricing the membership as a loss-leader access fee, but the *buyer* doesn't see the supplier-side economics, so the buyer reads $249 as the whole story and pattern-matches to "cheap = unserious." The rep has to do extra work to overcome a self-inflicted positioning wound.

**Strategic positioning learning:** At Hugo's tier ($300K T&E, $650M fund partner), the buyer's reference price is *the family office desk down the hall* — $1,500–$3,000/month. Coming in at $249 against that reference is not a value-positive surprise; it's a credibility-negative one. Model A works for Aaron (the original Stage 1 persona, $70K agency founder) because $249 feels appropriate for his volume. **Against Hugo, Model A is the wrong model.** Model B ($349 Office Unlimited) is closer to defensible. Even Model C's $499 would have read as "okay, you're a real business."

**Retention probability:** If Hugo signs, retention at 12 months is probably 70–80%, but for a non-obvious reason. He won't churn over service quality — the desk is genuinely good and the SLA is tight. He'll churn because *at $249/mo he keeps forgetting the membership exists* and Marisol defaults back to Amex Travel for half the bookings. Low price points produce low salience. Mid-year usage is likely to hit 40–60% of bookings, which is the Sarah-equivalent retention risk in Stage 4 of the brief. **The pricing model is creating its own future retention problem.**

**Referral value:** High, and this is Model A's hidden upside. A $650M-fund VC partner who signs and stays referred laterally into his portfolio company founders is worth 5–15 downstream Aaron-equivalents. The rep didn't surface this in the call (correctly — too early), but it should be a deliberate Month 3 conversation. Hugo's referral value is probably $40K–$120K in downstream ARR over 24 months if even three portfolio founders convert.

**The hidden risk:** Model A + Hugo creates a credibility asymmetry that propagates. If Hugo refers a portfolio CEO at Series B who's used to enterprise SaaS pricing, the $249 sticker will hit *that* founder the same way it hit Hugo — and the founder won't have Hugo's generosity. The downstream conversion rate on referrals from Hugo at Model A pricing is probably 25–40% lower than it would be at Model B's $349 — because the price keeps doing the same credibility damage at each new doorstep, and not every prospect bothers to ask the recovery question Hugo asked ("how many clients does your VA carry?"). **The model's pricing is a negative referral multiplier at this segment.**

**Net business read:** This sim revealed that Model A is segment-correct for SMB founders and segment-wrong for VC/PE partners and RIA principals. The $300K T&E persona is the wrong buyer for $249. The founder should either (a) restrict Model A marketing to sub-$100K T&E personas, (b) introduce a Model A "Partner" tier at $599–$799 for the high-T&E professional segment, or (c) move this segment to Model B exclusively. The current uniform Model A pricing is leaving meaningful ARR and meaningful credibility on the table at the top of the funnel.

## Section 4 — Model + FTG Scorecard (~300 words)

**Did the price land or stick? — STUCK, then partially recovered.**
$99 Light was never mentioned by Hugo — irrelevant to him, but the existence of a $99 tier in the pricing page contaminates the perceived seriousness of the $249 tier by association. $249 Office hit a clear friction wall: *"Squarespace subscription," "I don't know what to do with $249," "can you afford to give a shit about me."* The rep recovered through the VA-load explanation and the "small business pretending to be a small business" line, but the recovery cost ~3 minutes of a 10-minute call. **Score: 4/10 on price landing.** The recovery was skilled; the wound was self-inflicted.

**Did the FTG mechanic move the buyer? — HELPFUL but not decisive.**
Hugo explicitly said the $1K labor credit was *not* the thing that moved him at $300K T&E volume — "the loss isn't $1K in labor, it's the meeting." But he credited the *gesture* of naming a number. FTG1 functioned as a credibility signal, not as a risk-reversal mechanism. At this T&E tier, FTG2 (free month + service credit) would have been similarly inadequate; FTG3 (30-day money-back, no questions) would have read as more confident and therefore more persuasive. **Score: 5/10.** Move-the-needle quotient: low. Signal-of-seriousness quotient: medium-high.

**Would the buyer have bought under a different model? — YES, more likely under Model B.**
Model B's $349 Unlimited removes the under-positioning wound and removes the design-fee escalator (which Hugo would hit 8–14 times/year). Model C's $499 reads even more credibly at his tier but loses the simplicity advantage. **Best fit for Hugo: Model B + FTG3.**

**Net read:** Model A is wrong for this persona. Right offer architecture, wrong price point, FTG mechanic too small to matter at his T&E scale.
