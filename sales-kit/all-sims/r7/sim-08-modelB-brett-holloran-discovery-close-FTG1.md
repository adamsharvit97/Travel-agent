Three principals fly weekly into Northeast and Midwest secondary markets. EA Janelle (4 years) currently runs Concur + a Delta corporate desk that he describes as "fine, not bad, not good." Up to five active travelers — you, your two partners, and you'd have two slots left for the senior associates when they go to a sponsor meeting. **Founder:** That's all in. And the IROPs are in. The 2am rebooks are in. The "I need to be in Chicago at 7am and Delta just cancelled" calls are in.

**Founder:** Most rework eats two to four hours of senior desk time. A bad one eats six. Our internal rate on senior agent hours is roughly one-sixty an hour. ## Section 2: SALES COACH EVALUATION

**Outcome:** Close. Atlas Office Unlimited at $349/mo, FTG1 deployed against a named trip (Cleveland-Pittsburgh-Detroit), Janelle onboarded into the EA seat. Annual contract value $4,188. Time to close from first discovery: 8 days, 2 calls.

**What worked, specifically.** The rep opened by surfacing what Brett said in the prior call — *"I don't want to be auditing trip invoices"* — and used it as the bridge to the model change. This is the single most important move in the call. By the time the rep names the $349 price, Brett has already been told *why* the rep is showing him a different number than last week, and the reason is Brett's own words. That eliminates the "you're upselling me" reading. The line "I had two options... I picked the second" is unusually good — it shows the rep made a judgment call on Brett's behalf, which is exactly the relationship a desk should have with a managing partner.

The "three things aren't included" move is the second pivotal beat. Brett's objection — "I've been burned twice by all-you-can-eat" — is a real, scarred objection, not a negotiating tactic. The rep doesn't reassure. He names three actual exclusions, two of which (events, non-roster travelers) are crisp and one of which (4+ ground segments international) is specific enough to feel real. This is the operator-correct register the brand calls for. A weaker rep would have said "everything's included, you'll love it" and lost him.

FTG1 is delivered well. The rep doesn't lead with the guarantee — he waits until Brett asks for it, which means the guarantee lands when Brett's interest is highest, not when the rep needs a crutch. The "name the next trip that scares you" reframe is the best line in the call. It converts FTG1 from a trust signal into a tactical instrument. Brett laughs, which is the sound of resistance dropping.

The unit economics close ("$4,188 against $120K T&E, 3.5%, benchmark is 4-7%") is clean. Brett is a numbers guy; he was going to want this exact ratio and the rep gave it to him unprompted.

**What broke.** Two things. First, the rep let Brett drive the FTG segment ("now do the guarantee") instead of weaving it into the price defense. That worked here because Brett is sophisticated, but with a less engaged buyer it would have left the guarantee underweight. Second, the rep didn't get explicit on month-to-month versus annual commitment. Brett asked for "the agreement" and the rep said "one page." There's no contract length in the close. That's a loose thread.

**Three improvements.**

1. **Lock the commitment term in the close.** "One page, month-to-month, you can leave with 30 days notice" — or, if it's annual, say so. The ambiguity will surface in the agreement review and create a second negotiation.

2. **Get the second-month checkpoint on the calendar before hanging up.** Brett's a retention risk profile (M&A partners default to Concur under stress). Booking the 30-day review now, on this call, while he's leaning in, is cheap insurance.

3. **Name the Janelle relationship explicitly.** The rep said Janelle gets the seat. He didn't say what the desk's first move with Janelle will be (load standing instructions, audit her last 90 days of bookings, identify three patterns to standardize). That's the work that converts a signed contract into stickiness.

**Most important behavioral change.** Stop waiting for the buyer to ask for the guarantee. In this call it worked because Brett is curious and direct. In four out of five calls the buyer won't surface it and the rep will leave the strongest risk-reversal instrument in the bag. The rule should be: FTG enters the conversation no later than the moment the buyer says "let me think about it" or two minutes after the price is named, whichever comes first.

## Section 3: BUSINESS EVALUATOR

**Unit economics at this conversion.** Atlas Office Unlimited at $349/mo yields $4,188 ARR per member. Against estimated fully-loaded desk cost per active Office member of ~$1,800/year (allocating ~11 hours/month of agent time at a $14 internal rate, plus tooling and EA seat overhead), gross margin on this seat is approximately 57%. The FTG1 exposure is bounded: if the named trip fails, the firm refunds $349 and absorbs up to $1,000 in labor, a maximum hit of $1,349 — equivalent to roughly 3.9 months of revenue. At an observed first-trip failure rate of ~6% across the existing book, the expected FTG1 cost per close is ~$81, which reduces effective Year-1 margin on this member from 57% to 55%. This is materially below the Model A escalator-driven margin (~63% gross) but the conversion lift more than compensates: Model A's variable-fee structure was generating ~14% close-rate friction in the prior cohort, with two-thirds of stalled deals citing the audit-surface issue Brett named explicitly.

**Strategic positioning learnings.** Brett's behavior validates the central Model B thesis: M&A and PE-adjacent buyers will pay a flat-rate premium to eliminate internal procurement friction even when the variable-rate option is mathematically cheaper on expected value. The deciding factor is not the price; it is whether the price is *one line*. This is a finance-buyer insight, not a travel-buyer insight, and it's the right lens for this segment. The implication for positioning: lead with the predictability of the line item, not the inclusion of design fees. Brett didn't care that design fees were included. He cared that there was nothing else on the invoice. The internal pitch document should be rewritten with that hierarchy.

**Retention probability.** Moderate-to-high, with one specific risk. Brett used the phrase "the reason I'm slow is I've been burned twice." He has muscle memory for vendor disappointment. The first 90 days are decisive. If the Cleveland-Pittsburgh-Detroit trip goes clean, his retention probability at 12 months is ~85%. If it goes sideways and FTG1 fires, the brand outcome depends entirely on execution of the rework — a clean recovery actually raises 12-month retention because the guarantee performed under stress, while a botched recovery cuts retention to ~30%. The FTG1 mechanic is therefore higher-variance than FTG3 (the money-back) but higher-ceiling. Brett's profile rewards the FTG1 bet.

**Referral value.** High. M&A partners refer inside two networks: their own partner cohort (other M&A boutiques in the Southeast) and their PE/sponsor clients. Brett mentioned "I told her I'd loop her on the term sheet" — he runs his firm with explicit decision protocols, which is the same operating style as the buyers in his network. A clean first 90 days converts to ~2.3 warm intros at the segment average, weighted higher for managing partners. Lifetime referral value on Brett, assuming standard conversion of warm intros, is approximately $11K in downstream ARR over 24 months.

**Hidden risk.** Janelle. She wasn't on the call. Brett made the decision unilaterally and then said "I told her I'd loop her on the term sheet." This is a classic principal-buyer move and it carries a known failure mode: the EA, who was not consulted, develops resistance to the new tool because the workflow change wasn't co-designed with her. Janelle has four years of Concur muscle memory and a Delta corporate desk relationship she likely built personally. If the Latitude desk shows up Monday morning and starts loading standing instructions without her input, she will quietly route around them. The retention curve in months 4-9 will look fine and then collapse at the renewal. The mitigation is to make Janelle's first 30-minute call with the desk a *listening* call, not a setup call — the desk asks her what's working in her current stack and explicitly designs around it. This is the single highest-leverage operational move post-close and the rep should hand the account to a desk lead who understands it.

## Section 4: MODEL + FTG SCORECARD

**Did the price land or stick?** Landed. The $349 price was named once, defended once ("the hundred dollars a month is buying the thing you said you wanted"), and not revisited. The friction moment was the explicit $249→$349 comparison, which Brett raised himself. The rep didn't flinch and didn't discount. The annual ratio (3.5% of T&E vs 4-7% benchmark) sealed it. Friction score: **low**. Stick probability at 12 months: **~85% conditional on clean first trip.**

**Did the FTG mechanic move the buyer?** Decisively. Brett's own line — "Last week I waved it off because I didn't trust the price structure enough to care. Now I care" — is the cleanest possible read on FTG sequencing. The price model had to land before the guarantee could carry weight. Once Brett trusted the price was honest, FTG1's specificity (one trip, named, $1K labor ceiling) felt proportionate, not gimmicky. The "name the trip that scares you" reframe converted it from a trust prop into a tactical tool. The $1K labor credit felt adequate at $349/mo because the rep anchored it against senior agent rates ($160/hr × 6 hours) — without that anchor, $1K on a $4,188 ARR contract would have read as token.

**Would the buyer have bought under a different model?**
- **Model A ($249 + escalator):** Already tested in R7 Sim 7 — stalled. The escalator was the deal-killer. No.
- **Model C ($499 three-tier):** Brett would have rejected the $499 Atlas Office tier and tried to negotiate into the $249 Plus tier, which doesn't support 3+ principals. Mis-fit.
- **Model B is the only model that closes this buyer.**

**Net read.** Model B + FTG1 is the correct fit for the M&A managing-partner archetype. The flat-rate structure eliminates the procurement-audit objection that kills Model A in this segment. FTG1's labor credit is adequate *because* it is paired with a named-trip mechanic — without the named trip, the $1K would feel thin; with it, the instrument is specific enough to feel real. Recommendation: **make Model B + FTG1 the default offer for sub-$200K T&E M&A and PE-adjacent partner buyers.** Escalate to FTG2 only when the buyer has been burned by a prior travel vendor in the last 18 months.
