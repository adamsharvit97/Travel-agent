## Section 1: TRANSCRIPT (~1,500 words)

**Setting:** Tuesday, 11:05 AM ET. Zoom. Vanessa Coyne (VC), principal of Coyne Wealth, Charleston SC — $410M AUM, founded 2009, eight advisors, EA Marcia of seven years. Founder of Latitude 43 (F) on camera from Toronto. Vanessa booked the 45-minute slot through the calendar link in the founder's LinkedIn post about why she stopped using Amex Platinum's travel desk.

**F:** Vanessa, thanks for the calendar pin. Before I ask anything — what made you click? LinkedIn posts get a lot of nods and not a lot of bookings.

**VC:** *(small laugh)* The post about the Platinum desk re-routing your client through Frankfurt to save $180. That was almost word-for-word a call I had with Marcia in February. We had a client in Geneva, the desk routed him through Frankfurt to save what turned out to be $240, and he missed a private bank meeting. So I read your post and I thought — okay, this person has actually felt this.

**F:** That client cost you what, internally?

**VC:** *(pause)* Hard to put a number on it. He didn't fire us. But the meeting was the entire reason for the trip and we — *we* — paid for the rebook in business class home because that was the only way to make it not be a disaster. So call it $9,000 visible. Call it whatever-relationship-damage invisible.

**F:** Okay. I want to spend the next 35 minutes actually understanding your travel. Not pitching. If at the end Latitude's not right, I'll tell you. Fair?

**VC:** That'd be a first this quarter, but fair.

**F:** Walk me through how a trip currently happens. Start with Marcia.

**VC:** Marcia handles roughly 80% of my travel and about 60% of the advisors'. We're on Concur because compliance wants the receipts in one place. For anything custom — and most of my client trips are custom, I do site visits, family office meetings, sometimes I'll meet a prospect at their vineyard or their boat — Marcia will use a Virtuoso advisor we've worked with for six years. Her name is Linnea, she's based in Atlanta.

**F:** And Linnea charges how?

**VC:** $350 per trip planning fee, waived if there's a hotel commission. She's good. She's not fast. *(pause)* And she's increasingly not available — she took on a corporate account last year and our turnaround went from 48 hours to about a week.

**F:** What's the trip volume across you and the eight advisors?

**VC:** Me personally — probably 22 trips a year, half client-facing. The advisors collectively, maybe another 40. So sixty-something. T&E budget I sign off on is roughly $150K, that includes conferences.

**F:** Of those 22 of yours — how many would you call "high-design" trips? Meaning: not just a flight and a hotel, but actual itinerary work, sequencing, ground, the dinner reservation that has to land?

**VC:** *(thinks)* Of mine? Probably eight. Maybe ten if it's a heavy client year. Of the advisors' — almost none. They're going to Schwab IMPACT, they're going to Orion Ascent. They need a flight, a hotel, an Uber. They are not going to Provence.

**F:** Okay, that's useful. Tell me about a recent design trip that went wrong or right — whichever's more vivid.

**VC:** Right and wrong, same trip. October. I took two clients and their spouses to Napa for two nights — it was a thank-you for a referral that turned into $40M in new assets. Linnea designed it. The Auberge property was perfect. The driver was perfect. The dinner at SingleThread — wait, no, it was Press — was perfect. The wrong part was that Linnea booked me on a connection through Dallas that left at 6 AM, and I landed in Charleston at midnight, and I had a client review at 8 AM the next morning that I was useless for.

**F:** Did you tell her she'd screwed up the back end?

**VC:** *(beat)* No. Because the front end was so good I didn't want to be the difficult client.

**F:** That's the most expensive sentence anyone's said to me this week. Hold that thought. Marcia — how does she feel about Linnea?

**VC:** Marcia *adores* Linnea. They've been working together six years. If I switched providers Marcia would have a quiet but real opinion about it.

**F:** Got it. So any conversation we have has to make sense to Marcia, not just you. *(pause)* Let me ask the uncomfortable one. The Virtuoso fee — $350 a trip, waived on commissioned hotels — across your 22 trips, what do you actually pay her in a year?

**VC:** *(opens laptop, types)* Last calendar year… $4,200 in planning fees that didn't get waived. Plus — and I've never added this up — I think we tip her at Christmas, $500. So call it $4,700.

**F:** And how often do you wish you could call her at 11 PM on a Sunday?

**VC:** *(laughs)* Almost never on a Sunday. About once a quarter I wish I could call her at 7 AM on a Monday and she doesn't pick up until 10.

**F:** Okay. Let me tell you what Latitude actually is, and then let me tell you the part you're going to push back on, because you will.

**VC:** Go.

**F:** Latitude is a travel office. We're hosted under Fora — meaning we have the supplier access of a top-tier agency, the Virtuoso-equivalent relationships, the hotel programs, the upgrades. The membership has two tiers. Atlas Light is $99 a month — one traveler, basic booking, Marcia can email the desk for a flight or a hotel and we handle it inside 60 minutes during US daytime. Atlas Office is $249 a month — up to five active travelers under one account, includes a companion seat for Marcia so she's transacting inside our system directly, and you get our PHT-time-zone coverage during the day plus me personally on evenings, weekends, and overnights. Sixty-minute SLA on anything urgent.

**VC:** Okay. And the design work? The Napa-style trip?

**F:** Here's the part you'll push on. Design is a separate fee structure. Zero design fee on trips under $5,000 total spend. Above that, a design escalator — $250 at the low end up to $5,000 for the most complex multi-city builds. **Members get 50% off the escalator.** So your Napa trip — that probably designs at, what, $1,200 list, $600 to you as a member.

**VC:** *(long pause)* So I'd be paying $249 a month — $2,988 a year — *plus* $600 for the Napa trip, plus probably another $400-$600 for the other design trips. I'm doing the math live. That's $5,000-ish all-in.

**F:** Yes.

**VC:** And right now I'm paying Linnea $4,700 and she's slow.

**F:** Yes.

**VC:** So you're $300 more for — what, exactly? Convince me. Because if you tell me "faster, better, more access" I'm going to roll my eyes.

**F:** I'm not going to. I'll tell you three things and you decide. One: Marcia gets a seat inside our system. She stops being the relay. She books directly and we backstop. Two: the eight advisors who currently get nothing custom — they get the $99-tier-equivalent included under your Office account, meaning Schwab IMPACT trips for them happen inside our system at zero design fee, and if one of them ever does need design it's at 50% off. Three: when you land in Charleston at midnight before an 8 AM review, that's because *I* didn't catch it. Linnea booked the front end. We'd own the whole envelope, including the part where I push back on you and say, "Vanessa, fly out a day earlier or take the nonstop."

**VC:** *(quiet)* The third one is the one that matters.

**F:** I know.

**VC:** What's the catch on the design fee? I'm a principal. I don't love paying twice — subscription and per-trip.

**F:** It's a fair tension. The honest answer: design work is real labor. A 5-day Napa build with reservations and ground and the SingleThread sequencing is 6-8 hours of human time. If I bundled it into the $249 I'd either have to triple the membership or ration the design, and I won't do either. The 50% member discount is the lever. You're paying less per trip than Linnea, and the subscription is buying you the rest of the desk — the IROP coverage, the advisors' booking access, Marcia's seat.

**VC:** *(thinks)* Okay. Tell me about the guarantee. You mentioned it in the post.

**F:** First Trip Guarantee. If your first trip with us fails on our end — not weather, not the airline going bankrupt, on *our* end — we refund that month's membership, we give you the next month free, and we issue a $500 service credit toward a future trip. So if we blow it, you're out nothing and you have $500 of our work in your pocket.

**VC:** *(pause)* That's generous to the point of suspicious.

**F:** *(small laugh)* I know. Two reasons. One: I'd rather eat the cost than have you tell six RIA principals at a Charleston dinner that Latitude was fine. Two: it's a deliberate-buyer signal. You're not going to abuse it. You're going to use it once, if at all, and it's going to be the moment where you decide if we're real.

**VC:** *(long pause)* I want to think. I have a board-equivalent advisor I run vendors past. Can I come back to you Thursday?

**F:** Yes. One ask — when you talk to him, tell him the price is $249 a month plus design at 50% off the list. Don't soften it. If he flinches at $249 he's the wrong advisor for this decision, and I'd rather you find out now.

**VC:** *(laughs)* Noted. Thursday.

## Section 2: SALES COACH EVALUATION (~700 words)

**Outcome:** Soft yes pending advisor consult Thursday. Probability of close: 70-75%. This was a competent, deliberately-paced discovery call that earned the right to ask for the price. The founder did not oversell, did not flinch on the $249, and surfaced the single most valuable insight of the call — Vanessa's *"I didn't want to be the difficult client"* moment — by listening rather than pitching.

**What worked, specifically:**

The opening question — *"What made you click?"* — was the single best move on the call. It surfaced a concrete pain story (the Frankfurt re-route) in the first 90 seconds and let the founder anchor everything that followed to a real, recent, named failure. Compare to a generic "tell me about your travel" — Vanessa would have given the rehearsed answer.

The diagnostic on Linnea was surgical. The founder did not denigrate the competitor; he priced her. *"$4,200 in planning fees… $4,700 with the Christmas tip."* Vanessa's own math became the anchor against which $249/mo had to be evaluated, not against zero. This is the right way to handle an incumbent: make her count it.

The Napa story extraction was the move of the call. *"Tell me about a recent design trip that went wrong or right."* The founder got both a success story AND the failure mode (Dallas connection, useless next morning) in one answer, and then drove the dagger in: *"That's the most expensive sentence anyone's said to me this week."* He named the cost of her conflict avoidance with Linnea. That single line repositioned Latitude from "another option" to "the thing that would have caught it."

The price drop was clean. *"$99… $249… here's the part you'll push on."* He pre-named the objection (paying twice), which is the founder's strongest verbal habit — narrating the buyer's likely reaction before she has it. Vanessa's *"I'm doing the math live"* moment is the buyer doing the founder's work for him.

The FTG2 delivery was textbook for a deliberate buyer. He framed it not as risk reversal but as a *signal* — *"a deliberate-buyer signal. You're not going to abuse it."* For Vanessa, who is 49, principal, eight advisors below her, this matters more than the dollar value of the guarantee. She read it as confidence, not desperation.

**What broke:**

The founder under-explored the Marcia dimension. Marcia's six-year relationship with Linnea is the single biggest unmanaged risk in this deal, and the founder noted it (*"any conversation we have has to make sense to Marcia"*) but did not propose a concrete way to bring Marcia in. He should have offered a 20-minute call with Marcia before Thursday. That omission could cost the deal if Marcia goes home Tuesday night and tells Vanessa Linnea-is-fine.

He also did not quantify the eight-advisor benefit. The advisors' 40 trips/year of basic booking is included in the $249 Office tier — that's enormous and he mentioned it in passing as point two of three. It deserved its own beat. At a hypothetical $30 of advisor time saved per booking, that's $1,200/year of operational lift the founder left on the table.

He did not anchor against the cost of the *next* failure. The Geneva re-route cost $9,000 visible. He could have said: "One catch like that pays for the membership for three years."

**Three improvements:**

1. **Bring Marcia in earlier.** Offer the EA call inside the discovery, not after the close. EAs kill more RIA deals than principals do.
2. **Quantify the advisor tier benefit explicitly** — name a dollar figure for the 40 advisor trips/year handled under the Office account.
3. **Use the failure cost as a price anchor.** "One Frankfurt re-route a year pays for this for three years" is the line that should have closed the price segment.

**Most important behavioral change for this rep:** *Stop being symmetric with deliberate buyers.* The founder treated Vanessa like a peer who needed honesty. That worked. But deliberate buyers also want one moment of advocacy — one moment where the founder says "you should do this and here's why." The call ended on her terms, not his. For a 70%-likelihood deal, end on a clearer ask: "Vanessa, I think Office tier is right for you. Talk to your advisor. I'll send you a one-pager he can read in four minutes. Thursday at 11?" That's the missing close.

## Section 3: BUSINESS EVALUATOR (~700 words)

**Unit economics at this conversion:**

Vanessa converts on Atlas Office at $249/mo = **$2,988 ARR base**. Layer in design escalator revenue: she has 8-10 design trips/year of hers, plus assume 1-2 from the advisors. At an average of $600/trip member-discounted design fee (mid-range; Napa-class trips are $600, simpler ones $250), that's roughly **$5,400-$6,600/year in design revenue**, of which Latitude books gross margin perhaps 40-50% after planner labor (call it $2,400-$3,000 contribution).

So Vanessa's total annual contribution: **~$2,988 subscription + ~$2,700 design margin = $5,688 contribution**, with subscription effectively all-margin (the desk is a fixed cost that's already being paid for the first 10-15 members).

Critically, **the design escalator is doing real work in this account**. Without it, Vanessa is a $2,988 ACV. With it, she's roughly $8,400 gross / $5,700 contribution. The design fee is not a tax on her — it's the lever that makes the $249 sustainable without rationing labor. This is the central thesis of Model A vs. Model B: Vanessa is a heavy-design buyer, and Model A captures the design value directly. Model B at $349 Unlimited would be **worse for the desk** in her case — she'd pay $4,188 and consume ~$3,500 of design labor, leaving roughly $700 contribution. Model A is the right model for high-design buyers; Model B is the right model for low-design Atlas Office buyers running point-to-point flights.

**Strategic positioning learnings:**

The diagnostic surfaced a structural insight: **RIA principals have a hidden incumbent — the long-tenured Virtuoso advisor — that is both a competitor AND a relationship asset the EA owns.** The founder cannot win this segment by competing on price against the Virtuoso advisor (Linnea is $4,700 to Latitude's ~$8,400). He wins by repositioning *what's being bought*. Latitude is not a planning fee replacement — it's a desk that absorbs the Virtuoso advisor's function while also covering the eight advisors and the IROP risk. This is a different category, not a cheaper version.

The Charleston-RIA niche is the bullseye. Eight advisors below her means latent expansion: if Vanessa converts to Office and the advisors actually use it, there's a natural upgrade path to a custom enterprise tier at year two ($500-$750/mo equivalent) once the EA-Companion-Seat usage data justifies it.

**Retention probability:** High — 80-85% at 24 months. Vanessa is a deliberate buyer who paid down conflict avoidance with Linnea for years rather than switch. That same trait works in Latitude's favor once she's a member: she will tolerate small failures and stay if the operator is confessional about them. Her churn risk is not the price — it's Marcia silently routing trips back to Linnea. EA enablement in month one is the retention lever.

**Referral value:** Exceptional. Vanessa explicitly named "six RIA principals at a Charleston dinner" as her reference group. RIA principals refer through tight, geographic, profession-specific networks. One Vanessa is structurally worth 1.5-2 net new members within 18 months, with effectively zero CAC. **Vanessa is a flagship account, not a margin account.** The right move is to optimize the first 90 days for storytelling, not for upsell.

**The hidden risk:**

The hidden risk is not Marcia, though Marcia is the visible one. The hidden risk is **the design fee narrative drifting from "50% off the escalator" to "they nickel-and-dimed me."** Vanessa explicitly said *"I don't love paying twice."* That sentence is a tracer round. If Latitude bills her $600 for Napa design *after* she's already paying $249/mo, and the trip is anything less than excellent, the second-payment friction becomes the story she tells at the Charleston dinner — and it becomes the story Marcia tells Linnea ("see, this is why we should have stayed").

The mitigation is **proactive pricing transparency on every design build**. Before any design fee invoices, the desk sends a one-paragraph scope-and-price note: "Napa Oct 12-15, design scope X, list fee $1,200, member fee $600, approve to proceed." That converts the second payment from "another bill" to "a quote I approved." This is operationally trivial and strategically essential for Model A buyers in this segment.

FTG2 is the right mechanic for Vanessa specifically — the free month + $500 credit signals confidence to a deliberate buyer in a way FTG1's labor credit ($1,000 of rework) would not have. FTG3's 30-day money-back would have felt transactional and beneath her. FTG2 is the only one of the three that reads as *operator confidence* rather than *risk insurance*.

## Section 4: MODEL + FTG SCORECARD (~300 words)

**Did the $249 price land or stick?**
**Landed, with one tracer-round of friction.** Vanessa did the math live (*"$2,988 plus $600 plus $400-600… $5,000-ish all-in"*) and named the cognitive friction directly: *"I don't love paying twice — subscription and per-trip."* The founder held the price cleanly with the labor-honesty answer (*"design is real labor… I won't ration it"*) and the 50%-off framing reframed the escalator as a member benefit rather than a fee. **Friction score: 4/10 — present but not dealbreaking.** The risk is post-sale narrative drift, not pre-sale objection.

**Did FTG2 move the buyer?**
**Helpful, not decisive.** Vanessa's response — *"generous to the point of suspicious"* — is the deliberate-buyer tell. She read the free month + $500 credit as a confidence signal, not an insurance product. It mattered, but it was not the lever that closed her. The Napa-story repositioning was. **FTG2 score: 7/10 for this buyer.** It does its job by lowering the barrier to "I'll talk to my advisor Thursday" rather than "let me think for two weeks."

**Would she have bought under a different model?**
- **Model B ($349 Unlimited):** She'd have hesitated harder on the $349 sticker — but ironically would have been a *worse* unit for Latitude (heavy design consumption against fixed price). She might have bought, but the relationship would have been margin-negative.
- **Model C ($499 three-tier):** She'd have routed to the Plus tier at $249, behaviorally identical to Model A — Model C adds no value here.

**Net read:** **Model A is correctly fit to Vanessa.** She is a high-design, deliberate, network-rich buyer where capturing design revenue per-trip is more sustainable than bundling it. **FTG2 is the right mechanic** for her specifically — it reads as operator confidence rather than transactional insurance. **Model A + FTG2 is the right pairing for the Charleston RIA segment.**
