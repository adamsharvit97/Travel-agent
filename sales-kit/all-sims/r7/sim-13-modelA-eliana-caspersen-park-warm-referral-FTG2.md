## Section 2 — Sales Coach Evaluation (~700 words)

**Outcome:** Closed. Atlas Office at $249/mo, first trip named (Kyoto, November anniversary), EA onboarding scheduled within two weeks, expectation set for an 18-month Society conversation. Call wrapped 14 minutes under hold — a positive signal in a warm-referral context, not a negative one.

**What worked, with specifics.**

The opening line — "the only context in which I want him talking about me" — did three things at once: acknowledged the referral, signaled comfort with the relationship, and refused to grovel. Warm intros are often botched by founders who over-thank the referrer; Marc treated James as a peer, which is the register Eliana lives in. She mirrored it immediately ("I wrote it down").

The diagnostic shortcut at "you don't need a travel agent, you have one" was the single highest-leverage move on the call. Eliana telegraphed in her second turn that she was not here to be discovered. Marc collapsed discovery into a one-paragraph hypothesis and let her correct him. She didn't have to. That saved 15 minutes and — more importantly — signaled that Marc had read the situation. For a family office principal, being understood quickly is a quality marker.

The handling of the price-feels-too-low objection (which is the actual R7 question for this sim) was excellent. Marc did not flinch, did not raise the price, did not invent a higher tier. He explained the unit economics honestly: membership is access, commission is engine. The line "we make money when you travel, we don't make money by charging you a number that matches your AUM" is reusable and should be canonized. It reframes the $249 from "suspiciously cheap" to "structurally correct."

The Society mention was textbook restraint. He named it, dated it (late 2027), refused to sell it, and tied it back to her timeline (12–18 months). The phrase "I won't sell vaporware" is the kind of confessional register the brand requires. He did not overplay the hand.

The FTG2 handling was the most interesting moment. Eliana correctly identified that FTG2 is over-engineered for her trust level. Marc's pivot — "don't think of it as a thing I'm selling, think of it as a thing I'm publishing… how I keep my own team honest" — converted a feature she didn't need into a discipline she could respect. That's a save, not a sale.

**What broke.**

Almost nothing, but two small things. First, Marc never asked about her husband by name or her children's travel patterns. For a $250K T&E family office account, those data points should have been captured on the close call, not deferred to the Margaret onboarding. He left intel on the table.

Second, the "honest sentence" at the end — warning her that Atlas Office might not be enough — was strategically smart but slightly too long. He made the point in two sentences and then kept talking. With this buyer, he should have stopped after "that's a real outcome" and let her respond.

**Three improvements.**

1. **Capture traveler intel on the close call, not later.** Names, frequency, preferred carriers, the husband's status with which alliance. A two-minute checklist would have done it. Margaret can fill gaps; she shouldn't be the source of record.

2. **Shorten the honesty monologue.** Marc's best moments on this call were his shortest. The Society note was 35 seconds and perfect. The "honest sentence" was 50 seconds and started to drift. Discipline the close.

3. **Name the renewal moment explicitly.** He hinted at 12–18 months but didn't put a date on the calendar. With a referred family office principal, putting a soft "let's revisit at month nine" stake in the ground would have started the Society conversion clock without overplaying.

**The most important behavioral change for this rep.**

Trust the warm intro enough to stop selling earlier. Marc closed this deal somewhere around minute 14. He kept selling until minute 26. Half of those last twelve minutes were value-add (the FTG reframing, the Society note, the Margaret requirement). The other half was him not noticing the deal was already done. With referred buyers at this tier, the post-close conversation is the deal — book the next interaction and get off the phone.

## Section 3 — Business Evaluator (~700 words)

**Unit economics at this conversion.**

Atlas Office at $249/mo yields $2,988 in annual membership revenue. That is, as Eliana correctly observed, trivial against her $250K T&E. The real economics are downstream. A $250K T&E family office with two principal travelers plus occasional family travel will generate, conservatively, $80–120K in commissionable hotel and tour spend annually. Fora-hosted commission rates in the 10–15% blended range imply $8K–$18K in commission revenue, of which Latitude retains its split (call it $5K–$12K net). Add the membership and you're at $8K–$15K in annual contribution from this account. That is 3–5x the median Atlas Office economics and arguably the most profitable seat on the book.

The strategic question is whether $249 is the right number for that revenue profile. Defensible answer: yes, because the membership is doing brand work, not P&L work. Charging Eliana $999/mo would have closed (she said as much), but it would have set a price expectation that Latitude cannot consistently deliver against at scale for $999 customers — and it would have made the upgrade to Society feel like a renegotiation rather than a graduation. Holding the line at $249 preserves the Society ramp. This is a 2027 decision dressed up as a 2026 close.

**Strategic positioning learnings.**

The R7 question — does the warm intro make $249 feel undersized — surfaces a real risk: under-pricing the top of the funnel can read as under-confidence. Marc neutralized it with the unit economics explanation, but the fact that he had to is a signal. At the family office tier, the membership number is not the binding constraint; the perceived seriousness of the operation is. The mitigation is operational: deliver the Kyoto trip at a level that confirms the price was a feature, not a bug.

The second positioning learning is that the warm referral channel is a different product than cold inbound. Eliana skipped discovery, skipped objection-handling on FTG, and accepted Society as a deferred promise. None of that happens on a cold call. If 20% of Latitude's growth comes through warm intros from existing members, the sales motion for that segment should be explicitly different — shorter, tighter, more confessional, with an EA-onboarding requirement and a 12-month Society checkpoint baked in.

**Retention probability.**

High, with one fork. If the Kyoto trip goes well, Eliana is a 36-month member with a >70% probability of converting to Society at launch. The forcing function is Margaret. If Margaret is brought in well, she becomes the operational backbone of the account and retention stabilizes. If Margaret feels displaced, she will quietly route around Latitude and the account hollows out — the Sarah/Sophie problem from Stage 4. Marc's insistence on a non-optional 30-minute Margaret call was the single most important retention move on this call.

**Referral value.**

Eliana sits inside two networks that are extraordinarily valuable to Latitude: the Greenwich family office community (high-density, high-trust, low-volume buyers, exactly Society's target) and the broader RIA/PE principal world her husband and children's careers will touch. A single warm intro from Eliana 12 months from now is worth more than 30 cold inbounds. The implication: the account team should explicitly invest in over-delivery on this account for the first six months, treating it as a reference account, not a revenue account.

**The hidden risk.**

Margaret. Not Eliana. Eliana is sold. But Margaret has 22 years of tenure, an established workflow, and a defensive posture toward outsiders. The Sarah/Sophie failure mode — EA defaults back to the legacy provider — is the highest-probability failure mode here. Marc named it on the call ("the members who don't introduce their EA are the members who churn at month four") but the operationalization of the Margaret relationship is now the single point of failure. The desk should treat Margaret as a co-buyer for the first 90 days: weekly check-ins, named relationship owner, explicit acknowledgement of her authority on the account. If Margaret is not won by month two, the account is at risk regardless of how well Kyoto goes.

Secondary hidden risk: the Society 2027 promise. If Society slips to 2028 or gets repositioned, Eliana will remember the conversation Marc had with her in May 2026. He should log this expectation in CRM and the leadership team should treat it as a commitment, not a directional statement.

## Section 4 — Model + FTG Scorecard (~300 words)

**Did the price land or stick?**

Landed, but with the friction the R7 brief anticipated. Eliana flagged $249 as suspiciously low ("makes me slightly nervous"). Marc held the price and reframed it via unit economics. Friction moment: minute 9, "two forty-nine a month" followed by a real pause. Resolution moment: minute 11, "the membership is the access fee, the commission is the engine." Net: price stuck, but only because Marc had a credible structural answer. Without that answer, $249 to a family office principal reads as under-confidence. **Score: 7/10.** Landed, but Model A is structurally undersized for this buyer; the win is temporary.

**Did the FTG mechanic move the buyer?**

No, and the buyer said so explicitly. Eliana waved off FTG2 — "I don't need that." Marc's recovery — repositioning the guarantee as an internal discipline rather than a buyer-facing concession — was a real save, but the mechanic itself did zero work on this buyer. FTG2's "maximum trust signal" framing is wasted on a referred buyer who already has maximum trust. **Score: 3/10 as a closing lever; 6/10 as a brand artifact** because the reframe gave Marc a line he can reuse.

**Would the buyer have bought under a different model?**

Yes to all three. Model B (Unlimited $349) would have closed cleanly and felt more proportionate to her T&E — likely the strongest fit. Model C ($499 Office) would also have closed and would have answered her "this is too cheap" reaction directly. Model A closed but required the most rhetorical work.

**Net read.**

For this buyer at this stage, **Model B with no FTG mention** would have been optimal. Model A + FTG2 is the worst-fit pairing on the matrix for a warm-referral family-office close — under-priced offer, over-engineered guarantee. Marc closed it on rep skill, not on product fit. The lesson for the playbook: warm-intro family-office prospects should default to Model B (or Society pre-launch positioning) and skip the FTG conversation entirely.
