## Section 2: SALES COACH EVALUATION (~700 words)

**Outcome:** Closed at full price, month-to-month, no discount given, two non-price concessions deployed (founder-on-brief pilot, peer intro). Year-in-Review offered but declined by buyer — which is actually a win, because it means the buyer self-selected the concessions that mattered to him. Strong close.

**What worked specifically.**

The opening move was the most important moment in the call. *"The answer on both of those is no — and I want to tell you exactly why before I tell you what I can do."* That single sentence does three things at once: it holds the line, it pre-empts the buyer's expectation of a counter-offer, and it earns the right to be heard on the reframe. Tomasz is a professional negotiator. He respects the no precisely because it arrives without hedging. The classic founder mistake here is to soften — *"well, let me see what I can do"* — which signals movement is possible and trains the buyer to keep pushing. The founder didn't soften. That's the call.

The second strong moment: *"I'm offering you a one-month commit at full price. The risk is already on my side of the table."* This is the structural reframe of the negotiation. Tomasz came in framing the deal as buyer-takes-risk-vendor-extracts-money, and asked for a discount as risk compensation. The founder flipped the frame: *the risk is already with the seller*, in two specific forms — month-to-month terms and FTG1. Once the frame flipped, the discount stopped making sense even to Tomasz, who is sophisticated enough to recognize a clean structural argument when he hears one.

The third strong moment: explicit repositioning of FTG1 as *"I'm already taking risk for you. Before you've paid me a full quarter."* This is the deliverable the brief specifically called out, and it landed. The founder named the $1K labor credit in concrete terms (fifteen rework hours, not eight), and then immediately pivoted to the *meaning* of the dollars rather than the dollars themselves — margin-on-the-table-before-renewal versus the SaaS norm of margin-protection-until-churn. Tomasz pushed back on the $1K being small relative to the month price, and the founder didn't defend the number; he reframed it as a signal, which is the correct move when the literal number is contestable.

**What broke.**

One real weakness: the founder offered three concessions when he should have offered two. Naming the Year-in-Review alongside the pilot and the peer intro diluted the menu and let Tomasz pick-and-choose, which is exactly what professional negotiators do when you give them too many options. The Year-in-Review is a month-eleven artifact and doesn't belong in a day-zero negotiation. The cleaner play would have been pilot-plus-peer as a tight two-item package, with the Year-in-Review held in reserve for the renewal conversation eleven months later — where it actually does retention work.

Second weakness: the founder said *"clever"* was Tomasz's reaction to the price-integrity argument, then accepted *"I don't entirely buy it but it's clever"* and moved on. That's a missed opportunity to deepen the price-anchor argument with one more concrete proof point — for example, a story about a real member who downgraded their own engagement after asking for and receiving a discount. Stories beat logic with negotiator-type buyers. The founder went logic-only.

Third weakness: the founder never asked Tomasz what *specifically* was driving the discount ask. Was it budget? Internal optics? Habit? Tomasz volunteered the *"I deal with vendors for a living"* framing, which is the habit answer, but a sharper rep would have probed for the budget answer too. The $80K personal T&E means $249/mo is 3.7% of his annual travel spend — almost certainly not a real budget constraint. Naming that math out loud would have closed the discount conversation faster.

**Three improvements.**

1. Cut the concession menu to two items, not three. Hold the Year-in-Review for renewal.
2. When the buyer says *"clever but I don't buy it,"* don't move on. Deepen with a story.
3. Diagnose the real driver of the discount ask before reframing. *"What's actually driving the number for you — budget, optics, or habit?"* takes ten seconds and surfaces the real lever.

**Most important behavioral change for this rep:** stop offering concessions in threes. Two is a choice. Three is a menu. Menus train buyers to negotiate. Choices close them.

**Strategic positioning.** The more interesting read is what the *negotiation itself* signals about Model A's position in market. Tomasz didn't object to the value or the model architecture. PE associates with $80K personal T&E are not price-sensitive at $249; they are *negotiation-sensitive*. They need to feel they extracted something. **Retention probability.** High. Three reasons. First, the buyer self-selected the concessions that matter to him, which means he has personal ownership of the deal structure — a known retention predictor versus buyers who passively accept what's offered. **Referral value.** This is where the deal gets interesting. Tomasz is at a $2.4B mid-market PE shop. The conversion math gets aggressive — one happy PE associate in year one likely generates 2-4 warm intros in years one and two. The founder's refusal to buy a paid testimonial actually protects this dynamic. The day-90 organic referral from Tomasz, if it comes, is worth 10x the discounted-testimonial version because it carries the credibility of "I paid full price and chose to talk." This is the McKinsey-canonical *signal-versus-noise* trade — and the founder picked signal correctly.

**The hidden risk.** The hidden risk is not Tomasz. The hidden risk is the founder's *next* negotiation with a less sophisticated buyer who has heard, through the inevitable PE-LinkedIn-grapevine, that "Tom held the price but threw in a personal brief review and a peer intro." Concessions leak. The pilot-with-founder-on-the-brief structure is currently a true exception — but if it becomes a standard expectation among PE-segment buyers, the founder has just inadvertently re-priced his own time at zero. Mitigation: document the concession menu internally with explicit caps (founder-on-brief reserved for first ten PE-segment closes, then retired or repriced as a paid add-on). If the founder doesn't put a fence around this, the concession that won this deal becomes the cost-of-doing-business in eighteen months.

The deeper strategic note: the founder has discovered something real in this sim — that *non-price concessions consistently close negotiation-type buyers at full price*. That's worth productizing. But productizing concessions is what creates the leakage risk. The discipline is to keep the menu small, the supply scarce, and the framing always personal.
