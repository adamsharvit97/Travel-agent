# SALES SIMULATION: Thomas Voss — The 90-Second Buyer

---

## 1. TRANSCRIPT

**[Zoom connects. Thomas's camera flicks on, then partially off. He's clearly on a second monitor.]**

**FOUNDER (Maya):** Thomas — thanks for the —

**THOMAS:** Yeah, hi. Listen, I have fifteen minutes, and honestly closer to ten. Bottom line — what does this cost and what do I get? Because if this is Navan with a fancier website, I'm going to save us both some time.

**[Thomas glances at his watch. Eight seconds in.]**

**FOUNDER:** Okay. Fair. Three tiers — Principal $3,600 a year, Office $12,000, Firm $36,000. Based on the fact that you travel eighteen times a year and have an EA, you are an Office buyer. Twelve thousand a year. Buys you a named human, one Slack channel, sixty-minute response 24/7, and the Virtuoso and Four Seasons Preferred Partner rates your EA cannot access on Expedia. That's it. That's the whole thing.

**THOMAS:** How is this not Navan.

**FOUNDER:** Navan is software your finance team logs into. We're a person your assistant emails. Navan's AI confirms seat 12A. We call the GM at the Mandarin when your flight cancels at 11pm. Different category — some of our members use both.

**THOMAS:** Mm. And my EA does all this already.

**FOUNDER:** Maybe. Quick test — last time a flight went sideways, did your EA fix it before you noticed, or did you find out from the airline app at the gate?

**[Three-second pause. Thomas leans back. This is the only moment of the call where his eyes leave the second monitor.]**

**THOMAS:** ...Fine. The app, usually. Look, send me the one-pager. I'll read it tonight.

**FOUNDER:** I'll send it inside the hour. Two things before you go — can I get fifteen minutes next Tuesday or Thursday to walk you through what an Office membership actually looks like in a normal travel week? I'll keep it to fifteen. I'll send a calendar hold with both options, you pick.

**THOMAS:** Tuesday. Maybe. Send the hold, I'll see if it survives the week. What time?

**FOUNDER:** 7:45am your time, before your day starts. Fifteen minutes. If by minute three you don't see it, you hang up and I refund you the twelve minutes.

**THOMAS:** *(half-smile, first one)* Fine. Send everything. I gotta jump.

**FOUNDER:** One last thing — the worst trip you had this year, just the city. One word.

**THOMAS:** Frankfurt.

**FOUNDER:** Got it. Talk Tuesday.

**[Thomas drops. Total elapsed: 4 minutes 20 seconds.]**

## 2. OUTCOME

**"Send me materials" — leaning toward soft yes on the follow-up.**

Not a sign. Not a walk. A *conditional Tuesday*. Realistic probability the Tuesday call actually happens: ~55%. Realistic probability of conversion **if** Tuesday happens and the one-pager lands tonight: ~40%. Net expected close from this single touch: ~22%, which for a 4-minute call is actually strong — but only because the founder abandoned the playbook entirely inside the first ten seconds.

The "Frankfurt" extraction at the end is the most valuable artifact of the entire call. That's the pain handle Maya will lead with on Tuesday: *"Last time we spoke you said Frankfurt. Walk me through it."* That's the diagnostic question from the playbook — just asked four days late.

## 3. PLAYBOOK WINS — what survived at 10 minutes

**Almost nothing. But three things did.**

1. **The pricing script survived verbatim.** "Principal $3,600, Office $12,000, Firm $36,000... based on what you described, you're in [Tier]" — this exact line from the playbook is the only piece that worked under fire. Crucially, the founder skipped "Then stop talking" and instead immediately delivered the value bundle, because Thomas would have used silence to leave.

2. **The Navan reframe held.** The playbook's FAQ answer — *"Navan is a software company... Their AI does not call the Mandarin Oriental on your behalf"* — compressed cleanly from four sentences to two and still landed. This is the cleanest part of the entire playbook because it was written for an objection, not for a conversational arc.

3. **The "EA already handles this" rebuttal — partially.** The playbook says *"Your EA is a generalist. Travel is a specialty that takes 10,000 hours."* Maya didn't use that line — too lecturing for ten seconds of patience. She used the *diagnostic* version: "did your EA fix it before you noticed?" That's a much better version of the rebuttal and the playbook should steal it back.

That's it. Three pieces. Everything else in a 30-minute playbook is unusable when the buyer's giving you ten.

## 4. PLAYBOOK BREAKS — quoted

**Break #1 — The Opening.**

> *"Thanks for the time. Quick housekeeping — I usually keep these to 20 minutes, then leave the last 10 for your questions. Fair? Good. Before we get into Latitude, I want to understand how you travel today and what's broken."*

This gets Maya hung up on at second twelve. Thomas does not care about housekeeping. He does not want to tell a stranger how he travels. The phrase "before we get into Latitude" is the exact construction that makes a CFO check his watch. **The playbook's opening assumes the buyer has agreed to be sold to.** Thomas has not.

**Break #2 — The Diagnostic.**

> *"Walk me through a typical week of travel for you. How many trips, how complex, who books it today?"*

This is an eight-minute section. Thomas gave Maya 4 minutes 20 seconds total. The diagnostic, as written, **cannot exist in a sub-15-minute call.** It's the most valuable section of the playbook and it's the first thing that has to go when the buyer is hostile to context-setting. There is no compressed version. There needs to be.

**Break #3 — The Reframe.**

> *"Two patterns I keep seeing. First, most operators in your seat aren't paying for travel — they're paying twice for it..."*

This is a beautifully written paragraph. It is also a 45-second monologue delivered to a man with 90 seconds of patience, where every word that isn't "price" or "what I get" is a word that increases the probability of being told to send the one-pager. **The reframe is content for minute six of a thirty-minute call. At minute one of a ten-minute call, it is suicide.**

**Break #4 — The trust-transfer sentence.**

> *"What I want to test on this call is whether you'd actually benefit from this. If you wouldn't, I'd rather tell you that now than sell you a year of something you don't need."*

The playbook says: *"That last sentence is the most important in the entire call. It's the trust transfer. Use it every time."* It cannot be used here. Thomas hears it as more throat-clearing before the offer. The trust transfer is a tool for buyers who will give you a sentence; Thomas will only give you a number. **The playbook's most important line is unusable on its highest-value buyer.**

**Break #5 — The Strong-Signal close.**

> *"Good. Two things. I'll send you an application — it's a short form, takes about ten minutes."*

Thomas will not fill out a ten-minute form tonight. He will not fill it out this week. The close assumes a buyer who has emotionally agreed and now wants logistics. Thomas has agreed to *receive a PDF*. The close needed for Thomas is a calendar hold with two options and a unilateral "I'll send it" — which Maya improvised. **The playbook has no close for "send me materials."**

## 5. THREE IMPROVEMENTS

### Improvement #1 — Add a "90-Second Elevator Version" to the playbook

The playbook needs a new top-level section, before "Opening," titled **"When the buyer gives you 90 seconds."** It should read approximately:

> *Some calls — usually with CFOs, GCs, and senior partners — open with "what does it cost and what do I get." You have ninety seconds before they decide. Do not run the diagnostic. Do not run the reframe. Run this:*
>
> *"Three tiers — $3,600, $12,000, $36,000. You're a [Tier] buyer. What it buys you: a named human, one channel, sixty-minute response, Virtuoso and Four Seasons rates your EA can't book. Different from Navan — Navan is software, we're a person. Different from your EA — they're a generalist, we do this 10,000 hours a year. The honest test is whether your last bad trip would have gone differently with us in the loop. Worth a fifteen-minute deeper look next week?"*
>
> *Then stop. Ask for the calendar. Send the one-pager inside the hour. Do not try to close on this call.*

This is the single largest hole in the existing playbook. It is built for a buyer who has accepted the frame "we are going to talk." A meaningful slice of the ICP — arguably the *highest-LTV* slice, since busy CFOs of 200-person companies are the dream Office tier buyer — will never accept that frame on call one.

### Improvement #2 — Replace the EA rebuttal with the diagnostic version

The playbook's current EA answer is a lecture: *"Your EA is a generalist. Travel is a specialty..."* Lectures lose to busy buyers. Maya stumbled into a better version on the call: **a one-question diagnostic that makes the buyer answer their own objection.**

> *"Last time a flight went sideways, did your EA fix it before you noticed, or did you find out from the airline app?"*

This is structurally superior because:
- It takes 4 seconds instead of 25.
- The buyer talks, not the seller — which is the playbook's own stated principle ("Listen for 80% of this section") but applied to objection handling.
- It surfaces the hidden truth (EA is mediocre, buyer hasn't noticed) that the lecture version cannot.

The playbook should make this the primary EA rebuttal and demote the "10,000 hours" line to a follow-on used only if the diagnostic question doesn't land.

### Improvement #3 — Build a "send-me-materials" close, because that's the actual most common outcome

The playbook has three closes: strong signal, medium signal, weak signal. **There is a fourth signal that is more common than all three combined for time-pressured buyers, and the playbook doesn't acknowledge it exists: the "send me materials" exit.**

Required addition:

> *"Send-me-materials" close:*
> *"Done — I'll send it inside the hour. Two options for a fifteen-minute follow-up next week — Tuesday 7:45am or Thursday 7:45am, before your day starts. I'll send both holds, you decline one. If by minute three of that call you don't see the value, hang up and I owe you the twelve minutes back. Deal?"*

The mechanics that matter:
- **Specific times, not "what works for you"** — busy buyers can't choose, they can only veto.
- **Pre-business-hours slots** — protects their actual calendar.
- **Unilateral action ("I'll send both holds")** — removes a step from their plate.
- **The "minute three" promise** — gives them an exit ramp, which paradoxically makes them more likely to stay past minute three.
- **The one-pager *first*, the call *second*** — inverts the playbook's current order, which assumes the call is the artifact and the one-pager is the follow-up. For Thomas-type buyers, the one-pager is the artifact and the call is the follow-up.

## CLOSING HARSH NOTE

The discovery playbook is well-crafted writing — but it is written for a buyer who has emotionally bought into having a conversation. The Thomas Voss persona is the most valuable buyer Latitude 43 will ever speak to, and the playbook would have lost him at second twelve. Maya only got the soft-Tuesday because she **disregarded the playbook's structure and ran an improvised compressed version**. That improvised version is the playbook's missing chapter. Until it's written down, every founder-sub or sales hire will lose every Thomas they get on the phone, and they won't know why — they'll just think "CFOs don't buy on the first call." That conclusion is wrong, and the playbook is what's making them believe it.
