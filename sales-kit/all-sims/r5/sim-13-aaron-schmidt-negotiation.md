## 4. PLAYBOOK BREAKS — quoted

The playbook has a hole the size of this entire call's second half. Aaron's three-move negotiating run was the deal — and the playbook offers nothing.

- **No negotiation section exists.** The "hard questions" cover *"Isn't this a lot of money for someone who travels twenty times a year?"* — which is a **value objection**, not a **negotiation gambit**. The playbook's answer (*"It's three hundred dollars a month... you're not buying ROI. You're buying never thinking about it again"*) is useless when the prospect already agrees on value and is now extracting price. Maya had to improvise the entire "no, and here's why no" architecture.
- **The playbook conflates "objection" with "ask."** Per playbook: *"The objection that almost killed it. What they hesitated on."* Aaron didn't hesitate. He attacked. The playbook has no taxonomy for distinguishing a stall ("let me think") from a probe ("what can you do for me") from a hard ask ("$20K or I walk"). Each needs a different response and the playbook treats them as one bucket.
- **No guidance on which concessions preserve dignity vs. signal weakness.** Maya invented the 90-day pilot, the in-person Year-in-Review, and the peer-member introduction on the fly. The playbook lists no pre-authorized concessions, no "things you can give that don't touch price." If a less experienced founder ran this call, they'd give the $20K because they had nothing else in the holster.
- **The "early customer / unproven firm" attack is uncovered.** The closest the playbook gets is *"How do I know you're not just going to disappear in six months?"* — answered with *"I'm under Fora Travel as a host."* That answer addresses **continuity risk**, not **discount-justification risk**. Aaron's "I'm doing you a favor" is a totally different attack and the playbook is silent.
- **No script for the multi-year-for-discount swap.** This is the single most common ask any subscription business hears. The playbook has zero language for it. Maya's response — "if I give it to you at twenty, I'm pricing under Office" — is correct but it's improvised math, not a rehearsed move. On a worse day, with a sharper buyer, that math wouldn't survive contact.
- **No script for the per-head pro-rata swap.** Same gap. Aaron's "$25K for 10" was actually his strongest move because it has internal logic. Maya's "Firm is priced by desk capacity not headcount" is *a good answer* but it isn't in the playbook anywhere.
- **The playbook ends at the close, not at the negotiation.** Per playbook: *"Three possible closes, by signal strength."* There is no fourth close labeled *"Strong signal, but they're going to try to take a chunk out of price first."* That's most of the actual book.

## 5. THREE IMPROVEMENTS — what the playbook needs

### Improvement 1: Add a "Holding Price" section between Reframe and Close.

The playbook needs an explicit module — call it **§ Holding Price** — sitting between *The Price* and *Their Questions*. It should open with the philosophy and then give scripts. Suggested content:

> **The discount ask is not an objection. It is a test.** Buyers in your segment respect founders who can articulate why their price is their price. If you discount, you have told them the number on the page was fiction, and they will discount you again at every renewal. **The price is the price. What flexes is the package, not the number.**

Then three rehearsed responses, one per common attack:

- **"Multi-year for a discount."** *"Multi-year doesn't lower my cost to serve you — it raises my exposure. I'd rather earn year two by being worth it than lock you in at a number that hurts both of us by month fourteen."*
- **"Founder / early-customer rate."** *"The brand is new. The desk is not. If I price the first year as a learning year, I've told you year two's price is invented. It isn't."*
- **"Per-head pro-rata."** *"The tier is priced by desk capacity, not headcount. Pay per-head and you pay me more in eighteen months when you hire. I'm pricing the seat, not the seats."*

### Improvement 2: Publish a fixed "Concession Menu" — what you CAN give, in writing.

Right now the founder has to invent concessions under pressure. That is how price gets cut. The playbook should publish a **pre-authorized concession menu** — things that cost the desk little, signal generosity, and never touch the headline number:

1. **Complimentary 90-day pilot window** (deferred meter start, walk-away with no charge)
2. **In-person Year-in-Review** (founder travels to member's office, 90 min)
3. **Planning-fee waiver on the first FAM / leisure trip**
4. **Peer-member introduction** (two members of comparable scale)
5. **Dedicated quarterly review** thrown in on Office tier (normally Firm-only)
6. **Profile build done by founder personally**, not the desk

Rule: **offer one. Never two without a second ask.** The discipline is what makes the concession valuable. The playbook should say so explicitly.

### Improvement 3: Add a "Negotiation Read" diagnostic — how to tell what kind of buyer you have.

The playbook teaches the founder to listen for pain. It does not teach the founder to listen for **negotiating posture**. Add a short diagnostic:

> **Three signals to read in the price moment:**
> - **Do they name a specific counter-number?** ("$20K not $36K") → They're serious and informed. Hold price; offer one concession.
> - **Do they invoke their own leverage?** ("I'm doing you a favor as an early customer") → They're testing whether you believe in your price. Folding loses the deal. Naming the leverage respectfully ("appreciated — here's what an early reference is worth to me") wins it.
> - **Do they ask an open-ended "what can you do?"** → They have not done the math. Do not offer money. Offer time, access, or a deliverable.

And one rule, in bold, at the top of the section:

> **If the buyer asks for a discount three different ways in three minutes, they are negotiating, not objecting. The deal is yours to lose by flinching, not theirs to give by pushing.**

That sentence is the single highest-leverage line that does not currently exist anywhere in the sales kit. Aaron's last line in the transcript — *"I respect that you didn't blink. I would've taken the twenty"* — is what every founder needs to read before their next pricing call.

---

**Bottom line:** the playbook is excellent for *getting to* the price moment. It abandons the founder *at* the price moment. The single biggest upgrade to revenue per call in the next 50 conversations is writing the chapter the playbook is currently missing — the one where the buyer agrees to buy and then tries to take 40% out of the number on the way to signing.
