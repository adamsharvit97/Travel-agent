## 3. WHAT THE PLAYBOOK HANDLED WELL

- **The LinkedIn open worked.** "Saw you closed the Series A six weeks ago — Bessemer-led" landed because it was specific. Playbook line: *"You will reference it within the first 90 seconds."* Correct instruction.

- **"What's the worst trip in the last 12 months"** is the killer question. Priya tried to dodge ("they all kind of blur") and the follow-up *"Anything in the last month?"* cracked it open. Playbook line: *"This is the question. Whatever they say next is the pain point you sell to."* — empirically true here.

- **The trust-transfer sentence held up under pressure.** *"What I want to test on this call is whether you'd actually benefit from this. If you wouldn't, I'd rather tell you that now than sell you a year of something you don't need."* Even though the founder didn't quote it verbatim, the *spirit* — making a no-strings free-fix offer — was a direct extension of that posture, and it's what kept Priya in the room.

- **"How long did the rebooking take you?"** as a forcing question worked. The playbook's instruction to *"listen for 80% of this section"* and let the prospect's specific failure surface was correct. Without that pause-and-press, Priya's "it's noise" deflection would have ended the call.

## 4. WHERE THE PLAYBOOK BROKE

### The reframe paragraph almost killed the call.

Playbook line: *"at your spend level, that's $4,000 to $9,000 a year of upgrades and credits sitting unused."*

For Priya, this is **the wrong number and the wrong frame**. She doesn't have an $80K T&E budget — she's probably at $30–40K, flying basic economy. Quoting $4–9K of "unused upgrades" to a founder who optimizes for the $80 basic-economy delta makes the seller sound like they're talking to a different customer. She would have heard it as a fantasy upsell. The founder in the transcript was smart enough to skip the number and lead with the desk — but that's *despite* the playbook, not because of it.

### "You're buying never thinking about it again. The same way you don't calculate the ROI of your accountant."

This is the FAQ line for the "isn't this a lot of money" objection. **It's a luxury-buyer line dressed up as a frugality line, and Priya would have walked.** She *does* calculate the ROI of her accountant. She has a fractional CFO on a Stripe invoice with a clear deliverable. She does not have "things I don't think about." The line assumes a buyer who has already accepted that delegation is normal. Priya hasn't.

### "Latitude 43 is a travel office. One human, on call, with the tools to make your week work."

The word "office" is **a class signifier she explicitly recoiled from** in the transcript ("sounds like something a managing director at Goldman has"). The whole "membership not transactional" framing has the same problem — it reads as "we are exclusive on purpose," which to a frugal founder reads as "we are charging a premium for the velvet rope, not for the service." The playbook says *"Not a booking platform. Not a card concierge. A small office with named members and a published response time."* The "named members" phrase is country-club language. It will lose every Priya.

### The a-la-carte refusal needs a better answer.

Playbook doesn't address it at all. When Priya asked for transactional pricing, the founder had to extemporize. The honest answer the founder gave — *"the model doesn't work below thirty members"* — actually worked, but the playbook should have had it.

### The weak-signal close would have failed.

*"If your next IROP costs you four hours of your weekend, will you let me know?"* — Priya would have heard this as smug and theatrical. She doesn't want to be told "you'll be back." The free-fix offer (improvised) was the only close that held.

## 5. THREE SPECIFIC IMPROVEMENTS

### Improvement 1: Add a "frugal early-stage founder" persona track to the diagnostic.

**Current playbook language (line 13):**
> *"Founder of 12-person seed-stage: $40–80K."*

**Proposed rewrite:**
> *"Founder of 10–15 person Series A: $30–60K. CRITICAL — if their T&E is in this band AND they book their own travel today (no EA), do NOT lead with rewards or upgrade math. The numbers are too small to be credible and they will read it as upsell. Lead with the desk and the time cost of a single IROP. Save the points conversation for renewal."*

**Why:** The current band lumps Priya in with founders who have an EA and a $80K travel budget. Those founders buy on rewards math. Priya doesn't. The persona needs to fork at the diagnostic step.

### Improvement 2: Replace the "accountant" ROI line with a customer-revenue line.

**Current FAQ language (line 112):**
> *"It's three hundred dollars a month. At your spend level — call it eighty thousand a year — we typically capture three to nine thousand in upgrades, credits, and rebooked savings. Even at the low end the membership pays for itself. But more honestly, you're not buying ROI. You're buying never thinking about it again. The same way you don't calculate the ROI of your accountant."*

**Proposed rewrite for the under-$60K-T&E founder:**
> *"It's three hundred dollars a month. The rewards math at your volume is real but small — call it a thousand to three thousand a year back. That's not the case. The case is one prevented mistake. One customer dinner you don't miss because you're rebooking a flight. One investor meeting where you walk in calm instead of frazzled. If the membership prevents one missed meeting that mattered in a year, it's paid for itself by an order of magnitude. That's the math. Not ROI on points — ROI on attention."*

**Why:** Reframes the price as insurance against a single, specific, high-leverage moment rather than as a luxury you stop thinking about. Priya's NYC trip cost her face-time with a $180K ARR account. *That's* the number that lands.

### Improvement 3: Replace "office" and "named members" with operational language.

**Current playbook language (lines 51–52):**
> *"Latitude 43 is a travel office. One human, on call, with the tools to make your week work. Not a booking platform. Not a card concierge. A small office with named members and a published response time."*

**Proposed rewrite:**
> *"Latitude 43 is a one-person desk on a 60-minute SLA. When your flight cancels, you send one message and go back to your day. We hold a hard cap on how many people we cover so the response time stays honest. Call it a membership because that's what makes the SLA math work — not because we're trying to feel exclusive."*

**Why:** Strips the country-club register that triggered Priya's "skin crawl" reaction. Keeps the structural truth — capped membership, published SLA — but anchors it to the operational reason (response time integrity), not to status. A frugal founder will *respect* a capped-supply SLA. She will not respect "named members."

### Bonus (because the playbook deserves it): Add an a-la-carte rebuttal to the FAQ.

**New FAQ entry:**
> ***"Can I just pay you per trip when something breaks?"***
> *"No, and the honest reason is structural, not philosophical. A 60-minute response time means someone has to drop what they're doing on a Tuesday afternoon. That only works if the desk is funded by recurring members — not by hoping enough transactional calls come in to cover the month. If we took per-trip work, the SLA would be the first thing to slip. The membership is what protects the response time. That's the trade."*

This is the single most useful FAQ addition for any founder-stage buyer. It will come up every call below the $50K T&E band.
