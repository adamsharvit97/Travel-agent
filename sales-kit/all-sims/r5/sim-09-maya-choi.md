## 2. OUTCOME

The realistic outcome — given a founder who has trained on the playbook as written — is **Option A: medium-signal close → ghost**.

Maya will not respond to email three. She is a lawyer, she is conflict-averse, and she does not want to send the email that says "I've decided not to." Easier to let it die. By the time she has her next IROP, she will have forgotten this call ever happened — and even if she remembers, calling Latitude back would require admitting she was wrong to delay, which is a worse psychic cost than just continuing to suffer.

**Probability split given the current playbook:** Yes 15% / Soft-yes-then-ghost 70% / Hard no 15%.

**Probability split if the founder forces decision:** Yes 60% / Hard no 30% / "Truly need a week" 10%.

The playbook leaves 45 percentage points of conversion on the table for the Maya-shaped customer — and Maya is **the modal Latitude 43 customer**.

## 4. PLAYBOOK BREAKS — the close section

The close section has **three** scripted responses, indexed by signal strength: Strong, Medium, Weak. Maya is Medium. So the playbook tells the founder to say:

> *"Understood. Let me send you a short brief with the comparison… Sleep on it. I'll follow up next [day]."*

This is the killer line. It is a perfectly polite, perfectly competent **lose script**. Here is what it gets wrong:

**It treats "Let me think about it" as a real signal.** It is not a real signal. From a conflict-averse buyer it is identical to "I'm in," identical to "I'm out," and identical to "I'm bored." The playbook can't distinguish between them, so it punts on all three.

**It rewards stalling with more sales activity.** The founder leaves the call with homework (send brief, send comparison, schedule follow-up). The prospect leaves with no homework. The energy of the relationship has just inverted — the seller is now chasing, the buyer is now being chased. That dynamic produces ghosts.

**There is no script for the aggressive forcing close.** Nowhere in the document does the founder say "I need a yes or a no today, and a no is fine." The closest line is the *weak*-signal close — *"if your next IROP costs you four hours of your weekend, will you let me know?"* — which is gentle to the point of being decorative. It is designed for someone who has clearly opted out. It is not designed for the politely-opted-in.

**The "After the call" section makes the problem worse.** It says: *"One more high-quality touch, not another email. Then: silence. Do not chase."* That's wise for some buyers — but for Maya, "silence" means "she never gets back to you." She doesn't have a next bad trip that connects in her brain to your name. The playbook's restraint is correct for the cold-outreach archetype but wrong for the warm-call-then-evaporate archetype.

**The Track What's Working section measures the wrong thing.** It tracks "the objection that almost killed it." Maya gave no objection. She gave **enthusiasm followed by absence**. There's no field in the CRM for "killed by politeness." So the founder, six months in, will still think Maya was a maybe — and will not realize that 70% of his "maybes" are Mayas.

**The hard-questions list anticipates skeptical questions.** It does not anticipate the buyer who agrees with everything. Agreement is harder to handle than objection because there is nothing to dispute.

## 5. THREE IMPROVEMENTS — the force-a-decision close

### Improvement 1: Add a fourth close — "Polite-Positive / Forced Decision"

Insert between Strong and Medium:

> **Polite-positive signal ("This is really interesting" / "This sounds great" with no concrete next step volunteered):**
> *"Maya, before I send anything — I want to ask the question directly, because I've been on the receiving end of the polite no and the polite yes-that-turns-into-no, and they cost both of us time. Based on what we've talked about, is this a yes, a no, or a genuine need-more-information? A no is completely fine — I'd rather hear it now than chase you for six weeks."*
> If the answer is "need more information," the next question is: **"What specifically? Name the thing."** If they can't name it, it isn't real, and you press one more time. *"If the money were a yes, would this be a yes?"*

The whole skill being added is **isolating real objections from social-grease objections**. Lawyers, doctors, Asian-Canadian buyers, second-generation immigrant buyers, anyone with a "don't make a fuss" upbringing — they will not volunteer their actual decision. They will volunteer a delay. The playbook must learn to test delays.

### Improvement 2: Add a diagnostic at the top of the call that flags forcing-prone buyers

In the pre-call prep section, add a fifth bullet:

> **5. Decide whether this prospect is forcing-prone.** Signals: high-stakes professional career (law, medicine, finance partner), conflict-averse cultural background, communication style on LinkedIn that's heavy on "honored / grateful / delighted." For these prospects, do not use the medium-signal close. Default to the forced-decision close. The most polite person on the call is the most likely ghost.

This pre-arms the founder. Otherwise he will read Maya's warmth and choose the gentle close out of mirroring instinct — which is exactly the wrong instinct.

### Improvement 3: Replace the post-call section for forcing-prone buyers

Currently the playbook says: send brief → comparison sheet → one more touch → silence. For Maya, this is a slow funeral. Replace with:

> **Forcing-prone buyer follow-up (24h):** Do not send the brief. Send one line: *"Maya — based on our call, I think the answer is yes but you're being polite. If I'm wrong, send back the word 'no' and I'll never email you again. If I'm right, here's the application."* Attach the application. Not a brief. Not a comparison. **The decision itself.**
>
> If no reply within 72 hours, do not send a check-in. Send: *"Closing your file as a no. No hard feelings. If you change your mind, you have my number."* Roughly 30% of forcing-prone buyers respond to the close-the-file email with a yes. The other 70% were never going to buy and you've recovered six weeks of pipeline time.

This converts the silent-ghost into either a yes or a closed-lost — both of which are useful. The current playbook's silence converts the silent-ghost into an open Salesforce row that you stare at in February wondering if you should send one more.

## Bottom Line

The discovery playbook is a 95th-percentile *opening and diagnostic* document and a 40th-percentile *closing* document. It is built for the buyer who tells you the truth — the skeptical VC, the curious RIA, the founder who says "I'm in." It has no answer for the buyer who lies politely. That buyer is a huge fraction of the Toronto boutique-professional market that Latitude 43 is being built for. Fix the close, or watch the Mayas evaporate one by one and call it "the market wasn't ready."
