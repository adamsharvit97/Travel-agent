**[Zoom. 2:03 PM ET. Aaron joins from what looks like a glass-walled office. Camera on, AirPods in, laptop angled. He's eating something.]**

**Founder:** Aaron, hey. Appreciate you taking the time. I know cold inbound calls are usually a waste, so I'll try not to make this one.

**Aaron:** *(quiet for a second)* Okay that landed. *(pause)* But here's my concern. I've tried services like this. I had a — there was a thing called like, I don't remember the name, one of those concierge —

**Founder:** I can't say the word but I know which category you mean.

**Aaron:** *(laughs)* Right. Anyway. The problem was every interaction took longer than just doing it myself. I'd email them, wait four hours, get a question back, wait four more hours. By then I'd already booked it.

## Section 2: SALES COACH EVALUATION

**Outcome: Conditional — 30-day trial accepted, no card, no commitment. Call this a 55% close on day 30.**

This was a competent call. Not great, competent. You got the meeting Aaron didn't want to take and you converted it into a trial Aaron didn't walk in planning to accept. That's a real outcome. But you also left meaningful chips on the table, and the trial structure you offered is going to cost you margin and create a renewal cliff. Let me break it down.

**What worked:**

The opener — *"I know cold inbound calls are usually a waste, so I'll try not to make this one"* — bought you the first ninety seconds of real attention. Founders respond to founders who acknowledge the format is broken. Don't lose this.

The reframe at *"is that forty minutes worth solving, or have you just accepted it as the cost of doing business?"* was the call. That's the line that moved Aaron from posture to thinking. You can see it in the lean-back. That question is your wedge for this archetype and you should use it on every call until you find a better one.

The Madrid callback — *"did you check whether your Bonvoy points would've gotten you a better redemption"* — was excellent because it converted a vague pain into a specific monetary leak. Aaron's grimace is the tell. You took an abstract complaint and made it dollars he left on the table. Do this more.

The Priya question — handling it with *"work with her, not replace her"* — was the single best objection handle in the call. EAs kill more deals than price does, and you neutralized that landmine in one sentence. Bank this.

Naming the banned word ("I can't say the word but I know which category you mean") was charming and built trust. It signaled you weren't that category without you having to defend against being that category. Keep this move in your kit.

**What broke:**

You let Aaron drive the close. *"What do you need from me"* is Aaron offering you the close, and instead of closing, you handed him two options. Two options is one option too many on a discovery call. The reference path was always going to be rejected — busy founders don't do reference calls — so you should have led with the trial alone. You burned a beat offering a path he was never going to take.

The trial itself is the bigger problem. Thirty days no-charge for someone with three trips means you're going to do roughly forty hours of work, eat any supplier hiccups, and hand him the product before he's paid anything. That's fine as a tactic but the day-30 conversion math is brutal. Industry baseline on no-card trials is 30-40%. You need to either (a) require a card with a generous cancel window, or (b) make the trial shorter — fourteen days, one trip — so the perceived sunk cost is real on both sides.

You also missed two qualifying questions that would've changed your pricing posture. You never asked how big his team's travel footprint is — he mentioned a 14-person consultancy and you didn't follow up on whether any of those people travel. Atlas Office at $249 may have been the right tier from question one. You sold down without knowing. Second, you never asked about his comp structure or whether his consultancy reimburses T&E — that determines whether $99/mo is a personal expense or a P&L line, which changes everything about how he'll think about renewal.

Last broken thing: you didn't establish the next concrete touchpoint beyond the email tonight. No day-7 check-in scheduled, no day-14 review, no day-28 conversion call. Trials die in silence. You have to architect the cadence into the trial at the moment of signup.

**Three playbook improvements for the founder-with-EA archetype:**

1. **Qualify team travel footprint in the first five minutes.** Anyone running a 10+ person services business has hidden travel volume. Default-quote Atlas Office; let them downgrade themselves to Light.

2. **Make Priya the hero in the pitch, explicitly.** Not "we work with your EA" — "Priya will look like a magician to you within two weeks because she'll stop being the bottleneck on travel changes." Sell the EA's relief to the founder; the EA becomes your internal champion at renewal.

3. **Replace the open-ended trial with a structured pilot.** Three specific trips, fourteen days, card on file with a clear cancel-by date, two pre-scheduled check-ins. Trial → pilot is a vocabulary upgrade and a structural upgrade.

**Coaching one-liner:** *Stop selling at the moment of yes — close, schedule the next touchpoint, then keep talking.* You let Aaron close you instead of you closing him, and you didn't architect the next four weeks before he dropped. Yes is the start of the sales motion, not the end of it.

## Section 3: BUSINESS EVALUATOR

**Unit economics on Aaron at $99/mo:**

Year 1: $1,188 ARR if he signs at the end of trial. Subtract roughly $40-60/mo in fully-loaded service cost (founder time + PHT VA allocation + tooling), call it $600/yr COGS. Gross margin per Aaron is ~$588 Y1, ~50%. Supplier commissions on his $70K T&E spend, assuming 4-6% blended on roughly 60% commissionable bookings (hotels mostly, some air), adds $1,700-2,500/yr. Total Y1 gross contribution: $2,300-3,100. Y3, assuming he stays and you've held him at Light: roughly $7K-9K cumulative. If he upgrades to Atlas Office because his team starts using it: double those numbers.

CAC for Aaron, honestly accounted: cold email send + two days of founder follow-up + 21-minute call + 30-day trial servicing. Call it $400-600 fully loaded. Payback inside Y1. The unit math is fine. It's not extraordinary.

**ICP fit — and this is where I'm going to be unkind:**

Aaron is *adjacent* to ICP, not center-of-target. Your stated ICP is US founders, VC/PE partners, RIA principals, family office principals. Aaron is a founder, technically, but he runs a 14-person design consultancy. That's a services business with thin-to-medium margins, project revenue cyclicality, and a founder whose travel is operationally necessary but not strategically high-stakes. Compare him to a VC partner doing 40 LP visits a year where a missed connection is a real cost-of-capital event, or an RIA principal whose clients judge them on logistics polish. Aaron's downside on a botched trip is annoyance and a rebooking fee. He'll churn the first time the value feels marginal.

The deeper issue: Aaron's travel is *replaceable by Priya plus better tooling*. A VC partner can't push travel onto an EA because the EA doesn't have context on which LP meeting matters. Aaron can, and probably should as he scales. You're effectively competing with a future version of Priya who's 20% better at her job.

**Retention probability:**

I'd put Y1 retention on Aaron at 60-65%. He converts off the trial (call it 55%), then faces a renewal-shaped moment around month 4-6 when the novelty wears off and he hasn't had a real cancellation crisis. If the desk hasn't visibly saved him in the first 90 days, he churns at month 6. If it has — one good 11 PM rebook, one hotel save — he goes to 18+ months.

The retention math is bimodal. He's either gone by month 6 or he's a 3-year customer. Almost no middle.

**Referral value:**

This is Aaron's actual best feature. Atlanta founder community is tight. Design consultancy founders network laterally — agency owners, dev shops, brand studios, fractional CMOs. If Aaron loves you, you get 2-4 warm intros in Y1, of which maybe 1-2 convert. At $1,200 ACV plus commissions, one referred member is worth $2K-3K Y1 to you. So Aaron-as-referrer is potentially worth more than Aaron-as-customer, *if* he stays delighted.

But — and this matters — Aaron's referral network is other Aarons. So referrals from Aaron reinforce the adjacent-ICP problem, they don't fix it. He will not introduce you to GPs.

**Hidden risk — the investor question:**

The failure mode an investor would flag is this: members like Aaron train your operations to serve a customer who isn't your ICP, and you build a service that's good at $99-tier problems and bad at $1K-tier problems. You wanted to build Latitude Society at $1K-2.5K/mo, invite-only, 12-15 seats. Society members need a fundamentally different desk — proactive, anticipatory, briefed on their portfolio companies, on first-name terms with hotel GMs. You don't get there by serving a hundred Aarons. You get there by serving fifteen GPs deeply.

If your member book becomes 80% Aaron-shaped, you've built a different company than the one in your deck. It might be a fine company — call it a $2-3M ARR services-founder travel office — but it's not Latitude Society and it won't command Society-tier pricing or margins. The strategic risk is *capacity capture by the wrong segment.*

**Recommendation:**

Pursue fewer Aarons, and pursue them differently. Specifically:

1. **Cap Atlas Light at a fixed roster size**, maybe 50-75 members, and run a waitlist after that. Light is your top-of-funnel and reputation product. It is not your strategy.

2. **Use Aarons as referral engines, not revenue drivers.** Build a referral program with material rewards (one month free, or service credit) and explicitly ask Aaron at day 60 who else in his network might benefit. Don't wait for organic referrals; engineer them.

3. **Shift founder time toward GP/RIA/family office outreach.** Aaron took a 21-minute call cold. A GP at a top-quartile fund will take a warm intro from a portfolio company founder. That's a fundamentally different sales motion and it's the one that builds Society. Every hour spent closing Aarons is an hour not spent closing the people who will pay you ten times as much.

The honest answer is Aaron is a good *tactical* yes and a marginal *strategic* yes. Sign him, serve him well, mine him for referrals and for product-market signal — and then redirect 70% of your outbound energy upstream.
