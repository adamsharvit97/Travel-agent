# Latitude 43 Sales Simulation — Constance Albright

## 1. TRANSCRIPT

**Setting:** 30-minute Zoom. Constance dials in at 2:00pm sharp from her glass-walled corner office. Camera on, no smile, blazer, a half-finished espresso. FOUNDER (38, Toronto, two months into selling Latitude 43) joins ten seconds late.

---

**FOUNDER:** Constance, thanks for the time. Quick housekeeping — I usually keep these to twenty minutes, leave the last ten for your questions. Fair?

**CONSTANCE:** Fine.

**FOUNDER:** Before we get into Latitude, I want to understand how you travel today and where the friction is. Then I'll tell you what we are, and we can figure out together whether it's worth continuing. Sound right?

**CONSTANCE:** Go ahead.

**FOUNDER:** I saw the panel you did at SuperReturn Berlin last month — the LP allocation conversation. Did you fly direct from Newark or did you connect?

**CONSTANCE:** *(pause)* Newark direct. United. Why.

**FOUNDER:** Curious whether you were one of the people stranded by the ground stop on the back end. A few of our members were.

**CONSTANCE:** I came back the next morning on the BA flight out of Heathrow. Jennifer handled it.

**FOUNDER:** That's actually where I wanted to start. Walk me through a typical week. How many trips, how complex, who books them?

**CONSTANCE:** I do roughly fifty trips a year. About a third are LP meetings, a third are portfolio company visits, the rest is boards and the odd conference. Jennifer books all of it. She's been with me eight years and she's exceptional. So if this call is going where I suspect it's going —

**FOUNDER:** It isn't. Keep going. What's the worst trip you've had in the last twelve months?

**CONSTANCE:** *(beat)* Singapore in October. The hotel walked me at midnight — the Fullerton — over a renovation overrun they hadn't disclosed. Jennifer found me a room at the Capella by 1am. It was fine.

**FOUNDER:** Where was Jennifer at 1am Singapore time?

**CONSTANCE:** *(slight pause)* At home. In Greenwich. It was lunchtime there.

**FOUNDER:** And what was she doing the rest of that day?

**CONSTANCE:** *(cooler)* I don't audit Jennifer's calendar. What's the question.

**FOUNDER:** It's not a leading question, I promise. I'm trying to understand the shape of her week, because I suspect she carries more than just your travel.

**CONSTANCE:** She supports me and two other partners on calendar. Travel is mine.

**FOUNDER:** Got it. How often does something go sideways — an IROP, a walk, ground transport falling through — and what's the process when it does?

**CONSTANCE:** Probably six, seven times a year that it's material. Jennifer handles it. She's very good.

**FOUNDER:** I believe you. Last one on this — points and status. Are you actively optimizing or just letting it happen?

**CONSTANCE:** I have Centurion, Marriott Ambassador, United 1K. Jennifer tracks them in a spreadsheet. Honestly I don't think about it.

**FOUNDER:** Okay. I'm going to stop with the questions and tell you what I'm hearing, and then what we are. Three statements, fast.

First — Jennifer is excellent. Everything you're describing tells me she's the best in the business. I'm not here to suggest otherwise, and if at any point I drift in that direction please cut me off.

**CONSTANCE:** Noted.

**FOUNDER:** Second — at fifty complex trips a year across three partners, what Jennifer is doing for you on travel alone is probably fifteen to twenty hours a week of pure execution. The Singapore walk at 1am Greenwich time is the tell. She's available because she has made herself available, which is what great chiefs of staff do. The question I'd want to ask if I were in your seat isn't *can Jennifer do this* — she clearly can — it's *what is Jennifer not doing this week because she is doing this.*

**CONSTANCE:** *(silence, maybe four seconds)* Go on.

**FOUNDER:** Third — Latitude 43 is not a replacement for Jennifer. It's an *instrument* for Jennifer. We're a travel office — one named human, on call, with the supplier infrastructure of Virtuoso, Four Seasons Preferred, Marriott STARS, Hyatt Privé. When Jennifer wants to hand off a trip — or just the rebooking when Singapore goes sideways at midnight local — she has somewhere to hand it to. When she doesn't want to, she doesn't. We work *to* her, not around her.

**CONSTANCE:** So you're a service Jennifer would use.

**FOUNDER:** Yes. The member is you. The user, most days, is Jennifer.

**CONSTANCE:** That's a meaningful distinction.

**FOUNDER:** It's the whole product. The members who get the most out of us are the ones whose chiefs of staff treat us like a tool in their kit. Jennifer keeps the relationship with you, the calendar, the judgment calls about which board dinner matters. We take the four hours of hold-music-and-hotel-confirmations.

**CONSTANCE:** What does it actually look like, operationally.

**FOUNDER:** Three things. One, rewards — every booking goes through the luxury programs, so you get upgrades, breakfast, fourth-night-free, hotel credits Jennifer can't pull on Marriott.com. At your travel volume that's probably six to nine thousand a year of captured value she doesn't have time to chase. Two, convenience — one Slack channel and one phone number. Jennifer is on it. You can be on it or not. We remember that you sit in 2A, that the Fullerton walked you and we don't book it again, that Jennifer prefers email confirmations not texts. Three, the desk is 24-7. Sixty-minute response, day or night. So when the next Singapore happens, Jennifer doesn't have to be online at lunchtime Greenwich. We are.

**CONSTANCE:** And the price.

**FOUNDER:** Three tiers. Principal is thirty-six hundred a year, one traveler. Office is twelve thousand, up to five. Firm is thirty-six thousand, fifteen. You're a Principal — unless you'd want to extend it to the two partners Jennifer also supports, in which case Office is the right shape and it's still well under a thousand a month.

*(Stops talking.)*

**CONSTANCE:** *(longer pause)* Let me ask something directly. Why would Jennifer not see this as someone trying to replace her.

**FOUNDER:** Because she'd onboard us. The first call after you sign is a fifteen-minute call with *her*, not you. She tells us how she wants us to operate — does she want every itinerary cc'd to her, does she want us to call her before we call you, does she want us invisible to you entirely. We default to her preferences. The members where this works best are the ones where the chief of staff was skeptical on day one and the loudest advocate by month three, because we gave them their evenings back.

**CONSTANCE:** I'd want to speak to Jennifer before I committed to anything.

**FOUNDER:** That's the right instinct. I'd actually push you further — don't *tell* Jennifer, *ask* her. Frame it as: "I'm considering this, I want your read, and I want you to design how we'd use it." If she says no, we're not the right fit. If she says yes, she'll mean it.

**CONSTANCE:** What if she says it's redundant.

**FOUNDER:** Then she's right and you shouldn't sign. But I'd suggest one diagnostic question for her: ask her how many hours she lost last quarter to your travel specifically. Not in a tone of "is that too much" — in a tone of "I want to know." The answer will tell both of you whether this is for her.

**CONSTANCE:** *(short nod)* That's a reasonable question.

**FOUNDER:** One more thing, then I'll let you go. The risk if you don't do this isn't that the next Singapore is worse. The risk is that Jennifer eventually tells you she's tired, and you wish you'd given her this two years earlier. I'd rather you hear that now than from her.

**CONSTANCE:** *(very long pause)* I'll speak to Jennifer this week. If she's open, I'll come back to you. If she's not, I won't, and I'd ask you not to chase.

**FOUNDER:** Deal. I'll send a one-page brief tonight you can forward to her — written for her, not you. If she has questions she can email me directly. No copy to you unless she wants it.

**CONSTANCE:** That's the right move. Thank you.

**FOUNDER:** Thanks Constance. Safe travels next week.

*(Constance ends the call.)*

## 2. OUTCOME

**"Let me speak to Jennifer."** Strong-version. The founder didn't get the signature, but he got the only thing that was ever going to lead to one. Crucially, Constance volunteered the next step herself ("I'll speak to Jennifer this week") — the founder didn't have to ask for it. Probability of close, contingent on Jennifer's reaction: ~70%. Given the hidden truth (Jennifer is exhausted and would happily hand off), realistic close probability after Jennifer's call: ~85%. If the founder had pitched this as a Constance-tool rather than a Jennifer-tool, this is a clean walk.

## 3. PLAYBOOK WINS (quoted)

- **The 20-minute structure held.** The opening line *"Before we get into Latitude, I want to understand how you travel today and what's broken"* did exactly what it's supposed to do: it bought the founder permission to ask diagnostic questions to a hostile prospect. Constance gave a one-word "Go ahead" — that's enough. The structure transferred.

- **The 90-second specific reference.** SuperReturn Berlin, pulled from LinkedIn. The playbook says *"Find one specific thing... You will reference it within the first 90 seconds."* The founder did. It did *not* warm Constance up — nothing was going to — but it bought a beat of credibility that meant she didn't end the call at minute three.

- **The trust-transfer sentence.** *"What I want to test on this call is whether you'd actually benefit from this. If you wouldn't, I'd rather tell you that now than sell you a year of something you don't need."* The founder didn't quote this verbatim but used the structural equivalent ("If she says no, we're not the right fit"). With Constance, this was the load-bearing sentence — it's what made her trust the recommendation to involve Jennifer at all.

- **The pricing-then-stop.** *"Then stop talking. Let me them respond."* The founder priced and shut up. Constance's next sentence was a real question, not a deflection, which is the entire reason the silence works.

- **The weak-signal close, reframed.** The playbook's *"if your next IROP costs you four hours of your weekend, will you let me know"* was adapted to the Jennifer frame: *"the risk is that Jennifer eventually tells you she's tired, and you wish you'd given her this two years earlier."* Same mechanic — defer the trigger to the next inevitable pain — but pointed at Jennifer's fatigue rather than Constance's weekend, which is the only version that works on this buyer.

## 4. PLAYBOOK BREAKS (quoted)

- **The EA objection-handler is wrong for this buyer.** The playbook says: *"Your EA is a generalist. Travel is a specialty that takes 10,000 hours to be good at. Most great EAs are honest that they're booking on Expedia and Marriott.com because that's what they were trained on."* If the founder had read this verbatim, the call ends at minute four. Constance would hear "your EA is a generalist" as "Jennifer is mediocre," and she's gone. The 10,000-hours framing is *especially* corrosive — it implies Jennifer is unqualified at the thing she does every day. The line *"their travel hours drop by about half"* is correct content but the wrong framing: it positions Latitude as something Constance imposes on Jennifer, not something Jennifer chooses.

- **The diagnostic question #5 is dangerously phrased.** *"What does your assistant do today that you wish was handled differently?"* For most prospects this surfaces pain. For Constance, this is the question that ends the call — it presupposes Jennifer is doing something wrong. The founder explicitly did *not* ask this version. He asked about Jennifer's workload shape ("what was she doing the rest of that day?") which is sideways, not frontal.

- **The reframe block is too generic.** *"Most operators in your seat aren't paying for travel — they're paying twice for it. They pay an airline, and they pay the time it takes to clean up what the airline broke."* This is a great line for a founder or a solo operator. For a PE managing director with a chief of staff, *she* isn't paying twice — Jennifer is, and Constance is one degree removed. The reframe needs a version that names the delegate.

- **No "buyer vs user" framework anywhere.** The playbook treats the prospect as both the economic buyer and the user of the service. For roughly half of Latitude's plausible market (PE/VC partners, RIA principals, GCs, fund founders with chiefs of staff), those are two different people. The whole call has to pivot on that distinction, and the playbook doesn't acknowledge it exists.

- **The "weak signal" close presumes Constance experiences the IROP.** *"If your next IROP costs you four hours of your weekend."* Constance's IROPs cost *Jennifer's* weekend. The trigger has to land on the right person's pain.

## 5. THREE IMPROVEMENTS

### Improvement 1 — Rewrite the EA objection-handler

**Existing (quoted):**
> *"Your EA is a generalist. Travel is a specialty that takes 10,000 hours to be good at. Most great EAs are honest that they're booking on Expedia and Marriott.com because that's what they were trained on. Two things happen when we partner with an EA: their travel hours drop by about half, and the quality of the trip goes up because we have access to upgrade programs they don't. We work with your EA, not around them. If anything they become a fan first."*

**Proposed rewrite:**
> *"Then we work for your EA, not around her. The member is you; the user, most weeks, is her. The onboarding call is with her, not you — she tells us how she wants us to operate. The default is invisible: she stays on every thread, we take the hold-music and the rebookings, and she keeps the judgment calls and the relationship with you. The members who get the most out of us are the ones whose EAs are skeptical on day one and the loudest advocates by month three — because we gave them their evenings back. The right diagnostic isn't 'can your EA do this' — she clearly can. It's 'what is she not doing this week because she's doing this.'"*

Why: kills the "Jennifer is mediocre" implication, names the buyer/user split explicitly, gives the prospect a line they can repeat to their EA, and reframes the value as the EA's recovered hours instead of the principal's recovered hours.

### Improvement 2 — Add diagnostic question 5b for the chief-of-staff buyer

**Existing (quoted):**
> *"What does your assistant do today that you wish was handled differently?"*

**Proposed rewrite (add as 5b, kept 5a for solo operators):**
> *"You mentioned your chief of staff handles travel. Who else does she support, and what's the thing she's been pushing off her plate this year?"*

Why: it doesn't critique the EA, it asks about the EA's workload. The answer surfaces the same pain (overload) without requiring the principal to admit weakness in someone they're protective of. For Constance, the truthful answer ("she supports two other partners on calendar") was the lever the whole call turned on.

### Improvement 3 — Rewrite the reframe for delegated buyers

**Existing (quoted):**
> *"Two patterns I keep seeing. First, most operators in your seat aren't paying for travel — they're paying twice for it. They pay an airline, and they pay the time it takes to clean up what the airline broke."*

**Proposed rewrite (delegated-buyer version):**
> *"Two patterns. First — when someone in your seat has a great chief of staff, the question isn't whether travel gets handled. It does. The question is what your chief of staff isn't doing this week because she's on hold with United. Second — at your travel volume, there's six to nine thousand a year of upgrades, credits, and program benefits sitting unclaimed, because the platforms your EA was trained on don't have access to them. Not her fault. It's a supplier question, not a skill question."*

Why: it preserves the dollar figure (the playbook's strongest selling tool), but moves the "blame" from the EA to the suppliers. "Not her fault" is the magic phrase — it inoculates against the protectiveness reflex. And the "what isn't she doing" framing is the only reframe that lets a protective principal even hear the pitch.

---

**Closing assessment:** The discovery-playbook is sharp for solo operators and for buyers who book their own travel. It has a structural blind spot for the buyer-with-a-chief-of-staff segment, which is probably 40-60% of Latitude 43's actual TAM. The fix isn't a new playbook — it's a four-paragraph appendix titled "When the user isn't the buyer" with the rewrites above. If the founder runs the existing script verbatim against Constance, she walks at minute four. If he runs it with the proposed substitutions, he gets the Jennifer meeting, and Jennifer closes the deal for him.
