## SECTION 1: THE TRANSCRIPT

**[Zoom call. Tuesday, 2:00 PM ET. Sarah Brennan dials in from her Back Bay office. The founder — call him Marcus — opens his laptop in Toronto.]**

**Marcus:** Sarah, hey. Thanks for making time. How's your week going?

**Sarah:** Good, good. Quarter-end is brutal but we're through the worst of it. You?

**Marcus:** Same flavor of chaos, different industry. Listen, I want to keep this useful, not ceremonial. We're six months in and I do a check-in at this mark with every Atlas Office member. Sometimes it's a victory lap. Sometimes it's an honest conversation about whether we're actually earning the seat. I want this one to be the honest version.

**Sarah:** *(small laugh)* Okay. That's a strong opener.

**Marcus:** Can I share what I'm seeing on my side first, and then you tell me where I'm wrong?

**Sarah:** Sure.

**Marcus:** You've signed for four trips with us since November. Two were yours, one was a partner trip to London, one was the Miami offsite logistics for your team. They all closed cleanly, no escalations, NPS feedback was fine. But I also know you've taken roughly sixteen trips in that window based on what you told me at signing about your travel rhythm. So somewhere around three-quarters of your travel didn't come to us. I'd rather name that out loud than pretend the four trips is the whole story.

**Sarah:** *(pause)* I appreciate you saying that. I was actually — I'll be honest, I was a little anxious about this call because I knew the math wasn't great.

**Marcus:** You don't owe me anxiety about it. I'd rather know what's happening. What's the real picture from your seat?

**Sarah:** *(careful)* Honestly? Sophie has been with me eleven years. She has muscle memory for Concur. When I'm running between client pitches I just forward the dates to her and she books. It's not a comment on you guys at all — the trips you did were lovely, the London hotel was perfect — it's just that the default path in my brain is still "tell Sophie."

**Marcus:** That tracks. And that's not a Sarah problem, that's an onboarding miss on my end. When we set you up I spent the kickoff time with you, not with Sophie. I should have spent it with Sophie. Can I ask — has Sophie ever used us directly?

**Sarah:** *(thinks)* Once. The Miami one. She emailed your team about the room block.

**Marcus:** Right, I remember. So she's used us as a vendor, not as her tool. That's the gap.

**Sarah:** *(quieter)* And — I'll just say it — I have wondered whether I'm getting value for the $249. Not because the service is bad. Because I'm not using it.

**Marcus:** Yeah. I'd wonder the same thing. Let me not sell you for a minute and just think with you. There are basically three honest paths here and I want to walk through all of them, including the one where you leave.

**Sarah:** Okay.

**Marcus:** Path one: we did the onboarding wrong, and the real fix is I spend an hour with Sophie next week, build her a one-page workflow, give her a dedicated point of contact on my team, and we test whether her default shifts over the next 90 days. If it does, the seat earns its keep. If it doesn't, we know.

Path two: the Atlas Office tier isn't right for your usage shape, and we move you to something lighter — pay-per-trip, no monthly. You'd lose the SLA guarantees and the EA companion seat, but you'd stop paying for capacity you're not pulling on.

Path three: this isn't the right fit, you cancel cleanly, and if you ever want to come back the door's open. No exit interview theater.

I have a preference but I want yours first.

**Sarah:** *(long pause)* My instinct was path three, walking into this call. Mostly out of embarrassment, honestly.

**Marcus:** There's nothing to be embarrassed about. You bought a service and didn't use it. That's on the service, not the buyer.

**Sarah:** *(half-laughs)* That's a generous framing. — I think path one is the one I actually want to try, if I'm being truthful. The four trips you did handle were better than what Sophie pulls together. The London hotel — she would have put me at the Langham, you put me at the Connaught. That mattered. I just don't have a habit yet.

**Marcus:** Okay. So can I propose something specific? Three things.

One: I get on a 45-minute call with Sophie this week or next, not you. I learn her workflow, she learns ours. I bring a one-pager that lives next to her Concur tab. The goal isn't to replace Concur — it's to make us the easier path for the trips where we add real value: international, complex multi-city, anything where the partner's flying.

Two: we set a default rule. Anything international or anything with more than two segments comes to us. Domestic Boston-NYC-DC shuttles stay on Concur if that's faster for Sophie. We're not trying to be everything. We're trying to be the obvious choice for the trips that actually matter.

Three: we re-check at 90 days, not six months. If the usage curve hasn't bent, we move you to path two without me trying to talk you out of it.

**Sarah:** That's reasonable. The international rule is smart — that's where she actually struggles, the time zones and the visa stuff.

**Marcus:** Good. One more thing, and this is the part where I have to be careful not to sound like I'm selling. You have two partners traveling internationally a lot, right? Hammond and the other one —

**Sarah:** Patel.

**Marcus:** Patel. Atlas Office covers up to five active travelers. You're using one and a half seats. If Sophie's workflow clicks and Hammond and Patel get added in, you go from "I'm not sure this is worth it" to "this is obviously worth it" because the math changes. But I'm not asking you to add them today. I'm asking you to let me prove it with you and Sophie first, and we revisit Hammond at the 90-day mark.

**Sarah:** That's the right sequence. Don't sell me the expansion before you've earned the base.

**Marcus:** Exactly that.

**Sarah:** *(pause)* Can I ask you something honestly?

**Marcus:** Please.

**Sarah:** If I'd come into this call and said "I want to cancel," what would you have said?

**Marcus:** I would have asked you the same diagnostic questions and probably arrived at the same three paths. If you'd still wanted to cancel after that, I'd have processed it the same day and sent you a note in six months asking how you're doing. I don't fight cancellations. I think it makes the people who stay regret staying.

**Sarah:** *(small smile)* That's a good answer.

**Marcus:** It's also the truth, which is convenient.

**Sarah:** Okay. Let's do the Sophie call. I'll introduce you over email today. And let's put the 90-day check on the calendar now so it doesn't drift.

**Marcus:** Booking it before we hang up. Thank you, Sarah. Really.

**Sarah:** Thank you for not making this weird.

## SECTION 2: SALES / CUSTOMER SUCCESS COACH EVALUATION

**Outcome classification: Re-engaged, conditional.** Sarah agreed to a 90-day re-onboarding with a structural rule (international + multi-segment routes to Latitude). She didn't expand, she didn't downgrade, and she didn't churn. The retention is real but fragile — it's now contingent on Sophie's behavior change, which Marcus does not yet control.

**What worked.**

The opener — *"Sometimes it's a victory lap. Sometimes it's an honest conversation about whether we're actually earning the seat. I want this one to be the honest version."* — is the single highest-leverage line in the call. It collapsed Sarah's defensive posture before she had to build one. Boston lawyers do not volunteer dissatisfaction; they wait to be asked correctly. Marcus asked correctly.

Surfacing the usage math himself — *"four trips… roughly sixteen… three-quarters of your travel didn't come to us"* — removed Sarah's burden of confession. She was visibly relieved (*"I was a little anxious about this call because I knew the math wasn't great"*). When the vendor names the elephant first, the customer doesn't have to be the bad guy. This is a near-universal CS move that 70% of founders skip because it feels like self-incrimination.

Naming the onboarding miss — *"that's not a Sarah problem, that's an onboarding miss on my end. When we set you up I spent the kickoff time with you, not with Sophie"* — was excellent. It reframed the conversation from "are you a bad customer" to "I made a diagnosable mistake I can fix." That reframe is what bought him the path-one option.

The three-paths structure was clean. Crucially, he included cancellation as a real path, not as a fake one. The line *"I don't fight cancellations. I think it makes the people who stay regret staying"* is the strongest trust-building moment of the call. Sarah explicitly registered it (*"that's a good answer"*).

The expansion restraint — *"don't sell me the expansion before you've earned the base" / "Exactly that"* — is the moment Marcus avoided the known retention trap. Almost every undertrained founder would have pitched Hammond and Patel as the value-justification move. He named the expansion, then deferred it. That's discipline.

**What broke (or nearly broke).**

The Sophie problem is still abstract. Marcus said he'd do a 45-minute call with her and bring a one-pager. That's a deliverable, not a behavior-change plan. Sophie has eleven years of Concur muscle memory. A 45-minute call and a PDF will not move that. He needed a stronger commitment device — e.g., "I'm going to ask Sophie to forward me her next three international itineraries before she books them, and I'll show her the delta in real time." He didn't get that specific.

He never asked Sarah what success looks like at the 90-day mark in measurable terms. "Usage curve bending" is founder-language, not customer-language. The right question was: *"At day 90, what number of trips through us would make you feel this was obviously worth it?"* Without that, the 90-day call will re-litigate the same ambiguity.

The pricing conversation was skipped. Sarah said the quiet part out loud — *"I have wondered whether I'm getting value for the $249"* — and Marcus moved past it into solutioning. He should have sat in it for one more beat. *"What would make $249 obviously worth it to you?"* would have surfaced her actual mental model of value (probably: trips per month, complexity handled, hours of Sophie's time freed). Now he's guessing.

**Did the founder surface the real issue?** Partially. He surfaced the usage gap and the Sophie habit. He did not surface why Sarah personally hasn't built the habit of routing trips to Latitude even when Sophie isn't involved. That's a separate diagnosis he skipped — and it's the one that determines whether path one actually works.

**Three improvements to the check-in playbook.**

1. **Bring a usage dashboard to the call.** Don't describe the math, show it. "Here are your 4 trips, here's spend, here's the 12 trips that went elsewhere based on your calendar." Visualizing it makes the conversation 3x faster and removes founder-as-narrator bias.

2. **Define the renewal criteria in the customer's own numbers, in the call.** Force the explicit question: *"What does month 9 look like such that you'd renew without hesitation?"* Write it down on the shared doc before the call ends. Ambiguity at the check-in compounds into ambiguity at renewal.

3. **Lock the EA call before hanging up.** Marcus left "I'll introduce you over email today" as Sarah's homework. That's a 40% slip risk. The Sophie meeting should have been scheduled three-way during the call, even if it's two weeks out.

**The most important behavioral change for the founder.** Stop treating EAs as introduction points and start treating them as the primary user of the product. Sarah is the buyer; Sophie is the user. Marcus sold to the buyer and onboarded the buyer. The entire usage gap is downstream of that single mistake. The behavioral change is: *every Atlas Office onboarding kickoff must include the EA from minute one, and the founder must not accept a signature without it.*

## SECTION 3: BUSINESS EVALUATOR (MCKINSEY-TRAINED)

**The structural problem.** Sarah is not an edge case. She is the median outcome of any Atlas Office member whose company already runs a corporate travel platform with an entrenched EA. My estimate: **30–40% of Latitude's Atlas Office cohort will land in Sarah's shape by month six** — paying, polite, under-utilizing, quietly evaluating. This is not a service problem. It is a *displacement* problem: Latitude is trying to displace a deeply embedded workflow (Concur + long-tenured EA) without controlling the user (the EA) or the system of record. Until Latitude either integrates with or replaces Concur in the EA's daily tab order, every Sarah-shaped member will drift.

**Unit economics.** On the surface, Sarah is the dream customer: $249/month, near-zero fulfillment cost, ~$1,500 collected against maybe 8 hours of actual VA time over six months. Gross margin per Sarah is north of 85%. But this analysis is wrong, and it is wrong in the way that kills early-stage service businesses. Sarah's high margin today is a forward-loaded liability: she is paying for capacity she isn't using, which means her *perceived* value-per-dollar is collapsing month over month. Members with collapsing perceived value do not churn loudly — they churn at renewal, silently, all at once, and they tell three peers. The lifetime value of a Sarah who renews twice and then leaves is roughly $4,500 with a poisoned referral footprint. The lifetime value of a Sarah who is told "you should downgrade" at month six and stays for three years on a $99 light tier is roughly $3,600 with a positive referral footprint. The second outcome is materially better for the business even though the headline ARR is lower. **High margin on under-utilization is not a moat, it is deferred churn.**

**Should Latitude actively churn low-usage members before they churn themselves?** Yes — with surgical precision. Not "churn" but *re-tier*. The founder should build an automated 90-day usage trigger: any Atlas Office member below 2 trips/quarter receives a structured re-engagement call (this one), and any member still below threshold at month 9 receives a proactive downgrade offer to a Light/pay-per-trip tier. The cost of this discipline is short-term ARR. The benefit is (a) NPS protection, (b) referral integrity, (c) freed founder attention from members who aren't going to renew anyway, and (d) honest cohort data — you cannot diagnose Atlas Office product fit while half your retained members are inflating the numbers by under-using quietly.

**The Sophie problem — the founder's playbook.** The EA-as-blocker pattern is the single biggest structural threat to Latitude's SMB segment. The playbook has four moves, none of which Marcus is currently running systematically:

1. **No Atlas Office signature without an EA workflow kickoff in the contract.** Make it a deliverable, not an option. If the EA won't take a 60-minute call, the deal isn't ready.
2. **Build the EA, not the principal, the hero.** Sophie's KPI is "trips booked without errors and without Sarah complaining." Latitude must reduce Sophie's failure rate, not threaten her job. Position as "Concur-plus for the trips Concur is bad at," not as a replacement.
3. **Define the wedge trip type up front.** International + multi-segment is the right wedge for Sarah's firm. The founder should be saying this in the *sales call*, not discovering it at month six.
4. **Track EA engagement as a leading indicator of renewal.** If the EA hasn't initiated three bookings by month two, the account is in distress regardless of principal sentiment.

**The Y2 prediction.** Probability-weighted: 45% Sarah renews Atlas Office (the Sophie call works, Hammond gets added, expansion happens). 35% Sarah downgrades to a Light tier by month 9 or at renewal. 15% Sarah churns cleanly. 5% Sarah churns and expansion within her network is damaged. The expected value of this account is *higher* than it was before the call, but the variance is also higher. Marcus moved the account out of "silent churn" probability mass (~70% pre-call) and into a barbell. That is the correct trade.

**Cohort signal — Atlas Office product fit.** Sarah is telling you something important about the product. Atlas Office at $249/month is priced and structured as a *full displacement* product (5 travelers, EA seat, unlimited bookings under 10/mo, 24/7 SLA) but is being *bought* as an insurance / premium-trips product by a meaningful fraction of customers. There is a product-market fit fracture: the customers who fully displace their existing stack get massive value; the customers who augment their existing stack get ambiguous value at a flat-fee price. The pricing model assumes displacement. The buying behavior shows augmentation. This is the gap that creates Sarahs.

**One specific operational change to prevent the next Sarah.** Build a two-call onboarding gate. Call one is the principal kickoff (already happens). **Call two is a mandatory 60-minute EA workflow session within 14 days of signature, where the founder co-builds a "what comes to us vs. what stays on your existing platform" decision tree with the EA, signed off by the principal.** No Atlas Office account is considered "live" until call two is complete. Members who refuse or delay call two get flagged immediately — they are pre-Sarahs, and the founder needs to know in week three, not month six.

The most expensive customers in a service business are not the ones who complain. They are the ones who are polite, pay on time, and quietly decide they're not coming back.
