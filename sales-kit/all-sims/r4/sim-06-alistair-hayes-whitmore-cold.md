## Outcome

**Send-materials → probable ghost, possible reschedule in 2–6 weeks.**

This is a LOSS in the immediate-revenue column and a WIN in the long-game column. Realistic probability tree:
- 55% — Alistair never replies. Email sits read-but-unanswered.
- 25% — Alistair forwards the email to his EA with "thoughts?" and she either kills it or champions it.
- 15% — Alistair replies in 3–8 weeks with "let's do that fifteen minutes."
- 5% — Alistair signs up directly from the email without another call (he's a principal; principals sometimes just decide).

The founder did not close. The founder did the thing that makes a close *possible later*, which on a cold call to a distracted principal is the only real move on the board.

---

## What worked

1. **Reading the room at minute six.** Devin clocked the keyboard clicks, the "sorry can you repeat that," the off-mic side conversation, and named it. Most founders would have plowed through the deck.
2. **Offering the off-ramp without making it about himself.** "I'd rather not waste your twenty minutes" framed the exit as respect for Alistair's time, not founder fragility. No apologizing. No "if it's a bad time…" wheedle.
3. **The thirty-second version up front.** Devin front-loaded the value before asking any diagnostic question — correct sequence for a cold prospect with zero trust equity. The diagnostic still died at question one (as the brief predicts), but at least Alistair had heard the offer before it died.
4. **The four-sentence email actually being four sentences.** Discipline. Most founders write "short" emails that are 11 sentences. Devin counted.
5. **The closing line of the email: "otherwise no follow-up from me, and the door stays open."** This is the move. It removes the threat of a drip campaign, which is what distracted principals actually fear when they say "send me materials."

---

## What broke

1. **The diagnostic died instantly.** "How often are you rebooking versus your EA" got a one-word answer and a side conversation. Cold + distracted means the diagnostic frame is unusable — there's no trust to spend on questions that feel like qualification.
2. **No reframe attempted.** Devin never got to "the real cost isn't the flight, it's your EA's afternoon." Correct call — delivering the reframe to a half-listening prospect would have burned the line for later. But it does mean the pitch never actually happened.
3. **No price anchor in the live call.** The $249/mo number only landed in the email, where it's competing with 200 other unread messages. A version of this call where Devin says "it's $249 a month, here's the email, bye" might have higher recall.
4. **No commitment device.** Devin let Alistair end with "I'll come back to you" — open-ended. A tighter close would have been "I'll ping you Tuesday the 9th unless you tell me not to" — gives Alistair a single yes/no decision instead of an action item.

---

## One improvement (for the distracted-principal archetype)

**Build a "principal one-pager" specifically for this exit.** Not a deck, not a brochure — one page, four sentences of copy, three bullets of pricing, one line of social proof, one calendar link. When the call dies at minute six, the founder says: "I'm sending you a one-pager — it's literally one page, you'll read it in forty seconds." The specificity ("one page, forty seconds") does two things: it signals respect for time, and it removes the cognitive load of "ugh, another sales PDF." Right now Devin is improvising the four-sentence email under time pressure. With a pre-built one-pager, every distracted-principal call ends with the same clean artifact, and Devin's not relying on writing his best prose at 4pm on a Tuesday.

The smart move on a cold call with a distracted whale is not to land the whale. It's to leave the hook in the water and walk away without snapping the line.
