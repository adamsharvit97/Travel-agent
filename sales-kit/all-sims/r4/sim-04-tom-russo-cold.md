## Outcome

**Send-materials, soft maybe.** ~75% probability this is a polite kill — Tom opens the one-pager, skims for ten seconds, files it under "deal with later," never replies. ~20% he reads it, finds the rebooking SLA genuinely interesting, and replies in 1-3 weeks. ~5% he forwards it to Priya and she becomes the actual buyer. No close on the call. No follow-up booked. But — crucially — no ghost-fuel either: the founder explicitly gave Tom permission to reply "pass," which lowers the cost of a no and slightly raises the odds of any reply at all.

## What worked

**The 30-second re-anchor was clean.** Naming the subject line of the cold email ("the 40 minutes you lose every time a flight moves") did more work than any company description would have. It let Tom retrieve the memory himself instead of being told what to remember. By the time the founder said "agency founder pattern," Tom had already nodded internally.

**Asking permission to continue.** "Want me to keep going or call back tomorrow?" — this is the move that bought the next six minutes. It signaled the founder wasn't going to grind through a script regardless of context. Tom registered it.

**The diagnostic was two questions, not five.** Trips per year + who-solves-it-now. That's enough to confirm fit without burning trust the founder hadn't earned. The founder explicitly aborted the third question when Tom got distracted by the driver — that's the discipline that kept this from collapsing into a pitch.

**The exit was the strongest part.** "I don't think you should decide anything on this call" is the line that flipped Tom's posture from bored-tolerance to mild respect. It conceded the obvious — there's no trust equity yet — and removed the pressure Tom was bracing against. Offering a "reply pass" escape hatch was the cherry on top: it costs the founder nothing and signals confidence.

## What broke

**The pricing mention was unnecessary and slightly off-tone.** Saying "two membership tiers, ninety-nine or two-forty-nine a month" in the fast version was filler — Tom didn't ask, doesn't care yet, and now has a number anchored before he understands the value. On a cold call with four minutes of attention, every sentence has to earn its place. Pricing belongs on the one-pager.

**No attempt to surface the actual budget-holder.** Tom mentioned Priya twice. Priya is the operational buyer here, not Tom — Tom is the economic signer, but Priya is who would use the service or veto it. The founder didn't ask "would it be useful if I sent the one-pager to Priya as well, or just you?" That's a missed branch — looping Priya in is often how agency-founder deals actually close, because the founder signs but the EA owns the relationship.

**No anchor for what "interesting" looks like on the one-pager.** The founder asked "if the one-pager is interesting, what's the better next step" — but didn't tell Tom what to look for. A line like "the part to read is the SLA on the second paragraph — that's the thing that's different" would prime Tom to actually find the differentiator instead of skimming generic copy.

## One improvement for this archetype

**The "tired founder who barely remembers replying" archetype rewards one move above all others: name what they almost certainly forgot, in their own words, in the first 20 seconds.** The founder did this well with the subject line. The next-level version: pair it with the specific pain hypothesis from the original email, and *attribute it back to the prospect's situation* ("you run a 22-person agency, you fly client-facing, your assistant doesn't work nights"). For cold leads with zero recall, the founder is essentially re-selling the original email in 15 seconds. Practice that opener until it's automatic — it's the single highest-leverage move on a cold pickup, and it's what separates "who is this?" from "okay, go."
