# Latitude 43 — Sales Sim R4 #1: Megan Donovan (Cold, Hostile)

---

## TRANSCRIPT

**Megan:** Megan Donovan.

**Founder:** Megan, it's Arielle from Latitude 43. Thanks for the fifteen.

**Megan:** Yeah. Listen, before you start — your cold email was decent. Better than most. But I want you to know I almost didn't reply, and I'm taking this call mostly to give you honest feedback on the approach. I hate cold outreach. It's lazy.

**Founder:** Fair. Cold email is the least flattering way to meet someone, and you're right to be skeptical of it. I won't try to talk you out of that. Can I do this — I'll skip the pitch for ninety seconds, tell you what I actually know about you and why I reached out, and then you decide if it's worth the other thirteen minutes. If not, we hang up and you've lost nothing.

**Megan:** ...Go.

**Founder:** Donovan Freight closed your Series B in February — Insight led, with Bessemer participating. Sixty people, mostly Chicago and a Memphis ops hub. Your LinkedIn shows you've been in Memphis, Atlanta, and Rotterdam in the last six weeks. That's the pattern I noticed: post-raise founder, US-to-Europe carrier conversations, and almost certainly a few customer trips that got booked twice and rerouted once. That's why the email. Not a blast.

**Megan:** Okay. That's — fine. That's better than ninety percent of what hits my inbox. But "I researched you" isn't a reason for me to be a customer. So what?

**Founder:** Right. The "so what" is — I don't know yet whether you need what I do. I have a guess, and I'd rather just ask three questions and find out. If the answer is no, I'll tell you it's no.

**Megan:** Three questions. Go.

**Founder:** When you flew Rotterdam — who booked it?

**Megan:** My ops manager. Her name's Priya. She does it on top of forty other things.

**Founder:** And when something went sideways on that trip — a delay, a re-route, a hotel that wasn't what was promised — who fixed it?

**Megan:** *(pause)* Me. At eleven at night. From the lobby of a Marriott in Schiphol that had given my room away.

**Founder:** Third question. How many trips like that — yours plus your two or three other travelers — does the company do in a year?

**Megan:** Probably... I don't know, eighty? Maybe ninety. We've doubled travel since the raise.

**Founder:** Okay. I'll stop and tell you what I think, and you tell me if I'm wrong. Priya's spending maybe four to six hours a week on travel that isn't her job. You're the escalation desk at midnight. And the cost of one bad trip — one missed carrier meeting because of a delay you couldn't reroute around — is a six-figure conversation, not a hotel room. That's the shape of it. Am I close?

**Megan:** *(longer pause)* Yeah. That's close. Priya quit threatening to quit last month, partly over this. Go on.

**Founder:** I won't oversell what we are. We're a travel office. Two travelers or five travelers on a flat monthly. Twenty-four-seven desk with a sixty-minute response on anything urgent. You stop being the night-shift fixer. Priya gets her four hours back. The Schiphol thing — somebody else's problem, not yours. That's it. That's the whole product.

**Megan:** What does it cost.

**Founder:** Two-forty-nine a month for up to five travelers. Twenty-nine eighty-eight a year. Design fees are zero under five thousand a trip, which covers most of what you do.

**Megan:** That's less than I expected. Which makes me suspicious.

**Founder:** It should. The reason it's that price is we're not selling you a status object. We're selling you the desk. The travel itself is the same airlines and hotels you already book — we just do the booking and the fixing.

**Megan:** Okay. Here's where I get honest with you. I've heard a version of this pitch from three different "travel for founders" outfits in the last two years. Two of them are dead. One of them is fine but their night desk is an answering service in Manila that reads a script. Why is yours different and don't say "because we care."

**Founder:** I won't. Two things, and they're checkable. One — we sit inside Fora, which is IATA-accredited, so the back end isn't a startup back end, it's a real agency rail. Two — the sixty-minute SLA is on the founder, which is me, and one other person, for now. There's no Manila. When that breaks because we scale, I'll tell you it broke and we'll renegotiate. I'd rather lose you in year two over honesty than keep you in year one over a lie.

**Megan:** *(quiet for a beat)* Okay. That's a real answer.

**Founder:** I want to be respectful of the clock. We're at about minute thirteen. I'd rather force a decision than have you "think about it" and ghost me, because that's worse for both of us. Three options. One — we start you on Atlas Office today, I send paperwork in the next hour, Priya's off travel by Monday. Two — you're a no, and I'd genuinely like to know if it's the price, the trust, or the timing, because that's useful to me. Three — it's a maybe, in which case tell me what would have to be true in ninety days for it to be a yes, and I'll come back then and not before.

**Megan:** *(laughs, short)* You're forcing the decision. Okay. It's not a yes today. I don't sign things on first calls and I'm not going to start now. But it's not a no either, and that's me being honest with you, not polite. What would have to be true — Priya would have to be in the conversation. She's the actual user, not me. And I'd want to see one trip handled before I commit to a year.

**Founder:** Both of those are fair. Here's what I'll propose. Put me on with Priya in the next two weeks — even fifteen minutes. If she's in, we do a single trip on a one-month no-commitment basis. Two-forty-nine, one month, you cancel after if it's not real. If it works, you roll into the annual and I refund the month against it. If it doesn't, you keep your money and I keep the lesson.

**Megan:** That's a reasonable offer. Send me that in writing and put Priya on the email. I'll tell her to expect you. And — for what it's worth — your cold email was still annoying, but you handled this call better than I expected. That's a compliment, don't make it weird.

**Founder:** Noted. I'll send it within the hour. Thanks for the fifteen, Megan.

**Megan:** Yep. Bye.

## OUTCOME

**Soft conditional yes — trial structure.** One-trip, one-month no-commitment, contingent on a 15-min call with Priya (the ops manager / actual user). Realistic conversion probability from here: 50-60%. Not a closed deal, but a live one with a named user, a defined trial scope, and Megan's stated intent.

## WHAT WORKED

- **Owned the cold approach in one sentence and moved on.** No grovel, no "sorry to bother you." She tested for defensiveness and didn't find it.
- **Spent the credibility budget immediately** — the Series B, the Memphis hub, the travel pattern. Proved research before being asked to.
- **Diagnostic survived the cold open** because it was framed as "three questions, then I'll tell you if I'm wrong" — a peer's framing, not a salesperson's.
- **Pre-empted the "three dead competitors" objection** with checkable facts (Fora/IATA, founder-on-SLA) instead of conviction.
- **Forced the decision at minute 13** and offered a real "no" path, which is what unlocked the honest "not today, but."
- **Trial offer was concrete** — one trip, one month, refundable against annual. Removed the year-commitment friction without discounting.

## WHAT BROKE

- Nothing broke catastrophically, but the call rode a knife edge for the first 90 seconds. If Megan had been any more hostile — or if the founder had said "I totally understand" once — the call ends at minute three.
- The pricing reveal ("less than I expected, which makes me suspicious") is a recurring cold-lead pattern this playbook hasn't fully solved. The founder handled it, but the answer was reactive, not pre-empted.

## ONE IMPROVEMENT — for the hostile-cold archetype

**Surface the user, not the buyer, earlier.** Megan softened the moment Priya came up — because Priya is the real pain. On the next hostile-cold call, ask "who actually books your travel" inside the first two minutes. The founder-buyer's hostility drops faster when the conversation is about protecting their team than about selling them a product.
