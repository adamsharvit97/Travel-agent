## Outcome

**Soft maybe, properly handed off to email.** No diagnostic attempted. No close attempted. Ariel killed the call at minute four — about sixteen minutes early — and converted it from a doomed twenty-minute monologue into a single asynchronous artifact (the three-line summary) plus a conditional follow-up.

Realistic next-step probabilities:
- Robert opens the email tonight: **~65%**
- Robert replies: **~25%**
- Robert books a real follow-up: **~12%**
- Robert ghosts: **~35%**

That ~12% is roughly 3-4x what would have happened if Ariel had run a full pitch on Bluetooth.

---

## What worked

**1. Naming the medium problem out loud.** "This is a bad medium for what we're talking about" is the move. It does three things at once: it gives Robert permission to be distracted (relief), it signals Ariel respects Robert's time (trust deposit), and it reframes the call as a setup for a better call (not a failure). Robert laughs — that's a tell. The pressure dropped.

**2. Trading a long call for a short artifact.** "Three-line summary" is concrete and small. Not "a deck." Not "some materials." Not "an overview." Three lines. Robert can mentally agree to read three lines while driving. He could not have agreed to read a one-pager.

**3. One question, not seven.** Ariel asked exactly one diagnostic-adjacent question — family or solo — and only after the reschedule was already agreed. Family-with-teenagers is the single most useful data point for tailoring the followup. Anything more would have triggered disengagement.

**4. The exit was symmetrical.** "We either book or we don't" gave Robert a clean out. Cold prospects need to feel like saying no is allowed; otherwise they ghost. Permission to say no is what makes people not ghost.

**5. Ariel ended the call. Robert didn't have to.** This is the underrated move. When the founder ends a call early, the prospect feels respected. When the prospect ends a call early, the founder feels rejected. Same outcome, different residue.

---

## What broke (or would have broken)

**1. Anything resembling a diagnostic would have killed this.** "What's your worst trip in the last year?" on Bluetooth, with Robert mid-honk? He'd have said "uh, I don't know" and the call would have died right there. The diagnostic requires a desk, a coffee, and at least 60 seconds of established competence. None of those existed.

**2. The pillars pitch (24/7, 60-min SLA, rewards) got compressed to one sentence and probably still landed at 30% comprehension.** Robert will not remember "60-minute SLA." He might remember "someone picks up at 11 PM in Lisbon" — because it's a picture, not a metric. The followup email needs to lead with the picture, not the SLA.

**3. Pricing never came up, and that's correct here — but it's a risk.** If the email tonight leads with $249/mo before re-establishing the value picture, Robert reads the number cold and bounces. The email has to re-paint the Lisbon scene before the price appears.

**4. No credibility signal was deposited.** Ariel didn't mention Fora, didn't mention IATA, didn't mention any other clients. On a normal cold call this is a problem. On a Bluetooth call with a distracted driver, dropping credentials would have felt like over-claiming. So it's correct to omit — but the email has to carry that load alone, and that's a heavy ask for three lines.

---

## One improvement for this archetype

**Pre-send the three-line summary before the call, not after.**

The archetype here is "agreed-to-call-then-forgot RIA principal who lives in his car." For this person, the live call is the worst possible first touch. He's never not driving. He's never not half-distracted. The 20-minute desk call he's promising for "next week" is a call he will reschedule twice and then ghost.

Better playbook for the next Robert: in the calendar invite itself, include the three lines. Not "looking forward to chatting" — three lines that say what Latitude 43 is, what it costs, and what the call is for. So when Robert picks up on Bluetooth, he's not starting from zero. He's already read the frame at some point during the week (or his EA has, and flagged it).

Then the live call becomes a 4-minute confirmation — "did the email make sense, are the teenagers the main travel use case, want to do a real 20 minutes next Tuesday" — instead of a doomed first-contact pitch.

The cold-call playbook for car-drivers is: **never let the car be the first touch.**
