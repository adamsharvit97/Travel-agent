# Latitude 43 — Cold Sim R4: Jennifer Boswell, COO

## 1. Transcript

**Founder:** Jennifer, thanks for making the time. I know Tuesdays disappear fast.

**Jennifer:** Of course. Mike spoke highly of you on LinkedIn, and honestly your note was one of the better cold emails I've gotten this quarter, so I figured I'd give you twenty-five minutes. What are we talking about today?

**Founder:** Appreciated. Quick frame so I don't waste your time: Latitude 43 is a travel office for founders, partners, and principals who travel enough that booking it themselves is leaking hours, but not enough to justify a full-time EA running their itinerary. Two questions and then I'll know if there's a reason to keep going. Sound okay?

**Jennifer:** Sure, go ahead.

**Founder:** When you travel for work — how do you book it right now? You, an EA, a tool?

**Jennifer:** I have an EA, Marisha. She does most of it. I do the occasional one-off myself if it's last-minute. We use Navan for the team, but I personally don't love it for my own trips so Marisha mostly works around it.

**Founder:** Got it. And when something goes sideways at 11pm in a hotel lobby — flight cancelled, hotel won't honor the rate — who picks up?

**Jennifer:** *(small laugh)* Marisha, theoretically. Realistically, me, because I'm the one standing there. It's not a frequent problem though. Maybe twice a year.

**Founder:** Fair. Last one and then I'll tell you what we do. Of the last five trips you took — how many of them did you actually enjoy the booking and travel experience of, separate from the trip itself?

**Jennifer:** *(pause)* Honestly? Probably one. But that's kind of the deal, right? It's work travel. It's not supposed to be fun. *(beat)* This is an interesting angle though. Tell me more about how the model works.

*[Founder notes the deflection — "interesting angle" is a tell. She just shut down the diagnostic with a compliment. Decides to deliver the pitch but compressed, and watch for more politeness markers.]*

**Founder:** Quick version. Membership, two tiers that matter for you. Atlas Light is $99 a month for a single traveler, Atlas Office is $249 for up to five. **Founder:** Two things. Marisha is solving for "book the trip." We're solving for "the trip went well." Different job. And Navan is a tool — it works as well as the person operating it. **Jennifer:** Mmm. That's a real distinction. You've clearly thought this through. *(beat)* And the design fees you mentioned in your email — how do those work for, like, a longer personal trip?

**Founder:** *(gently interrupting)* Jennifer, can I do something a little uncomfortable?

**Jennifer:** *(laughs)* Sure?

**Founder:** I've done enough of these calls to know what "sit with it" usually means. And that's fine — but it's not fine for either of us to pretend. **Jennifer:** *(longer pause)* …Okay. I appreciate you doing that. *(beat)* It's a no for right now. Not because of the product — the product actually sounds well-built. **Jennifer:** I appreciate you not making me dance around it. That was — yeah. Refreshing, actually.

## 2. Outcome

**Polite no, cleanly extracted.** No ghost, no "send materials" kill, no six-week chase. Founder also pulled useful intel: the actual trigger event is a *bad trip Marisha can't fix*, not a calendar date. That's a re-engagement signal worth more than a follow-up cadence.

Probability she becomes a customer in 6-12 months if a travel disaster hits: meaningful. Probability if no disaster hits: near zero, and that's accurate information.

## 3. What worked

- **Reading "interesting angle" as a deflection, not a green light.** Most founders would have heard "tell me more" and accelerated. He recognized it as a polite reroute around the third diagnostic question and adjusted pace.
- **The Force-a-Decision close, delivered with a soft warning ("can I do something a little uncomfortable?").** That framing earned permission for what came next and removed the awkwardness.
- **Naming the pattern out loud:** "I know what 'sit with it' usually means." This is the move. It signals competence and respect simultaneously.
- **Accepting the no in one sentence and moving on.** No re-pitching, no "well what if we did $49 for the first three months." Dignity intact.
- **The 6-month question, framed as research not retargeting.** Jennifer gave a real answer because the founder pre-committed to not weaponizing it.

## 4. What broke

- **The third diagnostic question ("how many of the last five did you enjoy") didn't land cleanly.** Jennifer batted it away with "that's the deal, it's work travel." On a warm call that question opens the pain. On a cold call with a politeness-deflector, it just gives her another exit. Needs a cold variant.
- **The pitch was technically correct but didn't create urgency.** Jennifer's verdict — "the product sounds well-built" — is the worst kind of compliment. It means nothing the founder said punctured her "I'm fine" frame. Features were communicated; consequence wasn't.
- **No attempt to build a hypothetical pain scenario.** Something like "imagine Marisha's asleep, you're in Lisbon, the hotel just told you they don't have your room" — concrete, sensory, makes the 60-min SLA matter. He stayed abstract.

## 5. One improvement for this archetype (the chronic-polite non-decider)

**Force the decision earlier — around minute 8, not minute 18.** Jennifer had decided before the call. Every minute spent pitching past her decision wall is wasted. The signal to watch for: any compliment about the *model* rather than a question about *her use case*. The moment she said "interesting angle, tell me how the model works," the founder should have heard "I am evaluating this as an outsider, not a buyer" and called it then: *"Jennifer — before I go further, are you actually shopping for this, or are you taking the meeting because Mike is a mutual?"* That gets to the same clean no in ten fewer minutes, and the intel question lands with more time to breathe.
