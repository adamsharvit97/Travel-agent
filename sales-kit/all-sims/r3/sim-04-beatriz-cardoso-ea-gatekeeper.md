# Latitude 43 — Sales Sim: EA Gatekeeper Screening Call

**Setup:** Wednesday, 11:00 AM. Marcus Tan, founder of Latitude 43, dialing in. Beatriz Cardoso picks up at 11:00:02. No small talk.

---

## Transcript

**Beatriz:** This is Beatriz. You have twenty minutes. I'll be candid — Edward didn't ask for this call. I scheduled it.

**Marcus:** Understood. And thank you for taking it instead of forwarding my email to spam, which I assume was option B.

**Beatriz:** Option A, actually. Spam is faster.

**Marcus:** Fair. Look — I want to reset the frame before I waste your time. I cold-emailed Edward. That was a mistake. The person I should have written to is you. So can I treat this call like that's what it is — me pitching you, not pitching past you?

**Beatriz:** Go on.

**Marcus:** You've been with Edward thirteen years. Which means you've booked, what, eight, nine hundred trips for him? Probably more.

**Beatriz:** I stopped counting around six hundred.

**Marcus:** Right. So I'm not going to do the part where I tell you travel is hard or that EAs are overworked. You know both. What I want to know is: when something goes sideways on one of Edward's trips — Heathrow shuts down, the Aman in Tokyo loses his reservation, the G650 slips a slot — who fixes it?

**Beatriz:** I do.

**Marcus:** At what hour, usually?

**Beatriz:** *(pause)* Whatever hour it happens. He's six time zones away half the year.

**Marcus:** Okay. So here's the actual pitch. Latitude is a travel office. One desk, named humans, sixty-minute SLA, twenty-four seven. We're not software. We're not Navan. We're the people you call at 2 AM Lisbon time when the hotel won't honor the rate. The way I usually describe it: we're a layer above whatever Edward currently uses — we don't replace your Amex Platinum desk, we don't replace you. We sit underneath you.

**Beatriz:** Edward has me. Why does he need you?

**Marcus:** He doesn't need us. *You* might want us. Different question.

**Beatriz:** Explain.

**Marcus:** The pitch most vendors make is "we replace the EA." That's stupid, and it's also why those five vendors before me failed — I'm guessing you're going to say five.

**Beatriz:** Seven. But continue.

**Marcus:** Seven. They pitched Edward and treated you as the obstacle. We do the opposite. The member is Edward. The user — most days, most hours — is you. It handles the supplier pushback so you can spend Tuesday on the things only you can do for him — the board memo, the LP dinner, the school thing for his daughter that he forgot.

**Beatriz:** *(silence, three seconds)* You've done your homework on the role.

**Marcus:** I've talked to forty-something COSs over the last year. They all said versions of the same sentence: "I'm good at this, and I'm tired."

**Beatriz:** That's an accurate sentence.

**Marcus:** So here's what I want to be direct about. I'm not on this call to convince you to recommend us. I'm on this call to ask you — if you were going to recommend us to Edward, what would you need him to hear? Because you know him. I don't. And I'd rather you write that sentence than me.

**Beatriz:** *(longer pause)* That's the first time a vendor has asked me that.

**Marcus:** It shouldn't be.

**Beatriz:** What would Edward hear? *(thinks)* He'd hear two things. One: this saves Beatriz time, so Beatriz can do more of the work that requires judgment. Two: when something breaks at an inconvenient hour, the problem is already solved by the time I find out. He doesn't want to be told about the fire. He wants to be told the fire was put out.

**Marcus:** Write that down. That's the pitch. I'm not joking — if you tell him that in those two sentences, you'll get a yes.

**Beatriz:** And the pricing?

**Marcus:** For Edward, the right product is Atlas Office. $249 a month. Up to five travelers, so it covers him, his wife, and whoever else moves under his calendar. Design fees waived on members' trips. There's a higher tier coming — Society, invite-only, $1,000 plus — but I'd tell you not to start there. Atlas Office for ninety days, then we talk about whether Society makes sense.

**Beatriz:** And what's the catch.

**Marcus:** Two honest ones. First — I'm new. I don't have a fifteen-year benchmark to wave at you. What I can offer is First Loss Insurance: if we screw up one trip in the first ninety days, I refund up to a thousand dollars of your time fixing it. Named on this call. Second — I take supplier commissions on hotels. Marriott, Four Seasons, Hyatt. That's disclosed. **Beatriz:** Acceptable. Most vendors hide that.

**Marcus:** One more thing — and this is the part I want to get right. You're not just the gatekeeper here. You're the person whose life this actually changes. For your own travel — your sister in São Paulo, the vacation Edward keeps telling you to take and you don't.

## Outcome

**Conditional yes.** Beatriz will champion the call to Edward. Principal call scheduled pending the one-pager. Atlas Light for Beatriz accepted implicitly (she didn't refuse). Probable close on Atlas Office for Edward, 70%+.

## What worked

1. **"Pitching you, not pitching past you"** — explicit acknowledgment of the bypass attempt, in the first 60 seconds. Beatriz softened audibly.
2. **"The member is Edward. The user, most days, is you."** — the EA buyer/user split lifted directly from the playbook (lesson 4) and it earned the first long pause.
3. **"If you were going to recommend us to Edward, what would you need him to hear?"** — handed her the pen. She wrote the pitch herself ("he doesn't want to be told about the fire"). That's now Latitude's deck copy.
4. **The personal Atlas Light offer** — landed without feeling transactional because it was framed as closing a gap ("nobody has ever offered you the thing you book for him"), not as a kickback.

## What broke

1. **"He doesn't need us. You might want us"** — risky line. Worked here because Beatriz is self-aware. With a more defensive EA this flips the call hostile in one sentence. Needs a softer variant.
2. **The "seven vendors" moment** — Marcus guessed five, she corrected to seven. Small, but every guess that misses makes the founder look less prepared. Don't guess numbers at gatekeepers who count.
3. **First Loss Insurance got buried** — mentioned as one of "two honest catches" instead of as a standalone trust move. For an EA who's been burned by seven vendors, this should have been its own beat, not a sub-bullet.
4. **No discovery on Edward's actual travel pattern** — Marcus pitched a product before learning whether Edward flies private always, sometimes, or never; whether his wife travels with him; whether there's a family layer. The Atlas Office recommendation was a guess dressed as a diagnosis.

## One improvement

**Build a dedicated "EA gift" SKU and price it into CAC, don't improvise it on the call.** The personal Atlas Light for the EA is a structural move, not a one-off generosity. If it's policy — "every principal account includes a complimentary EA seat" — it stops feeling like a bribe and starts feeling like product design. Right now it reads as Marcus thinking on his feet. That works once. Codify it: *EA Companion Seat, included with every Atlas Office and Society membership, transferable if the EA leaves the role.* That's the playbook addition.
