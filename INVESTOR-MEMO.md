## The thesis in one sentence

The luxury business travel market is bifurcated, not crowded — and the middle is empty by accident.

## The market structure

Three layers exist today.

**Enterprise** ($5M+ T&E programs): Amex GBT (now 21.9% global share after closing the CWT acquisition in September 2025), BCD Travel ($22.9B GBV), Corporate Travel Management, FCM, Direct Travel. Sales motions targeting Fortune 1000. Inaccessible to entrants without nine-figure capital.

**Self-serve SMB**: Navan (IPO'd October 2025 at $25, now trading near $13; $702M revenue on $9.1B GBV; 31% YoY) and TravelPerk ($2.7B valuation, $200M raise January 2025) own the software booking layer. SAP Concur controls ~50% of T&E management as the expense backbone. Combined capital raised: over $2 billion.

**The middle** ($50K–$2M T&E): structurally empty. Every quality independent that served this band has been acquired:
- Ovation Travel → Amex GBT (2021)
- Frosch → JPMorgan Chase (2022)
- Altour → Internova/Certares (2017)
- Casto Travel → FCM (2023)
- CWT → Amex GBT (September 2025)

What remains in the middle is fragmented — boutique shops with 1995 brand registers, family-owned firms with leisure-first DNA, or PE-compressed roll-ups (Internova owns Cadence, ALTOUR, Tzell, Global Travel Collection). The branded service infrastructure for the founder-traveler / partner-traveler / RIA-principal-traveler has been gutted.

This is the wedge.

## The buyer

US-based founders, VC partners, PE deal teams, financial advisors, and solo professionals with $50K–$2M of annual T&E. 15–40 trips per year per traveler. Not road warriors. Not Fortune 500. Not leisure-only.

This buyer is currently served by:
- **Concur** (which they hate — Skift, June 2025: "travel managers are fed up")
- **Navan/TravelPerk** (which break for complex multi-leg or last-minute changes)
- **Their Amex Platinum concierge** (which had email support eliminated in 2021 and off-list reservations eliminated in 2022)
- **Their EA** (who is overloaded and not a travel specialist)
- **Themselves** on Google Flights and Marriott.com (which captures zero rewards optimization)

The market size: GBTA 2025 projects $1.57T in global business travel spend rising to $1.69T in 2026. SMBs are 26.1% of that ($834B globally) and ~75% is unmanaged. EQT, when sizing TravelPerk's TAM, called the SME+midmarket opportunity ~$200B across US/Europe, half unmanaged. The dollars are real and the buyer pain is real.

## The product

Latitude 43 is a members' travel office. Three priced tiers:

- **Atlas Principal** — $3,600/yr — one traveler
- **Atlas Office** — $12,000/yr — up to five travelers
- **Atlas Firm** — $36,000/yr — up to fifteen travelers

Pricing sits in the empty band between Indagare's Custom Planner ($2,850/yr) and Knightsbridge Circle's standard tier (£25,000/yr — $31,500). No branded service exists between those poles today.

Three pillars, none competing on price:

1. **Rewards** — every trip optimized for points and status through preferred-partner programs (Virtuoso, Four Seasons Preferred Partner, FHR, Marriott STARS, Hyatt Privé, Hilton Impresario, Rosewood Elite). Worth $3K–$9K per year in upgrades and credits at typical member spend.

2. **Convenience** — one human, one Slack/WhatsApp/email channel. Tell us once: it remembers. The principal owns the relationship; no pod model.

3. **24·7** — first response in 60 minutes, day or night. Named SLA. No queue, no code, no call center.

## The structural advantages

Latitude 43 wins on six things no direct competitor delivers:

1. **Public pricing** on the home page (ALTOUR, Cadence, Brownell hide everything)
2. **Named SLA** in writing (no competitor publishes a response time)
3. **A named principal** (competitors run pod models per Glassdoor and Yelp evidence)
4. **Modern brand register** (competitors' visual register is 2014; one even still uses a phone code "SV23G" on its after-hours page)
5. **The traveler is the buyer** (competitors built dashboards for travel managers; we built them for the principal)
6. **Operator-owned** (competitors are PE-compressed; ours is the operator's own equity)

## Why Toronto, selling to America

Toronto is in Eastern Time. Same business day as NYC, Boston, Atlanta, Miami. Cost of operating from Toronto is ~30% lower than NYC on labor, rent, and the founder's personal burn. We sell into the US under Fora Travel's host accreditation (ARC, IATA), invoicing US clients in USD. Canadian solicitation is restricted under TICO regulations; we don't market to Canadians and that's a feature, not a bug — it focuses the entire firm on the highest-density buyer pool on earth.

## The economics

Three-scenario five-year model. Base case net take-home: $171,370 by Y5. Bear case: $32,200. Bull case: $1,567,500.

The variable that moves the needle: whether the firm lands one anchor account in Y2 (small PE/VC fund with 5–15 traveling principals). One anchor + one referral chain = the bull case. No anchor = base case. Lost anchor before diversification = bear.

Unit economics work because:
- Atlas membership is the foundation revenue (predictable, prepaid, low servicing cost)
- Hotel commissions (10% on preferred-partner programs) sit on top
- Air ticketing fees ($50/ticket) cover the transactional layer
- Fora's host split ramps from 80/20 at launch to 90/10 at $2M sold, materially improving margins as we scale

## The competitive risks

Three named risks, with response plans:

1. **Bell & Bly Travel raises money and expands.** Sarah Groen's firm is the closest direct analog. Their pricing is transactional ($800/week of trip + rush fees), which we attack with annual membership economics. If they shift to subscription, the moat compresses. Our response: be first to publish pricing, first to publish SLA, first to brand-modernize.

2. **Amex GBT spins off a midmarket brand.** Possible after the Long Lake $6.3B take-private. They have the supplier reach but not the brand voice or the speed. Our response: own the operator-luxe register before they get there.

3. **Navan launches a "high-touch tier."** Navan Pro exists; expansion is plausible. They cannot deliver a named human at our price point because their unit economics are seat-based. Our response: be the brand that already owns "named human."
