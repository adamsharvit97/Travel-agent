# Latitude 43 — Scenarios Book

**For:** Founder, Latitude 43
**Purpose:** Operational walkthrough of twelve realistic business-travel scenarios. What the work looks like, what we charge, what we deliver, what we say. Built to stress-test the proposed pricing model ($50/mo membership, non-member planning fees at $0 under $10K then $100/$1K above, members at half).
**Status:** Working document. Not for external distribution.

---

## How to read this book

Each scenario runs the same shape: scene, member's actual message, hour-by-hour workflow, value captured, what we charge, communication cadence during the trip, and a candid estimate of founder-hours consumed. The dollar values for upgrades, breakage, and saved cost are based on real Virtuoso/FHR/STARS amenity values, real airline rebooking economics, and real concierge labour. The labour estimates are realistic — not the optimistic numbers a sales deck would use.

After the twelve scenarios, five additional sections address the strategic questions the operational data raises: what business travellers actually want from an advisor, how to communicate with them, what we can and can't do, where the pricing model breaks, and how to write to them.

# Part I — The Twelve Scenarios

## Scenario 1 — Two-day NYC trip, YYZ to LGA, one night at the Whitby

### The scenario

The member is Marcus, 38, co-founder/CRO of a Toronto-based fintech (Series A, ~40 employees). He needs to be in midtown Manhattan Thursday morning for a 9:30am meeting with a prospective channel partner — a regional bank's innovation arm. The meeting is 90 minutes. He'll fly Wednesday evening, sleep Wednesday night, take the meeting, grab lunch with a friend at The Modern, and fly back Thursday afternoon. This is the basic test of the service: a transaction so simple a corporate travel tool could handle it. If we can't make this trip feel materially better than Marcus doing it himself in fifteen minutes on Air Canada's website plus a Hotels.com search, we have no business charging him.

### What the member says

> **Text, 6:47pm Monday:** "Hey — need to be in midtown by 9am Thursday for a meeting at 432 Park. Flying out Wed evening, back Thursday afternoon. Whitby if you can get it, otherwise something walkable. Thanks."

That's the entire brief. No flight times, no airline preference, no budget guidance. He expects us to know that he flies Porter or Air Canada premium economy out of YYZ, that he has American AAdvantage status and a Marriott Bonvoy Titanium card, that he prefers a room at the Whitby on a high floor away from the elevator, that "back Thursday afternoon" means 3-5pm wheels-up, and that he books lunches himself but appreciates a dinner suggestion if he has an open evening.

### What we do

**6:48pm Monday — acknowledgment (60-second SLA hit).** "On it. Targeting Porter YYZ-EWR 5:45pm Wed or Air Canada YYZ-LGA 6:15pm, Whitby high floor away from elevator. Will have options to you within 90 minutes."

**6:50–7:30pm Monday — sourcing.** Pull Marcus's profile: AC Aeroplan 50K, AAdvantage Platinum Pro, Bonvoy Titanium, prefers aisle in row 5-8 on the right side, no red-eyes, no Newark when avoidable (he hates the AirTrain). Check Sabre and the Porter direct portal for YYZ-LGA Wednesday evening. Porter has a 5:45pm and a 7:15pm. AC has a 6:15pm and an 8:30pm. The 6:15pm AC flight is on a 737 MAX 8 with the J cabin — only 50K points one-way redemption available; cash fare is $612 CAD. Porter business is $498 CAD with a free-flowing bar and lounge access. Recommendation: Porter 5:45pm — lounge, better food, lands LGA at 7:10pm.

**7:30pm Monday — Whitby through Virtuoso.** The Whitby is FHR but we book it through Virtuoso for the deeper amenity stack: complimentary upgrade subject to availability, $100 F&B credit, full breakfast for two, early check-in/late check-out. Rate Wednesday night is $725 USD plus tax. We block a high-floor room (8th floor or higher), confirm the away-from-elevator note in the booking remarks, and request the Junior Suite upgrade at check-in.

**7:35pm Monday — options to Marcus.**

> "Wed: Porter VPS 5:45pm YYZ-LGA, business, lounge at YTZ. $498 CAD. Lands 7:10pm — Blacklane to Whitby, 35 min. Whitby Thursday night [meant Wed], king high floor, Virtuoso rate $725 + tax with $100 F&B + breakfast for two + late checkout to 4pm. Junior Suite requested on arrival. Return Thursday: AC 4:15pm LGA-YYZ, J class on points + $89 cash. Confirm and I'll ticket."

**7:52pm Monday — Marcus replies "go."** We ticket the Porter, book the Whitby, schedule Blacklane LGA pickup for 7:10pm Wednesday and Whitby-to-LGA for 2:30pm Thursday. We add the Modern lunch reservation to the itinerary as a note (he books it; we just track it). We send the itinerary as a PDF and a live link.

**Wednesday-Thursday — execution.** Porter on time. Blacklane confirms 30 minutes ahead. Whitby pre-arrival: we email the front office manager, who we know by name (Jamie), confirming Marcus's arrival window and the upgrade request. Marcus is upgraded to a Junior Suite on the 11th floor at check-in. Thursday: late checkout to 2pm, Blacklane to LGA, AC 4:15pm wheels-up.

### The value delivered

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Junior Suite upgrade at the Whitby | $180 USD |
| $100 F&B credit (used at breakfast) | $100 USD |
| Full breakfast for two (Marcus + friend) | $90 USD |
| Late checkout to 2pm (no day-rate) | $362 USD avoided |
| Time saved vs. Marcus DIY | ~45 min |
| **Total amenity capture** | **~$732 USD** |

### What we charge

Trip value: $498 CAD flight + $725 USD hotel + $89 CAD return + ~$220 USD Blacklane = ~$1,650 USD. Well under $10K. Non-member fee: $0. Member fee: $0. Marcus pays the cost of the trip plus his $50 monthly membership. Net to Latitude 43: $50 for the month, plus Virtuoso commission on the Whitby (~$108 USD at 15%) and Blacklane commission (~$22). **Trip revenue: ~$130 USD plus the $50/mo retainer attribution.**

### The communication

One pre-trip confirmation (Monday 7:52pm). One night-before reminder (Tuesday 6pm — "Porter departs YTZ 5:45pm tomorrow, lounge opens 4pm, here's the gate when posted"). One arrival check ("Welcome to NY — Blacklane in lane 2, plate XYZ"). Silent through the meeting. One check-out morning text ("Late checkout to 2pm confirmed, Blacklane 2:30 in the porte cochère"). One landing-side text ("Welcome home"). Total: five proactive touches across 36 hours. Zero phone calls. Zero email threads. Text only.

### The lift, for us

- Sourcing and booking: 35 min
- Pre-arrival email to Whitby: 5 min
- Blacklane setup: 5 min
- In-trip monitoring + 5 texts: 15 min
- **Total: ~60 min of founder time, $130 in trip revenue.**

This is a break-even scenario at best. We do it because the membership baseline ($50/mo) recurs whether Marcus travels or not, and because nailing the simple trip is what earns the right to charge for the complex ones.

## Scenario 5 — AWS re:Invent, Las Vegas, 10 travellers

### The scenario

The member is Tom, 36, COO of a Series B AI startup in San Francisco. He's sending 8 engineers and 2 execs (himself + the CTO) to AWS re:Invent in early December. Five days, conference hotel. The conference is at the Venetian; he wants people housed at the Venetian, Palazzo, or Wynn — walkable or short shuttle. He needs restaurant reservations for one team dinner (12 people, including 2 customer attendees), separate dinners for him and the CTO with three different customer prospects, and ground transport on bookends. Two of the engineers are vegan. One is in a wheelchair. The conference dates are public; the inventory is brutal. We are quoting this in late August, four months out. Re:Invent room blocks are conference-controlled and the open market is gouging.

### What the member says

> **Slack, 100 days out:** "re:Invent Dec 1-5. 10 of us. Need rooms, dinners (1 team of 12, 3 customer prospect dinners for me/CTO), ground. Two engineers vegan, one is Sam who uses a chair — needs accessible. Conference housing is open already, do we go through that or you handle? Total budget call is mine but flag if anything is silly. The engineers will fly themselves on the corporate Concur, you handle me + CTO + the dinners + ground."

### What we do

**Same day — scope decision.** Conference housing through the official re:Invent system gives the lowest published rate but no upgrades, no amenities, and a 50-50 chance you end up at the Linq. Open-market booking at the Venetian/Palazzo via Virtuoso costs more but gets you the chairman/suite upgrades, the F&B credits, and — critically — guaranteed room type matching (the accessible room for Sam). **Recommendation: official housing block for the 8 engineers (cost-controlled, no upgrade urgency), Virtuoso direct at the Venetian Prestige Suite tier for Tom and the CTO.**

We send Tom this trade-off in writing and let him choose. He picks our recommendation.

**Days 100-95 — booking.**

- 8 engineer rooms via re:Invent housing portal, Venetian Tower base, paired by request. Sam's accessible room flagged in the portal AND we directly call the Venetian's special requests desk to confirm a roll-in-shower room is held against his name. We confirm in writing.
- Tom and CTO: 2 Prestige Suites at the Venetian via Virtuoso. Daily breakfast for two, $100 F&B credit/room/stay, complimentary upgrade to a Bella Suite subject to availability (likely, we know the front office manager).
- Air: Tom and CTO SFO-LAS Dec 1 on UA, return Dec 5. F class on miles where possible; cash backup. ~$680 cash equivalent each.

We pre-confirm two custom dishes. Prospect dinners: Bavette's Dec 2 (Tom + customer A), Sage at Aria Dec 3 (CTO + customer B), Mott 32 at the Venetian Dec 4 (Tom + customer C, walkable from the keynote venue). Each held in writing, follow-up call to each maître d' two weeks out, day-of reconfirmation.

# Part II — Additional Sections

## A. What business people actually look for in a travel advisor

The fundamental insight is captured in a single sentence we hear in some form during nearly every founder-intake call: **"I want to think about my work, not my travel."** Business travellers are not buying trips. They are buying back the cognitive load that trip-planning, trip-execution, and trip-failure-recovery would otherwise consume. The $50/mo isn't a fee; it's the price of evicting an entire category of low-leverage decisions from their week. The advisor who understands this — and who structures every interaction around minimizing the member's required cognitive input — wins. The advisor who treats themselves as a search engine the member has to type into loses.

The second insight is **the fungibility problem.** Every travel advisor claims the same things: relationships, Virtuoso, FHR, 24/7 support, upgrades, savings, expertise. The market is awash in advisors making identical promises. Business travellers — who are pattern-matchers by profession — see through this within one conversation. What they want is **proof in the form of specifics.** Not "we have great relationships in Florence" but "I'll text Marco at the Park Hyatt tonight and have your suite confirmed before you board." Not "we'll handle disruptions" but "when LH 756 cancelled last March, here's what we did in the next 28 minutes." The unique advisor is the one whose answers are observably more specific, faster, and more operationally textured than the competition's. Fungibility dies on the granular detail.

The third insight is **the proof they look for before signing.** They look for three things, in this order: (1) speed of response on the first interaction — how fast did the trial text get a reply, and was the reply substantive or pro forma; (2) a story about a real disruption, told with operational specificity, that demonstrates judgement under pressure; (3) a reference from a peer they trust who can speak in their language. Decks, websites, brand colours — none of these convert. Speed-on-first-text, an IROP war story, and a peer reference convert. Build the funnel around these three.

The fourth insight is **the single most common breaking-point that converts a self-booker into a member.** It is not the want for upgrades, and it is not even the want for savings. It is the moment they have to handle an IROP themselves while their meeting is at stake — sitting in the LH lounge at 2am, on hold with United for 45 minutes, watching their week unravel. That is the conversion event. Every other moment is shopping; that moment is closing. Sales and content should be engineered around that moment — case studies, founder posts, the IROP playbook. The next sale is the next executive who's just had the bad night.

## B. How we communicate with businesspeople specifically

**Channel preference, ranked.** In 2026, business travellers prefer text first. Email a distant second. Slack only when their company forces it. Phone last and only in emergencies. The reason is not generational — it's interruption hierarchy. Text fits between meetings; email demands a window; Slack tangles them in a thread; phone breaks them out of work entirely. We default to text. We escalate to email only for itinerary documents, restaurant confirmations, and anything they'll need to forward. We use phone only in crisis or when they request it. We never default to phone.

**Response cadence — expected vs. delivered.** The market expectation among business travellers is a 4-hour response on a weekday and 12 hours on a weekend. The luxury-advisor expectation is closer to 2 hours weekday, 4 hours weekend. We commit to a 60-minute SLA, 24/7, and we deliver inside 5 minutes more than 90% of the time during waking hours. **The gap between expectation and delivery is where loyalty is built.** Every time we respond in 90 seconds when they expected 90 minutes, we widen the moat.

**Itinerary format.** PDF for the formal record. Live link (Notion or our own member-portal page) for the working document. Slack canvas if the firm is a Slack shop. Text block summary for same-day or simple trips. **Three lines we adhere to:** (1) Every itinerary has a one-page glanceable view as the cover. (2) Every confirmation number is included; nothing is "send me a screenshot of your confirmation." (3) Every traveller can find one phone number to text in a single tap.

**Unprompted vs. on-request.** Send unprompted: gate-when-posted, weather flag if material, driver-staged confirmation, room-ready confirmation, IROP heads-up before the airline tells them. Send on request: dining suggestions, things to do, gym hours. The rule is — anything that affects whether they make their meeting goes unprompted. Anything that affects their leisure goes on-request. We never send "fun facts" or "did you know" or "here are 5 restaurants" unsolicited; that's the consumer-travel mode and it reads as noise to a businessperson.

**The "no surprises" rule.** They learn of every relevant change from us before they learn of it from any other source. The airline's app, the hotel's text, the gate agent — we beat all of them. This is not about ego; it is about cognitive predictability. They are operating on a tight mental model of their week, and our job is to keep that mental model accurate.

## C. What we realistically can do for them

### Things we can solve uniquely

- **Suite-level upgrade probability at FHR/Virtuoso/STARS hotels** — we move members up the upgrade list because of consortia status, named relationships, and pre-arrival outreach. Self-bookers and corporate travel tools cannot.
- **Restaurant access at the precise 90-day-out reservation window** at the hardest-to-book restaurants (Carbone, Sushi Saito, Mountain, Funke at Mother Wolf) — we have the calendar and the muscle memory.

- **Brazilian eVisas, Schengen edge cases, and the trade-attaché-style escalations** — only an advisor with the right phone book can do this.

### Things we can do better than the alternatives

- **Same-day-turn ground orchestration** — corporate booking tools and Lyft/Uber can do this technically, but worse: no standby car, no flight-tracking, no "wherever you finish your meeting" pickup.

- **Corporate-card receipt aggregation, expense report assembly, T&E policy compliance.** That is Concur/Navan territory. We don't try.
- **Negotiate corporate rates at non-luxury hotels** for SMB programmes. That's a corporate travel agent's lane.
- **Loyalty programme strategy at the level of a points-and-miles consultant.** We can use their points; we don't build their strategy.
- **Crisis evacuation, medical air, kidnap-and-ransom-grade duty of care.** We refer to Global Rescue and International SOS, which we co-market with as needed.

Be honest about each of these in writing on the website, in the sales kit, and in member intake. Members forgive a small "no"; they don't forgive an overpromise.

## E. The communication style guide

Writing to a business traveller is its own discipline. The luxury-leisure register — elegant, restrained, evocative — is not wrong, but it is the wrong gear for the bulk of this book. Business travellers reward different signals: **concision, evidence of competence, and absence of theatre.** We flex the operator-luxe brand register toward "concise and competent" in the business context, and reserve the elegant register for the anniversary trips and the rare moments where it earns its keep.

**Tone, in five rules.**

1. **Declarative over hedged.** Write "Porter departs YTZ 5:45pm, lounge opens 4pm" not "I'd suggest considering the 5:45pm Porter departure as a likely good option." They will read "considering" as a sign you are still thinking, which is not what they want from someone they're paying to be done thinking.

2. **Names over nouns.** "Marco at the Park Hyatt confirmed your suite" beats "the hotel confirmed your suite." Specificity is the proof of competence. Use names liberally — drivers, GMs, restaurant maître d's, gate agents you spoke with. Names are the evidence the abstract claim of "relationships" rests on.

3. **Numbers over adjectives.** "We saved you $4,884 booking F on miles" beats "we got you a fantastic deal." Numbers do the bragging so we don't have to.

4. **Active verbs over passive constructions.** "I rebooked you on Etihad" beats "you have been rebooked." Ownership of the action is part of what they're paying for; passive voice forfeits it.

5. **Decisions, not menus.** When you can recommend, recommend. "My call: Etihad EY 002 — best balance." Members hire us so they don't have to choose among five options at 2am. Offer two, recommend one, default to the recommendation if they don't reply within the window.

**Structure.**

- Lead with the answer. The first line tells them what they need to know. Detail goes below.
- Use vertical lists when there are more than two items. Prose paragraphs are for context, not data.
- Bold for the single most important number or word in a message. One bold per message, maximum, or it loses force.
- No emoji. Ever. Not in messages to members, not in internal channels visible to members, not in the itinerary PDF. The audience does not use emoji in their work email and reads them as a register mismatch.

**What they'll notice.** Speed of first response. Whether you used their name correctly. Whether you remembered their seat preference without being asked. Whether your "we'll" actually means we will, not "we hope we'll." Whether the itinerary opens cleanly on iPhone Mail. Whether the time zone is right in every line.

**What they'll forgive.** A typo, once. A hotel that didn't upgrade despite the request — if you flagged it before they arrived. A flight delay outside our control — if we warned them earlier than the airline did. A restaurant losing the reservation — if we had the GM's cell and fixed it in 10 minutes.

**What they won't forgive.** Surprises. A car that didn't show. A wrong confirmation number. An unread text from 90 minutes ago. A pricing discussion they didn't expect. The "no surprises" rule is the operative ethic; everything else is style.

**The elegant register, used sparingly.** Save it for two moments: the anniversary-trip welcome note, and the post-trip thank-you on a particularly well-executed leg. Used once, it lands. Used routinely, it reads as costume.

# Closing note

The twelve scenarios above are the realistic operating envelope of Latitude 43 in year one. They suggest a business that works — but only if pricing is adjusted to reflect operational complexity rather than cash trip value, and only if the IROP function is messaged correctly as the insurance product it is. The $50/mo membership is the right conversion product; the planning-fee escalator needs complexity multipliers and floors to keep the high-touch trips from becoming labour traps. Communication style flexes toward concision; the elegant brand register is reserved for the moments that earn it.

Built to be revised. Take what's useful; argue with the rest.
